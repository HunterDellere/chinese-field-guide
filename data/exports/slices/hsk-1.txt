//角落書屋/Slices/HSK 1
爱	ài	love, to love, to cherish — Love in all its forms: romantic, familial, and the Confucian ethic of universal benevolence.
八	bā	eight; the luckiest number and the eight trigrams — Eight is the most coveted number in modern China through its near-homophony with 发 fā (to prosper), and the classical foundation of the eight trigrams that structure the Yijing.
白	bái	white, clear, plain, in vain — Sunlight on a single point. The character that names the color of mourning, the color of clarity, the empty plate, and the speech that hides nothing.
百	bǎi	hundred; all kinds of — 百姓, the hundred surnames, is classical Chinese for the common people, because the full range of ordinary family names stands in for everyone who is not nobility.
本	běn	root, origin, this, a book — A tree with its root marked: the character for where a thing comes from. From root grows origin, the fundamental, one's own, the principal sum, and the bound volume you hold in your hand.
不	bù	no, not, negation — The most used character in Mandarin, and the foundation of every denial, refusal, and negation.
菜	cài	vegetable, dish, cuisine — Plants gathered to be eaten. From the picked greens grows a whole table: the vegetable, the cooked dish, the regional cuisine, and the slangy put-down for anyone weak at a task.
常	cháng	constant, ordinary, often — A long banner of cloth that came to mean what endures: the constant and the lasting, the ordinary and the common, and the things that happen again and again.
吃	chī	to eat — To eat, and the extraordinary range of Chinese life encoded in a single food verb.
打	dǎ	to hit, to strike; the most versatile verb in Mandarin — To hit, and a hundred extensions: make a call, play a sport, pack a bag, get an injection. The most productive light verb in Chinese.
大	dà	big, great, large — Big, great, large: a person with arms wide open, the original measure of magnitude.
地	dì	earth, ground, land — Heaven above, earth below: the second power of the cosmos, and the root of every word about place, ground, and belonging.
第	dì	the ordinal prefix, rank, mansion — Bamboo over younger-brother, an image of things laid out in sequence. From it grows the prefix that turns a count into an order, plus the older senses of examination rank and grand residence.
点	diǎn	dot, point, a little, o'clock — From a single dot of ink the character spreads into points and decimals, into the hours of the clock, into ordering food and lighting a flame, and into the small quantity that softens a sentence.
东	dōng	east; eastern — The sun rising behind a tree, and the direction that anchored China's cosmological, political, and poetic imagination.
动	dòng	to move, to act, to stir — Force applied to a thing sets it in motion. From that one idea grow the words for sport and activity, for the stirred heart, for the drive that pushes a machine or a life forward, and for the verb itself.
读	dú	to read aloud, to study — Reading as the act of voicing what is written: the everyday verb for studying, declaiming a text, and (figuratively) interpreting a person, a situation, or a sign.
多	duō	many; much; more; how — Two evenings stacked: the character that encodes abundance, comparison, and the most common question about quantity in Mandarin.
二	èr	two; second — The number two encodes the foundational split that drives all of Chinese cosmology: yin and yang, the two forces from which everything else unfolds.
方	fāng	square, direction, method — One glyph that runs from the literal square to the cardinal directions, to the place a thing sits, to the method by which it is done. The geometry of orientation and approach.
非	fēi	not, wrong, un- — Two wings spreading in opposite directions: from that image of opposition grows the great negator. Not and non-, the line between right and wrong, and the un- prefix that turns a word against itself.
分	fēn	to divide, minute, point, cent — A knife splitting a thing in two. From the cut grows everything that comes in parts: the minute on the clock, the point on a test, the cent in your pocket, and the act of separating itself.
个	gè	the universal measure word, individual — The general-purpose measure word that counts almost anything, and the root of individual. From the bamboo-stalk tally grew the everyday classifier 一个 and the words for individual, personality, and build.
关	guān	to close, to concern, the frontier pass — The bolt that closes a gate, the mountain pass that controls a frontier, and the relation that ties two people together. One character holds all three.
国	guó	country, nation, state — A walled enclosure protecting its people: the Chinese concept of nationhood written in a single character.
汉	hàn	the Han river, dynasty, people, and a man — A river that named a dynasty, and a dynasty that named a people. From the water of 汉水 grew 汉族 the Han ethnicity, 汉语 and 汉字 the Chinese language and script, and 好汉, the brave fellow.
好	hǎo	good; to like — Good, well, to like, and the tone split that doubles its expressive range.
和	hé	harmony, and, with; peace — 和 carries the same concept across grammar and philosophy: grain and mouth together, the harmony of shared food and speech, a character that serves as both everyday conjunction and the Confucian ideal of a well-ordered world.
很	hěn	very; the empty intensifier — 很 is the word for very, but its strangest job is doing nothing: Chinese needs a degree adverb before a plain adjective, and 很 fills that slot so quietly that 我很好 means simply I am fine, not I am very well.
后	hòu	back, behind, after; empress — One simplified character collapsed two completely separate traditional forms: 後 (a trailing foot in motion) and 后 (a commanding mouth), binding the spatial sense of behind with the imperial sense of empress in a single glyph.
候	hòu	to wait, time, season, climate — A person standing watch beside the road. From the figure of one who waits and watches grows the everyday word for when (时候), the act of awaiting (等候), and the long rhythm of the weather (气候).
花	huā	flower, to spend, patterned — A plant caught in its transformed, blooming state: the flower, the act of spending money or time, and the patterned, fancy, or blurred. One glyph that runs from the garden to the wallet to the dazzled eye.
话	huà	words, speech, talk, language — Speech beside a tongue: the everyday character for spoken words. From it grow talking and conversation, the telephone, a dialect or standard language, and the topics and sayings people pass around.
欢	huān	joy, merry, to be pleased — A breath of open delight: the hand and the open mouth that make laughter. From this glyph come joy and merriment, to like and to welcome, and the lively, high-spirited mood.
回	huí	to return, to turn back, a time — Two squares nested like a coiling eddy. The character for coming back: returning home, turning around, replying, and counting the times a thing happens.
火	huǒ	fire, flame, anger, popularity — Three flames rising: the character for fire and one of the most productive radicals in Chinese.
记	jì	to remember, to record, a mark — Speech set beside the self: the act of fixing something so it will not be lost. From that image grow three senses: to remember, to write down, and the mark left behind.
家	jiā	home, family — House, family, school of thought, nation (国家): the fractal of belonging.
间	jiān	space between, interval, room — Sunlight in the crack between two doors: the character for the space between things. From that gap grow time, the middle ground, the human world, and the room you count one by one.
见	jiàn	to see, to meet, to appear — An eye on legs. The character that built Chinese verbs of seeing, meeting, opinion, and the moment a thing first comes into view.
教	jiào	to teach, teaching, religion — A hand with a rod beside a child at the lesson. From that scene of instruction grows a wide field: to teach and educate, to teach a skill in the spoken jiao reading, and the teaching that became the word for religion.
进	jìn	to enter, to advance, to take in — A foot on the road and a bird that only ever walks forward. From that image grows everything that moves ahead and moves in: to enter, to advance, to progress, to carry out, and to take goods in.
九	jiǔ	nine; the imperial number and the highest of the yang — Nine is the largest single digit and the highest yang number, reserved for the emperor and the heavens: nine dragons, nine gates, the Nine-Five supreme position, the ninth heaven.
开	kāi	to open, to start, to operate — Two hands lifting the bar across a closed gate. From that single image flow most of modern life: opening a door, starting a meeting, driving a car, writing a check, blooming.
看	kàn	to look, to watch, to read, to visit — 看 = hand above eye, shielding from the sun to see in the distance: visual attention with effort, encoded in a gesture everyone has made.
来	lái	to come, to arrive, toward the speaker — Oracle bone wheat plant turned direction-word: 来 pulls everything toward the speaker, including time itself.
里	lǐ	inside, within; Chinese mile — From field stacked on earth, 里 named the bounded cultivated interior of a settlement and became the unit of distance that measured the realm's interior reach.
六	liù	six; the six directions; a smooth and lucky number — Six closes space into the six directions (the four compass points plus up and down) and, through its near-homophony with 流 liú (to flow), became a number for things going smoothly.
门	mén	door, gate, family, field — A picture of a two-leaf gate. From the swinging doors grow the gateway, the household behind them, the school or trade you enter, and the measure word for whole courses of study.
面	miàn	face, surface, side — A pictograph of a face inside its outline that opened outward into surfaces, sides, and aspects, became a measure word for flat things, and in simplified script swallowed the separate character for flour and noodles.
名	míng	name, fame, reputation — 名 is 夕 dusk plus 口 mouth: darkness falls, you cannot see who approaches, so you call out a name. Identity asserted through speech.
木	mù	tree, wood — A tree seen whole: trunk, branches, roots. The pictograph that became a radical, a wuxing element, and the building block of 林, 森, 本, and 末.
你	nǐ	you — The everyday second-person pronoun whose formal twin 您 embeds 心 (heart) directly into the character: address with warmth.
年	nián	year — A person carrying a harvest of grain: year as the cycle of agricultural labor, and the heart of the Chinese New Year tradition.
七	qī	seven; the rhythm of mourning, festival, and the soul — Seven measures the cycles of death and renewal in Chinese culture: the seven-day periods of mourning, the seventh-night lovers' festival, and the seven emotions of the heart.
钱	qián	money, coin, currency — Money: the character that drives markets, proverbs, and the Chinese conversation about wealth.
去	qù	to go, to leave, to depart, to remove — The character that marks every departure, from leaving the house to the euphemism for death. 去 is the verb of things moving away.
人	rén	person, humanity — The character for human being, and its echo in 仁 rén, Confucian benevolence.
三	sān	three; thrice; repeatedly — Three is the number of completion in Chinese cosmology (Heaven, Earth, Human), and the numeral that generates the ten thousand things in Daoist thought.
山	shān	mountain, hill, sacred peak — Three peaks rising from the earth: the oldest pictograph and the axis of Chinese sacred geography.
上	shàng	above, up, to go up, to mount — The most productive spatial primitive in Chinese. 上 starts as a notch above a line and ends up meaning everything from logging on to getting addicted.
少	shǎo	few; little; young — Small with a stroke removed: the character for scarcity that doubles as the word for youth when read in falling tone.
生	shēng	life, birth, raw, student — A sprouting plant: life emerging from the earth. The most generative radical in Chinese.
十	shí	ten; complete; cross — 十 is written as a cross (a vertical stroke cutting through a horizontal one), the point where all four cardinal directions meet, making ten the numeral of total completion.
时	shí	time, hour, season — The sun moving: time as something visible, measurable, and morally weighted.
事	shì	matter, affair, thing-that-happens — The character for an affair, a matter, a thing that happens. From an ancient pictograph of a hand holding the implements of office, it became Chinese for everything abstract that occupies us: business, jobs, incidents, and the everyday "no problem."
是	shì	to be — The copula, the cleft construction, classical 是非 right-and-wrong.
水	shuǐ	water — The first of the five elements, the radical that runs through hundreds of compounds, and the Daoist image of how to act in the world.
说	shuō	to speak, to say, to explain — To speak, say, explain, and persuade: the character at the center of all communication.
四	sì	four; the four directions; the homophone of death — Four organizes the Chinese world into quarters, the four directions, four seas, four seasons, yet its near-homophony with 死 sǐ (death) makes it the most avoided number in modern China.
他	tā	he, him; another — 他 was gender-neutral for a millennium until 她 was coined in 1917 to solve a translation problem that Classical Chinese had never needed to solve.
她	tā	she, her — 她 is the youngest character in everyday Chinese, invented in 1917 to translate "she," and the cause of one of the loudest grammatical fights in modern Chinese letters.
太	tài	too, extremely, highest, grand — The character 大 (big) with one extra dot, pushing big past its limit into too-much. From that excess grow the everyday too, the highest and grandest, the sun, and the supreme pole of Taiji.
体	tǐ	body, substance, to experience — The person beside the root: the body as a person's base. From the physical body grow substance and system, the whole as against the part, and the act of grasping a thing by living through it.
天	tiān	sky, heaven, day — Heaven as moral authority, weather, time, and the ground of 天命 the Mandate of Heaven.
听	tīng	to listen, to hear, to obey — The simplified 听 = mouth + axe, but the traditional 聽 encodes whole-body Confucian listening: ear, eye, heart, and upright virtue arrayed together in a single glyph.
同	tóng	same, together, with — Voices joined under one cover: the character for sameness and shared ground. From it grow the words for being alike, doing things together, and the people who share your role, your class, your cause.
外	wài	outside, outer, foreign, besides — A diviner reading the shells at the wrong hour, off the normal schedule. From that image grows everything beyond the boundary: the outside, the outer surface, the foreign land, and the relatives on the mother's side.
万	wàn	ten thousand; myriad; all — 万 is the Chinese hyperbole unit. Where English reaches for millions, Chinese reaches for 万 to express the immeasurable, because the written number 10,000 originally meant scorpion.
问	wèn	to ask, to inquire, to ask after — A mouth at the gate, asking. From that image grow the everyday verb to ask, the noun for a question or problem, the warm act of asking after someone, and the stern act of holding to account.
我	wǒ	I, me, myself — The character for 'I' began as a toothed bronze halberd: the assertive grip of a weapon became the grammatical mark of a self.
五	wǔ	five; the organizing number of correlative cosmology — Five is the center plus the four directions, the number that completes the Chinese cosmos: five phases, five colors, five tones, five flavors, five viscera, all keyed to one master grid.
西	xī	west; western — A bird settling into its nest at sunset: the direction that gave Chinese the word for "things" when it paired with east.
喜	xǐ	joy, to like, happy events — A festive drum sounding above an open mouth: the sound of joy and the smiling mouth that answers it. From this image grow the words for liking, delight, and every happy occasion, above all the wedding.
系	xì	to connect, system, department — A hand holding silk threads. From that image of holding strands together come the great connecting words of modern Chinese: the system, the relationship, the link, the university department, and the colloquial act of tying a knot.
下	xià	below, down, to descend, to issue — The mirror image of 上: a notch below the line that governs everything from going downstairs to issuing imperial decrees.
现	xiàn	to appear, present, now — The luster of jade coming into view. From that glint of something becoming visible grows the whole vocabulary of appearing, of the present moment, and of what is ready and on hand.
小	xiǎo	small, little; humble — Three minimal strokes that became the Confucian axis of moral character: the 小人 (petty person) and the 君子 (exemplary person) are the two poles every learner of classical Chinese must know.
写	xiě	to write; to describe — 写 simplified from 寫, which shows a magpie under a roof: the bird that mimics and transmits, giving its voice to the written mark.
学	xu é	to study, to learn, learning — Hands guiding a child beneath a roof: the character for learning as both action and institution.
样	yàng	shape, kind, manner, sample — A word once rooted in wood that grew into the language of appearance and kind: how a thing looks, what type it is, the sample held up as a model, and the everyday this-way and how of manner words.
要	yào	to want, must, essential — Want, need, must, and will: the grammar engine disguised as a single character.
也	yě	also; too; classical particle — A pictograph that lost its original image entirely and became one of the most load-bearing particles in classical Chinese: the gavel at the end of every verdict.
一	yī	one; unified; as soon as — The simplest mark a brush can make carries the heaviest philosophical weight in Chinese civilization: 道生一，一生二，the Dao generates One, and One generates everything.
有	yǒu	to have, to exist — Possession, existence, and the have/be distinction that reshapes grammar.
月	yu è	moon, month — A crescent moon in profile, and one of the most poetically charged characters in Chinese literature.
在	zài	to be at, located, in progress — The character that locates: a seedling rooted in earth, expanded into location, preposition, and progressive aspect.
长	cháng	long; to grow; chief — A person with flowing long hair. One character, two readings: cháng (long) and zhǎng (to grow, elder), the richest dual-reading in Mandarin.
正	zhèng	upright, correct, exactly, in the midst of — A foot halting squarely on the target line. From that image grows everything straight and correct: the upright, the standard, the exact moment, and the ongoing right-now.
中	zhōng	middle, China, to hit the mark — An arrow piercing through the center of a target: the character that names China as the Middle Kingdom and encodes the Confucian virtue of equilibrium.
重	zhòng	heavy, important; again, to repeat — A figure bearing a heavy load. From that weight grows everything that carries gravity: the heavy, the important, the grave. Read again as chóng, the same load comes back, layered and repeated.
子	zǐ	child, seed, Master — A picture of a swaddled infant that grew into one of the busiest characters in the language: a child and a son, the honorific for a Master, the empty noun-suffix on a thousand words, and the seed of a thing.
字	zì	character, written word — The character that names itself, and the whole system of 汉字.
走	zǒu	to walk, to go, to leave — A figure in motion with feet below: the basic verb of movement that Chinese uses where English uses dozens of different words.
作	zuò	to do, to make, to compose — A person setting something in motion. From the act of beginning grows the whole vocabulary of doing, making, and composing: the job you work, the work you create, and the role you act out.
做	zuò	to do, to make, to act as — 做 is the everyday verb for doing and making, a late character built to carry the work that 作 had grown too literary to do, and the two still split the labor between them.
笔记	bǐ jì	notes, jottings — Notes, jottings, and the Chinese literary tradition of the brush-record.
东西	dōng xi	things — East-west as the word for 'stuff': a window into directional cosmology.
故事	gù shì	story, tale, narrative — Old affairs made into meaning: how the Chinese word for story encodes a philosophy of the past as the source of truth.
关系	guān xi	relationship, connections — The untranslatable social fabric: relational leverage, it matters.
国家	guó jiā	nation; state; country — A compound of the two most culturally loaded characters in Chinese: country and family, that encodes the Confucian political imagination in two syllables.
汉字	hàn zì	Chinese characters — The world's oldest continuously used writing system: how Chinese characters work, why they look the way they do, and the categories every learner needs to know.
江山	jiāng shān	rivers and mountains, the realm — The classical Chinese word for the nation: not a territory but a landscape; not a state but a living geography that dynasties rose and fell to possess.
君子	jūn zǐ	the exemplary person — The Confucian moral ideal that replaced aristocratic birth with cultivated virtue as the basis of social authority: anyone who does the work of 仁义礼智信 qualifies.
轮回	lún huí	samsara; the cycle of rebirth — The Buddhist karmic wheel that turns through six realms of existence: the foundational concept that motivates all Buddhist practice as a path toward liberation.
面子	miàn zi	face, honor — Giving, losing, and saving face: the social grammar of dignity.
普通话	pǔ tōng huà	Mandarin Chinese — The common speech: how China standardized its national language, what Mandarin actually is, and how it relates to Cantonese, Shanghainese, and the Chinese dialects.
人间	rén jiān	the human world; the mortal realm — The world of people: where karma plays out, where flowers fade in April, and where the only beings who can hear the Dharma are born.
人生	rén shēng	human life; one's life journey — Human life: not biological existence, but the arc of a life lived: its meaning, its seasons, its reckoning.
山水	shān shuǐ	mountains and water; the landscape — The paired word that names the Chinese landscape: not "scenery" in the Western sense, but a cosmological pairing of the still and the flowing that became the central subject of a thousand years of painting and poetry.
时代	shí dài	era, age — The word for a historical era or age, built from 时 (time) and 代 (generation), and how it differs from 时期 a period, 年代 a decade, 时候 a moment, and 现代 modern times.
时候	shí hou	time, moment — The everyday word for a point in time, and the indispensable 的时候 ("when") pattern that every learner needs.
时间	shí jiān	time — Time: the compound that anchors all temporal expression in Mandarin.
天地	tiān dì	heaven and earth; the cosmos — The foundational dyad of Chinese cosmology, pairing Heaven and Earth as the two poles of the created order within which all human life unfolds.
天人合一	tiān rén hé yī	Heaven and humanity as one: the unity of cosmic and human order — The organizing claim of Chinese cosmology: Heaven and humanity are not separate domains, and human flourishing depends on alignment with the natural order.
天下	tiān xià	all under heaven — The pre-nation-state Chinese political imagination: the civilized world.
问题	wèn tí	question, problem — One word for both "question" and "problem", and the everyday phrases (没问题, 有问题, 出问题) built from it.
喜欢	xǐ huan	to like — The everyday verb for liking, and how it differs from loving (爱), enjoying (喜爱), and being willing (愿意).
现代	xiàn dài	modern times — The word for the modern era, built from 现 (present, manifest) and 代 (generation, age), how Chinese history slices time into 古代 / 近代 / 现代 / 当代, and how 现代 differs from 现在 now.
现在	xiàn zài	now — The everyday word for now, where it sits in a Chinese sentence, and how it differs from 目前 (at present), 此刻 (this very moment), and 如今 (nowadays).
一会儿	yí huì r	a short while — The everyday word for a little while, how it marks both a short duration and the near future, and how it differs from the verb-softener 一下 and from 等会儿.
一起	yì qǐ	together — The everyday word for "together," the 跟…一起 frame for joint action, the "in one place" sense, the "altogether" use of 一起, and how it differs from 一块儿, 一同, and 一共.
缘分	yuán fèn	karmic connection — Buddhist-rooted fate that ties strangers, lovers, and old friends.
长城	Cháng chéng	the Great Wall — A series of fortification systems built across multiple dynasties, whose most visible sections are Ming-era brick: not the continuous ancient rampart of popular imagination.
正在	zhèng zài	currently doing, progressive aspect — The primary progressive marker in Mandarin -- placed before a verb to signal an action caught in mid-motion, exactly now, still in progress.
中国	Zhōng guó	the Middle Kingdom; China — Two characters older than the country they now name. 中 (center) and 国 (state) joined three thousand years ago to mean "the central states"; the modern country took the phrase for itself only in the 19th century.
中医	zhōng yī	Traditional Chinese Medicine — Two thousand years of systematic medical thought: qi, yin-yang, the Five Elements, acupuncture, and the living tradition that treats the person, not the disease.
班门弄斧	bān mén nòng fǔ	Showing Off Before the Master — Brandishing an axe at Lu Ban's door: the idiom for presuming to display skill before someone who has mastered it.
东施效颦	Dōng Shī xiào pín	to imitate blindly without understanding, and make things worse — A homely woman copies Xi Shi's famous frown and clears the street: Zhuangzi's parable about form without essence.
君子不器	jūn zǐ bù qì	the exemplary person is not a vessel — Confucius says the junzi is not a utensil: not defined by function, not limited to one use. The Analects' argument against reducing a person to their usefulness.
山清水秀	shān qīng shuǐ xiù	Mountains Clear, Waters Lovely — Clear mountains, lovely waters. The standard Chinese phrase for a picturesque landscape: four characters that compress the whole aesthetic vocabulary of natural beauty.
事与愿违	shì yǔ yuàn wéi	things go contrary to ones wishes — Traced to Ruan Ji of the Seven Sages of the Bamboo Grove: a literary formulation of the gap between intention and outcome, resigned rather than accusatory, with no blame assigned.
一毛不拔	yī máo bù bá	Not Give Even a Hair — Refuse to give even a single hair from your body. Mencius's phrase for the Yangist philosophy of radical self-preservation, and the word for a miser that it became.
愚公移山	yú gōng yí shān	The Foolish Old Man Moves the Mountains — Two mountains blocked his door. He and his sons began to move them, one basket at a time. A mocker laughed. He said: when I die my sons will continue; when they die, their sons.
与时俱进	yǔ shí jù jìn	to advance together with the times — A classical phrase that became a political slogan in 2001, used to justify the Communist Party accommodating private enterprise, and criticized for justifying whatever the party currently does.
欲速则不达	yù sù zé bù dá	Haste Does Not Bring Success — Speed desired, arrival missed: Confucius on why rushing produces the opposite of what you intend.
的话	de huà	if; in the case that — The soft conditional: how 的话 marks a premise without committing to it.
多少 / 几	duō shao / jǐ	how many, how much — The two question words for quantity: 几 for small countable numbers and 多少 for larger or unknown amounts, why 几 needs a measure word and 多少 may skip it, and how to ask prices and ages.
还是 / 或者	hái shi / huò zhě	"or" in questions vs. statements — Chinese splits English "or" in two: 还是 offers a choice in a question, 或者 offers an alternative in a statement. Using the wrong one is a classic error.
来	lái / qù	去 · directional complements — 上来, 下去, 出去, 进来: the motion complement system mapped.
连…都/也	lián … dōu / yě	even X — The intensifier construction: raising the stakes of a claim.
没有	méi yǒu	negation of possession and past action — The two most important negation characters, 不 and 没, and why they cannot be swapped.
起来	qǐ lái	directional complement: upward, inward, beginning — The most versatile directional complement in Mandarin: three distinct grammatical lives.
是…的	shì … de	the cleft construction — Focus and past-detail marking: the construction that trips every learner.
太…了	tài le	too, excessively — The emphatic degree structure: 太…了 expresses excess and strong feeling, from complaints to enthusiastic praise.
一边…一边	yī biān yī biān	while doing; at the same time — Simultaneous actions: the frame for describing two things happening at once.
一点儿 / 有点儿	yì diǎn r / yǒu diǎn r	a little — The classic confusion pair: 有点儿 comes before an adjective and leans negative ("a bit too…"), while 一点儿 comes after and is neutral ("a little more").
一下	yí xià	the verb-softener — The verbal-quantity word that makes an action brief and casual, how verb + 一下 softens a request, and how it differs from verb reduplication and from the time-word 一会儿.
一样 / 像	yí yàng / xiàng	same and similar — How to say two things are the same (A 跟 B 一样) or alike (A 像 B), and how this differs from the unequal comparison with 比.
在	zài	location, existence, and the progressive — One of the busiest words in Mandarin: the verb "to be at," the location coverb "at/in," and the progressive marker for ongoing action.
只有…才	zhǐ yǒu cái	only if…then; the restrictive conditional — The exclusive condition: only when X is met can Y happen. The grammatical frame for necessity.
