//角落書屋/Slices/HSK 1–4
爱	ài	love, to love, to cherish — Love in all its forms: romantic, familial, and the Confucian ethic of universal benevolence.
八	bā	eight; the luckiest number and the eight trigrams — Eight is the most coveted number in modern China through its near-homophony with 发 fā (to prosper), and the classical foundation of the eight trigrams that structure the Yijing.
白	bái	white, clear, plain, in vain — Sunlight on a single point. The character that names the color of mourning, the color of clarity, the empty plate, and the speech that hides nothing.
百	bǎi	hundred; all kinds of — 百姓, the hundred surnames, is classical Chinese for the common people, because the full range of ordinary family names stands in for everyone who is not nobility.
北	běi	north; northern; to flee — Two people standing back-to-back: the direction of cold and turning away that became the name of China's capital.
本	běn	root, origin, this, a book — A tree with its root marked: the character for where a thing comes from. From root grows origin, the fundamental, one's own, the principal sum, and the bound volume you hold in your hand.
笔	bǐ	pen, brush, a stroke, writing — Bamboo over hair: the bamboo-handled writing brush drawn as a character. From the implement grow the act of writing, the strokes that build a glyph, and a measure word for sums and deals.
必	bì	certainly; must; necessarily — A character rooted in the image of a blade that divides without exception: certainty as the sharpness that leaves no room for maybe.
变	biàn	to change, to transform — The character at the heart of the I Ching, the dynastic cycle, and the modern vocabulary of reform: change as the one constant.
表	biǎo	surface, to express, a table, a watch — The garment radical worn on the outside: 表 runs from the outer surface of things to showing feeling, the chart on a page, and the watch on a wrist.
病	bìng	illness, disease, to fall ill, fault — A person sweating on a sickbed with the phonetic 丙 inside: the radical 疒 frames every Chinese word for being unwell.
不	bù	no, not, negation — The most used character in Mandarin, and the foundation of every denial, refusal, and negation.
部	bù	part, section, ministry, measure word — The right-side city-mound radical marks an administrative division. From that grows the whole family of parts and wholes: a section of something, a government ministry, a military unit, and the measure word counted out for films, machines, and phones.
才	cái	talent, ability, only-then, not until — A sprout breaking ground, the first sign of growth. From that image grow two threads: talent as raw promise, and the adverb 才 that marks something later, harder, or smaller than expected.
菜	cài	vegetable, dish, cuisine — Plants gathered to be eaten. From the picked greens grows a whole table: the vegetable, the cooked dish, the regional cuisine, and the slangy put-down for anyone weak at a task.
茶	chá	tea — The character that gave the world its word for tea: a grass over wood over man, a plant that shaped civilization, and the locus of China's richest material culture.
产	chǎn	to produce, give birth, property, output — From birth to bounty: a character that spans biological generation and industrial output, linking the miracle of birth to the logic of production and wealth.
常	cháng	constant, ordinary, often — A long banner of cloth that came to mean what endures: the constant and the lasting, the ordinary and the common, and the things that happen again and again.
朝	cháo	dynasty, imperial court, to face toward; (zhāo) morning — The sun rising among grasses with the moon still in the sky. From that dawn scene grow two readings: zhāo, the morning and its youthful vigor, and cháo, the court that met at daybreak, the dynasty it served, and the preposition toward.
成	chéng	to become, succeed, accomplished — A halberd standing guard over a finished task. From that image grows the whole field of completion: to become, to succeed, to accomplish, the ready-made and the mature, and even the colloquial yes that settles a matter.
城	chéng	city, city wall, town — A rampart of earth raised around a settlement: the character where the wall and the city it protects are one and the same word.
吃	chī	to eat — To eat, and the extraordinary range of Chinese life encoded in a single food verb.
出	chū	to exit, to emerge, to produce — 出 is two plants growing upward out of the earth: emergence itself written as a glyph, from physical exits to publication to standing out from the crowd.
传	chuán	to pass on, transmit, spread; (zhuan) a biography, a commentary — The person radical carrying something onward: one figure handing a thing to the next. From that image grow the senses of 传, read chuan when it means to pass down, transmit, and spread, and read zhuan when it names a biography or a classical commentary.
春	chūn	spring, springtime, vitality, the stir of desire — Sun pressing up through young shoots: the season of growth, and the oldest Chinese metaphor for life-force and erotic longing.
词	cí	word, term, diction, the sung lyric — Speech beside a phonetic 司: the unit we call a word, the wording a speaker chooses, and the classical lyric form that grew up beside regulated verse to be sung to set tune-patterns.
打	dǎ	to hit, to strike; the most versatile verb in Mandarin — To hit, and a hundred extensions: make a call, play a sport, pack a bag, get an injection. The most productive light verb in Chinese.
大	dà	big, great, large — Big, great, large: a person with arms wide open, the original measure of magnitude.
代	dài	generation, era, to substitute, on behalf of — The person radical carrying the sense of succession: one figure standing in the place of another. From that single image grow the four faces of 代: to replace or act for someone, to do a thing on their behalf, the generation that succeeds the one before, and the era or dynasty in which a life is set.
当	dāng	to be, just at, ought; (dàng) appropriate, to treat as — A high-frequency polyphone with two readings. As dāng it means to serve as, to be at a given time or place, and ought to; as dàng it means appropriate, to treat something as, and to pawn. The tone tells you which sense is in play.
到	dào	to arrive, to reach; to — As a resultative complement suffix, 到 converts any action verb into a claim of completion: 听到 means not just hearing but hearing that arrived, perception proven by contact.
道	dào	way, path — The word that gave Daoism its name, and underlies every Chinese 'ism'.
德	dé	virtue, moral power — Virtue not as rule-following but as accumulated moral power: what radiates from a person whose conduct has been straight long enough to become second nature.
地	dì	earth, ground, land — Heaven above, earth below: the second power of the cosmos, and the root of every word about place, ground, and belonging.
低	dī	low; to lower; to bow down — A person stooping: the phonetic 氐 gives the sound while the human radical supplies the posture, and together they built one of the most culturally loaded words in modern Chinese.
第	dì	the ordinal prefix, rank, mansion — Bamboo over younger-brother, an image of things laid out in sequence. From it grows the prefix that turns a count into an order, plus the older senses of examination rank and grand residence.
点	diǎn	dot, point, a little, o'clock — From a single dot of ink the character spreads into points and decimals, into the hours of the clock, into ordering food and lighting a flame, and into the small quantity that softens a sentence.
电	diàn	electricity, electric, telegram — Lightning descending from a rain cloud, a character that jumped from describing the weather to naming the force that defines modern civilization.
调	diào	(diào) to transfer, tune, accent; (tiáo) to adjust, to blend, to mediate — A high-frequency polyphone with two readings. As diào it means to transfer, a melody or key, a tone, an accent; as tiáo it means to adjust, to blend, to mediate. The reading tells you whether 调 is moving things or harmonizing them.
定	dìng	to fix, settle, decide, surely — A settling element coming to rest under a roof. From that image of a thing made still and fixed in place grows the whole field of 定: to decide, to set, the fixed and stable, the certain and sure.
东	dōng	east; eastern — The sun rising behind a tree, and the direction that anchored China's cosmological, political, and poetic imagination.
动	dòng	to move, to act, to stir — Force applied to a thing sets it in motion. From that one idea grow the words for sport and activity, for the stirred heart, for the drive that pushes a machine or a life forward, and for the verb itself.
读	dú	to read aloud, to study — Reading as the act of voicing what is written: the everyday verb for studying, declaiming a text, and (figuratively) interpreting a person, a situation, or a sign.
多	duō	many; much; more; how — Two evenings stacked: the character that encodes abundance, comparison, and the most common question about quantity in Mandarin.
而	ér	and, but, yet (a classical connective) — A pictograph of a beard, borrowed early as one of the busiest function words in literary Chinese. 而 links clause to clause as and, but, or yet, ties manner to verb, and frames set phrases like 而且 and 而已.
耳	ěr	ear; to hear; handle — The pictograph of an ear that anchors Chinese words for listening, fame, and the furred handles on bronze vessels.
二	èr	two; second — The number two encodes the foundational split that drives all of Chinese cosmology: yin and yang, the two forces from which everything else unfolds.
发	fā	to send out, to develop, hair — One simplified glyph that swallowed two traditional characters. 发 carries the fa reading of sending, emitting, and developing (traditional 發) and the fa reading of hair on the head (traditional 髮).
法	fǎ	law, method, the Dharma — Water against the level mark. From that image of fair, flowing measure grows the whole field of 法: the law and the court, the method and the way of doing a thing, and the Buddhist Dharma itself.
方	fāng	square, direction, method — One glyph that runs from the literal square to the cardinal directions, to the place a thing sits, to the method by which it is done. The geometry of orientation and approach.
非	fēi	not, wrong, un- — Two wings spreading in opposite directions: from that image of opposition grows the great negator. Not and non-, the line between right and wrong, and the un- prefix that turns a word against itself.
分	fēn	to divide, minute, point, cent — A knife splitting a thing in two. From the cut grows everything that comes in parts: the minute on the clock, the point on a test, the cent in your pocket, and the act of separating itself.
风	fēng	wind; style; custom — The character that moves crops, carries seeds, and became the Chinese word for cultural character itself.
夫	fū	husband, a grown man, laborer; the literary particle fú — A grown man pictured with a hairpin marking adulthood: the husband and the married couple (丈夫 夫妻 夫人 夫妇), the adult man and laborer (农夫 渔夫 大夫), skill and effort (功夫 工夫), and the classical literary particle 夫 fú that opens a sentence.
福	fú	fortune, blessing, good luck — The most recognized Chinese character in the world: fortune, blessing, and the art of the upside-down 福.
父	fù	father, male elder, household head — A hand gripping a stone axe: the tool of the one who heads the household, drawn as the figure of the father himself.
富	fù	rich, wealthy, abundant, to enrich — A full wine jar tucked under a roof: the picture of a household storehouse packed to its rafters with plenty.
感	gǎn	to feel, to resonate — Feeling, perception, gratitude, and the cosmology of sympathetic resonance.
高	gāo	tall; high; elevated; surname — A tall palace gate rising above the ground, and the character that anchors Chinese words for excellence, happiness, and examination pressure simultaneously.
告	gào	to tell, to announce, to report — A mouth aimed upward: the character for delivering words to authority, and the root of advertising, accusations, and warnings.
哥	gē	elder brother, a familiar term for an older young man — Two 可 stacked into one glyph; the doubled phonetic came to carry the warmest word in the family, elder brother.
个	gè	the universal measure word, individual — The general-purpose measure word that counts almost anything, and the root of individual. From the bamboo-stalk tally grew the everyday classifier 一个 and the words for individual, personality, and build.
更	gèng	even more; to change; night watch — A character that split across two readings: the comparative pulse of spoken Mandarin and the sonic clock of premodern China.
工	gōng	work, craft, labor, worker — Three strokes encoding heaven, earth, and the human craft connecting them: the root character for all labor, skill, and making in Chinese.
公	gōng	public, fair, common, a duke, male — A line dividing away the private self. From that image grow everything held in common, every fair dealing, the metric measures, and the elder male: public, impartial, official, and a duke.
功	gōng	merit, achievement, skill, effort — Merit earned through effort: from meritorious service and success to the patient skill the Chinese call gongfu, and even work in physics.
古	gǔ	ancient, old, classical — The mouth radical holding what ten generations have spoken down the years: 古 is what is old because it has been handed on. From that single image grow the senses of antiquity (古代 古老 远古), the classical and the antique (古典 古文 古董 古迹 考古), and the quaint or unconventional (古怪 古板).
故	gù	reason; old; deliberately; therefore — 故 binds five senses into one glyph: cause, the old and former, deliberateness, the literary "hence," and the deceased. The thread is a settled cause behind what is done or what has passed.
关	guān	to close, to concern, the frontier pass — The bolt that closes a gate, the mountain pass that controls a frontier, and the relation that ties two people together. One character holds all three.
观	guān	to observe, view, outlook, Daoist temple — The eye that looks and the mind that judges. From watching a thing closely grow two families of meaning: to observe and to view, and the settled outlook through which a person sees the world. A second reading, guàn, names the Daoist temple.
馆	guǎn	a public building, hall, shop, establishment — A food-radical building: the eating-house that grew into the library, the museum, and the embassy.
广	guǎng	wide, broad, to spread — A radical and a word: the cliff-shelter that became the prefix for everything expansive, from Guangzhou to Guangdong to radio broadcasts.
国	guó	country, nation, state — A walled enclosure protecting its people: the Chinese concept of nationhood written in a single character.
海	hǎi	sea, ocean, a vast quantity — Water plus the phonetic 每: the sea, the ocean, and by extension any vastness. From 大海 and 海洋 to the coastal city 上海, the navy 海军, and the crowd that becomes a 人海.
害	hài	to harm, injury, calamity, to fear — Under the roof a mouth and a blade build the sense of injury done; the same glyph also means to fear, as in 害怕.
汉	hàn	the Han river, dynasty, people, and a man — A river that named a dynasty, and a dynasty that named a people. From the water of 汉水 grew 汉族 the Han ethnicity, 汉语 and 汉字 the Chinese language and script, and 好汉, the brave fellow.
好	hǎo	good; to like — Good, well, to like, and the tone split that doubles its expressive range.
喝	hē	to drink; (hè) to shout, to cry out — A mouth radical paired with the phonetic 曷, splitting cleanly into the everyday hē you drink with and the older hè you shout with.
合	hé	to join, combine, suit, close — A lid closing down over the mouth of a vessel. From that image of two parts meeting and fitting grows the whole field of joining, suiting, closing, and adding up into a whole.
和	hé	harmony, and, with; peace — 和 carries the same concept across grammar and philosophy: grain and mouth together, the harmony of shared food and speech, a character that serves as both everyday conjunction and the Confucian ideal of a well-ordered world.
河	hé	river, the Yellow River — 河 began as the personal name of one river, the Yellow River, before it widened to mean any river at all.
黑	hēi	black, darkness, and the illicit — Black on the surface, but the live sense is moral: the black market, the underworld, the hacker. The color that names everything that operates out of sight.
很	hěn	very; the empty intensifier — 很 is the word for very, but its strangest job is doing nothing: Chinese needs a degree adverb before a plain adjective, and 很 fills that slot so quietly that 我很好 means simply I am fine, not I am very well.
红	hóng	red, the color of luck and celebration — The cultural keystone color: weddings, New Year, the red envelope, and a century of revolutionary politics all run through this one character.
后	hòu	back, behind, after; empress — One simplified character collapsed two completely separate traditional forms: 後 (a trailing foot in motion) and 后 (a commanding mouth), binding the spatial sense of behind with the imperial sense of empress in a single glyph.
候	hòu	to wait, time, season, climate — A person standing watch beside the road. From the figure of one who waits and watches grows the everyday word for when (时候), the act of awaiting (等候), and the long rhythm of the weather (气候).
湖	hú	lake, the provinces around Dongting — A water radical against the phonetic 胡, naming every still inland water from West Lake to the jianghu of wandering swordsmen.
花	huā	flower, to spend, patterned — A plant caught in its transformed, blooming state: the flower, the act of spending money or time, and the patterned, fancy, or blurred. One glyph that runs from the garden to the wallet to the dazzled eye.
化	huà	to change, to transform, to melt — The character of transformation itself: melting, digesting, civilizing, and the productive -ize suffix that builds half of modern technical Chinese.
画	huà	to draw, a painting, a stroke — A hand with a brush drawing the boundaries of a field. From that image grows the whole world of pictures: to paint, a painting, and the single stroke of a character.
话	huà	words, speech, talk, language — Speech beside a tongue: the everyday character for spoken words. From it grow talking and conversation, the telephone, a dialect or standard language, and the topics and sayings people pass around.
欢	huān	joy, merry, to be pleased — A breath of open delight: the hand and the open mouth that make laughter. From this glyph come joy and merriment, to like and to welcome, and the lively, high-spirited mood.
黄	huáng	yellow, gold, the imperial color; to fall through — The color of loess soil and the emperor, of the mother river and the egg yolk, and the one color slang that means a plan has collapsed.
回	huí	to return, to turn back, a time — Two squares nested like a coiling eddy. The character for coming back: returning home, turning around, replying, and counting the times a thing happens.
活	huó	to live, to be alive, lively, work — Water gives the verb its life: a stream over a tongue, the flow that means being alive, and from there everything that moves, breathes, and gets done.
火	huǒ	fire, flame, anger, popularity — Three flames rising: the character for fire and one of the most productive radicals in Chinese.
鸡	jī	chicken, fowl, rooster — The simplified 鸡 swaps a chicken-coop hand 又 in where the traditional 雞 kept the sound-bearer 奚 beside the bird.
急	jí	urgent, anxious, hasty, rapid — A heart pressed from above: 急 stacks a phonetic element over 心, so the glyph itself feels the squeeze of urgency.
己	jǐ	oneself, self, the sixth Heavenly Stem — A picture of a coiled, bending thread borrowed long ago to mean the self. From it grows everything turned inward: oneself, the private view, self-restraint, and the sixth of the ten Heavenly Stems.
记	jì	to remember, to record, a mark — Speech set beside the self: the act of fixing something so it will not be lost. From that image grow three senses: to remember, to write down, and the mark left behind.
家	jiā	home, family — House, family, school of thought, nation (国家): the fractal of belonging.
价	jià	price, value, worth — A person standing beside 介 jiè: the worth a thing takes on only once two people sit down to trade for it.
间	jiān	space between, interval, room — Sunlight in the crack between two doors: the character for the space between things. From that gap grow time, the middle ground, the human world, and the room you count one by one.
简	jiǎn	simple, brief, terse; a bamboo writing slip — Before paper, a letter was a strip of bamboo; from that narrow slip 简 grew into the words for simple, brief, and a short note.
见	jiàn	to see, to meet, to appear — An eye on legs. The character that built Chinese verbs of seeing, meeting, opinion, and the moment a thing first comes into view.
件	jiàn	item, piece, measure word — The measure word for matters, garments, and gifts, and the character behind documents, parts, and cases.
角	jiǎo	corner, horn, angle — Corner, horn, angle: the character that opens this site's name (角落書屋).
脚	jiǎo	foot, base — The everyday word for foot, and by extension the base of any standing thing: a mountain, a wall, a script. The flesh-and-bone counterpart to literary 足.
教	jiào	to teach, teaching, religion — A hand with a rod beside a child at the lesson. From that scene of instruction grows a wide field: to teach and educate, to teach a skill in the spoken jiao reading, and the teaching that became the word for religion.
接	jiē	to connect, to join, to receive, to meet — The hand that joins: 接 puts a wrist behind every link, every catch, and every arrival you go out to greet.
街	jiē	street, avenue, thoroughfare — The walk radical 行 splits open into a crossroads, and the jade-tablet phonetic 圭 sits inside, marking the place where roads cross and people pass.
节	jié	joint, node, festival, to economize — A bamboo joint, the segment between the knots. From that node grows everything that comes in sections: the joint of a limb, the chapter of a book, the festival that knots the calendar, and the restraint that keeps a thing within bounds.
结	jié	to tie, to unite, to conclude, to bear fruit — A knot tied in silk thread. From that image grows everything that binds and resolves: the knot you tie, the union of two people, the conclusion that settles a matter, and the fruit a plant ties onto its branch.
解	jiě	to untie, to solve, to explain, to dissolve — The character for cutting apart and undoing: untying and liberating, solving and explaining, dissolving and relieving. A polyphone read jiě, jiè, and xiè, pictured as a hand prying an ox horn from the ox.
借	jiè	to borrow, to lend, to make use of — One glyph carries both halves of a loan: 借 means borrow and lend at once, the direction settled only by context.
金	jīn	gold, metal, money — Nuggets of gold beneath the earth, and one of the Five Phases that governs autumn, the west, and sharpness.
紧	jǐn	tight, taut, tense, urgent — Thread drawn taut over the silk radical; the same word carries a tight knot, a tense nerve, an urgent deadline, and a tight wallet.
近	jìn	near; close; recent — Walking toward something as close as a hatchet strike, and the character that covers both spatial proximity and the specific historical era Chinese scholars call the modern period.
进	jìn	to enter, to advance, to take in — A foot on the road and a bird that only ever walks forward. From that image grows everything that moves ahead and moves in: to enter, to advance, to progress, to carry out, and to take goods in.
经	jīng	classic, scripture, to pass through, warp threads — The lengthwise warp threads strung on a loom, the structural through-lines that hold a fabric together. From that image grow the canonical classic, the act of passing through, and the running of an enterprise.
九	jiǔ	nine; the imperial number and the highest of the yang — Nine is the largest single digit and the highest yang number, reserved for the emperor and the heavens: nine dragons, nine gates, the Nine-Five supreme position, the ninth heaven.
旧	jiù	old; used; former; worn — The antonym of 新, and much more than it. 旧 carries both the weight of cherished tradition and the stigma of backwardness, depending entirely on who is speaking.
救	jiù	to rescue, to save, to relieve — A hand wields a stick to pull someone out of danger; the phonetic 求 once meant a desperate plea for help.
句	jù	sentence, phrase, a unit of speech — A wrapping line drawn over a mouth: speech bundled into a single unit. From that image grows the sentence, the line of verse, and the measure word that counts the remarks we say.
开	kāi	to open, to start, to operate — Two hands lifting the bar across a closed gate. From that single image flow most of modern life: opening a door, starting a meeting, driving a car, writing a check, blooming.
看	kàn	to look, to watch, to read, to visit — 看 = hand above eye, shielding from the sun to see in the distance: visual attention with effort, encoded in a gesture everyone has made.
可	kě	can; may; worth; quite — A two-stroke mouth character whose ancient sense of affirming breath became the pivot of permission, capability, and worth in classical and modern Chinese.
空	kōng	empty, sky, void; in vain — A cave with the work of hollowing inside it. The character that names the open sky, the empty room, the wasted afternoon, and the Buddhist śūnyatā at the heart of Chan.
口	kǒu	mouth, opening, entrance — A simple rectangle for an open mouth: one of the most productive radicals in the script, present in hundreds of characters for speaking, eating, and openings.
苦	kǔ	bitter, hardship, suffering, painstaking — The grass radical over 古 names a bitter herb on the tongue, then stretches to every flavor of hardship and effort.
跨	kuà	to stride; to span; to bestride — The character of the long stride: a foot-radical verb that reaches from "step over a threshold" to "span an era" to the modern policy vocabulary of cross-border, cross-industry, cross-cultural everything.
快	kuài	fast, happy — One character carries two meanings that turn out to be the same thing: speed and joy both feel like the heart cutting loose.
困	kùn	to trap, hard-pressed, tired — A tree boxed inside four walls and unable to grow: the picture of being hemmed in, the source of both hardship and sleepiness.
来	lái	to come, to arrive, toward the speaker — Oracle bone wheat plant turned direction-word: 来 pulls everything toward the speaker, including time itself.
老	lǎo	old, venerable, experienced — A stooped elder with long hair: age as wisdom, seniority as moral authority, and the Daoist sage who wrote the Daodejing.
乐	lè	happy, joyful (lè); music (yuè) — A picture of a wooden frame strung with silk and a drum, an early musical instrument. The simplified 乐 carries two readings: yuè for music, the original sense, and lè for joy, the happiness that music gives.
离	lí	to leave, to be apart, away from — Once the name of a netted mythical bird, 离 was borrowed so long ago for to part that almost no speaker recalls its feathers.
礼	lǐ	rites, ceremony, propriety — The character that encodes Confucian social order: the ceremonial forms that bind family, society, and cosmos into one coherent whole.
里	lǐ	inside, within; Chinese mile — From field stacked on earth, 里 named the bounded cultivated interior of a settlement and became the unit of distance that measured the realm's interior reach.
理	lǐ	principle, reason, to manage, texture — The veins running through a piece of jade, the lines a carver follows when cutting. From that grain grows everything ordered: the inner pattern of things, principle and reason, and the work of putting a matter in order.
力	lì	strength, power, force, effort — A picture of a plough or a flexed sinew, the image of bodily exertion. From that one shape grows everything pushed by force: raw strength, the capacity to do, and the effort poured into a task.
历	lì	to experience, history, calendar — One simplified glyph that absorbed two traditional characters: 歷 (to pass through, history) and 曆 (calendar). The word that holds both lived experience and reckoned time.
连	lián	to link, to join, even — Carts moving in a line: a character that joins things in space, in sequence, and in emphasis, from 连接 to the famous 连...都 construction.
脸	liǎn	face, cheeks, social face, reputation — The flesh-radical face that doubles as a ledger of dignity: in Chinese you can lose it, save it, or tear it apart.
练	liàn	to practice, to train, to refine, skilled — Before it meant drilling and rehearsal, 练 was a verb for boiling raw silk soft, the repetition of treatment that hardens into skill.
两	liǎng	two (of); a pair; the tael, the other word for two — 两 is the two used before measure words, the two of quantity and pairing, as opposed to 二 the two of counting and order, a distinction that is one of the first real puzzles a learner meets.
量	liàng	quantity, capacity; to measure — 商量 shangliang, to discuss, is literally to measure something out together: the act of weighing it between two minds before anyone decides.
零	líng	zero; raindrops, the fractional remainder, and the late invention of nothing — Zero began as falling raindrops and the leftover odd remainder, taking on the meaning of mathematical nothing only late, the placeholder that Chinese numerals long managed without.
六	liù	six; the six directions; a smooth and lucky number — Six closes space into the six directions (the four compass points plus up and down) and, through its near-homophony with 流 liú (to flow), became a number for things going smoothly.
龙	lóng	dragon, the imperial symbol — China's most powerful mythic creature: benevolent, not fearsome; the emperor's emblem and the Chinese people's self-described totem.
路	lù	road, path, route — The walked road, where 道 is the navigated way: 路 is the surface beneath the foot, the route the messenger takes, the journey of a life measured in distance.
论	lùn	to discuss, a theory or treatise — Reasoned speech put in order: the verb for discussing, debating, and commenting, and the noun for a theory, a view, or a treatise. Also Lun in the title of the Analects.
马	mǎ	horse, a common surname — A three-stroke pictograph of a horse in profile that hides the mane, legs, and tail of its towering ancestor 馬.
慢	màn	slow, unhurried — Pace becomes a moral register: 慢 màn carries the same heart radical as 快, and extends from physical slowness into social neglect and arrogance.
美	měi	beautiful, USA, delicious — A large sheep carrying horns: beauty as abundance, sacrifice, and the pleasure of the highest good.
门	mén	door, gate, family, field — A picture of a two-leaf gate. From the swinging doors grow the gateway, the household behind them, the school or trade you enter, and the measure word for whole courses of study.
面	miàn	face, surface, side — A pictograph of a face inside its outline that opened outward into surfaces, sides, and aspects, became a measure word for flat things, and in simplified script swallowed the separate character for flour and noodles.
民	mín	the people, a citizen, folk — The common people as a collective subject: the populace, the citizen, the folk and the nation, and the civilian set against the military. From 人民 the people to 民族 ethnic nation to 民主 democracy.
名	míng	name, fame, reputation — 名 is 夕 dusk plus 口 mouth: darkness falls, you cannot see who approaches, so you call out a name. Identity asserted through speech.
明	míng	bright, clear, tomorrow — Sun and moon combined: brightness, clarity, dawn, and understanding.
命	mìng	life, fate, command — A mouth speaking an order: 令 above, 口 inside. The character that holds together three of the heaviest words in Chinese: life, destiny, and the command from above.
木	mù	tree, wood — A tree seen whole: trunk, branches, roots. The pictograph that became a radical, a wuxing element, and the building block of 林, 森, 本, and 末.
目	mù	eye; to look at; section heading — The pictograph of an eye that became the Chinese radical for everything seen, watched, and classified.
南	nán	south; southern — The direction that Chinese buildings face and emperors receive guests: warmth, productivity, and the seat of imperial ritual power.
你	nǐ	you — The everyday second-person pronoun whose formal twin 您 embeds 心 (heart) directly into the character: address with warmth.
年	nián	year — A person carrying a harvest of grain: year as the cycle of agricultural labor, and the heart of the Chinese New Year tradition.
念	niàn	to recite, to keep in mind, to miss — 念 holds the present moment 今 over the heart 心: to keep something in mind, to recite aloud, to long for what is absent. The 念 of 念佛 recitation, 想念 longing, and the single thought-moment of Buddhist psychology.
牛	niú	ox, cow, cattle; (slang) awesome — A four-stroke pictograph of an ox head seen straight on, the horns still visible; in modern slang it means impressively good.
农	nóng	agriculture, farming, farmer, rural — A character compressed from a graph that showed working the fields at the time of the stars, the whole cycle of growing crops.
女	nǚ	woman, female — A kneeling figure with crossed arms: one of the most productive radicals in the script, present in characters for family relationships, feelings, and cultural concepts.
跑	pǎo	to run; to flee; to operate — The everyday verb of running: a foot-radical character whose semantic range stretches from "the legs in motion" to "running an errand" to "the engine is running" to a small but productive family of escape-and-flee compounds.
朋	péng	friend, companion; of the same group — Two strings of cowrie shells hung side by side, an old unit of value, borrowed early for companions of equal standing who keep close company.
品	pǐn	article, grade, character, to taste — Three mouths stacked: a character about multiplicity, evaluation, and the discerning assessment of quality, from product grades to moral character.
平	píng	flat, level, even, peaceful, ordinary — A pictograph of something level and balanced. From that flat surface grows a wide field of meaning: the even and the smooth, the equal and the fair, the calm and the peaceful, and the plain and ordinary.
七	qī	seven; the rhythm of mourning, festival, and the soul — Seven measures the cycles of death and renewal in Chinese culture: the seven-day periods of mourning, the seventh-night lovers' festival, and the seven emotions of the heart.
其	qí	its, that, their — A winnowing basket that became the pronoun holding classical Chinese together: the silent 'its' behind thousands of literary phrases.
气	qì	breath, vital energy — From weather to anger to the cosmic force underlying TCM and martial arts.
千	qiān	thousand; a vast, uncountable many — Thousand is the order of magnitude where a precise count gives way to poetic vastness: a thousand miles, a thousand autumns, ten thousand changes, the number that means simply too many to count.
前	qián	front, before, forward; former — Chinese places the past in front (前) and the future behind: the known faces you while the unknown approaches from behind, the inverse of the English spatial metaphor for time.
钱	qián	money, coin, currency — Money: the character that drives markets, proverbs, and the Chinese conversation about wealth.
清	qīng	clear, clean, pure, quiet — Water beside the color of new growth. The character that names limpid water, distinct speech, a clean reputation, a settled account, and a cool quiet evening.
情	qíng	feeling, affection, situation — The heart radical beside the phonetic 青: the movements of the heart. From that one image grows feeling and mood, affection and love, and the wider sense of a situation or state of affairs.
去	qù	to go, to leave, to depart, to remove — The character that marks every departure, from leaving the house to the euphemism for death. 去 is the verb of things moving away.
全	quán	whole, complete, entire, all — Enter, and find the jade flawless. From that image of unblemished jade grows everything whole and complete: the entire set, the intact and unbroken, and the adverb that sweeps up all of it at once.
然	rán	so, thus, the -ly manner suffix — A character that once meant to burn and was borrowed for the abstract sense so, correct, this-is-how-it-is. Today it builds the connectives 然后 and 然而, the conjunctions 当然 and 虽然, and rides as a -ly suffix on words like 忽然 and 突然.
热	rè	hot, heat, fever, ardent, popular — The four dots of flame beneath 执 carry physical heat into the heat of crowds, fervor, and whatever is trending.
人	rén	person, humanity — The character for human being, and its echo in 仁 rén, Confucian benevolence.
认	rèn	to recognize, to acknowledge — The act of recognition: knowing something for what it is, owning up to it, and the small set of compounds that turn this on the world, on others, and on oneself.
日	rì	sun, day, Japan — The oldest pictograph in the script: a circle with a dot for the sun, now a radical in hundreds of characters related to time, light, and warmth.
容	róng	to contain, to allow, appearance — A roof over a valley: the space that holds. To contain, to tolerate, the face one shows, and the easy in rongyi.
肉	ròu	meat, flesh, the pulp of fruit — A pictograph of a meat slab with its marbling drawn inside; squeezed onto the left of a character it becomes 月, the flesh-moon of the body radicals.
如	rú	like, as, if, according to — A woman speaking and a mouth complying: from following a command grew the great comparison-and-condition word. 如 is how Chinese says like, as, for example, and if.
三	sān	three; thrice; repeatedly — Three is the number of completion in Chinese cosmology (Heaven, Earth, Human), and the numeral that generates the ten thousand things in Daoist thought.
色	sè	color, appearance, desire — A kneeling figure beneath a person: color as visible manifestation, and the character that links aesthetics to desire.
山	shān	mountain, hill, sacred peak — Three peaks rising from the earth: the oldest pictograph and the axis of Chinese sacred geography.
善	shàn	goodness; virtue; skilled — The character at the heart of Mencius argument that human nature is originally good: the moral sprouts waiting to be cultivated.
上	shàng	above, up, to go up, to mount — The most productive spatial primitive in Chinese. 上 starts as a notch above a line and ends up meaning everything from logging on to getting addicted.
少	shǎo	few; little; young — Small with a stroke removed: the character for scarcity that doubles as the word for youth when read in falling tone.
社	shè	society, an association, the god of the soil — The altar radical beside the earth: a shrine to the god of the soil and the community that gathered around it. From that image grow society itself, the many kinds of association and agency, and the ancient soil-and-grain spirits that once stood for the state.
神	shén	spirit; deity; divine — The character that names both the gods of Chinese folk religion and the focused attention of the human mind, because in Chinese thought they belong to the same category of phenomenon.
生	shēng	life, birth, raw, student — A sprouting plant: life emerging from the earth. The most generative radical in Chinese.
声	shēng	sound, voice, tone, reputation — A chime-stone struck before a listening ear. From that image grow the everyday word for sound and voice, the tone of speech, and the wider sense of making something known: a declaration, a reputation, a name.
十	shí	ten; complete; cross — 十 is written as a cross (a vertical stroke cutting through a horizontal one), the point where all four cardinal directions meet, making ten the numeral of total completion.
时	shí	time, hour, season — The sun moving: time as something visible, measurable, and morally weighted.
实	shí	real, solid, true, actual — A roof over a house full of goods. From that image of a full dwelling grows everything substantial: the real and the true, the solid and sturdy, the actual practice that gets things done, and the ripened fruit.
世	shì	world, age, generation, lifetime — A pictograph traditionally read as three tens joined together, the span of thirty years that makes one generation. From that root grow the four faces of 世: the generation that succeeds the one before, the lifetime or the life to come, the era stacked from many lives, and the wider world of human society in which all of it unfolds.
事	shì	matter, affair, thing-that-happens — The character for an affair, a matter, a thing that happens. From an ancient pictograph of a hand holding the implements of office, it became Chinese for everything abstract that occupies us: business, jobs, incidents, and the everyday "no problem."
是	shì	to be — The copula, the cleft construction, classical 是非 right-and-wrong.
手	shǒu	hand — A five-fingered hand drawn from life: one of the most productive radicals in the script, present in hundreds of action characters.
首	shǒu	head, first, chief, a measure word for songs and poems — A drawn head, with hair above an eye, and from it the senses of first, foremost, chief, leader, and the classifier you count songs and poems with. 首 is its own radical.
书	shū	book, writing, letter — Book, writing, calligraphy: the character that holds civilization together.
水	shuǐ	water — The first of the five elements, the radical that runs through hundreds of compounds, and the Daoist image of how to act in the world.
说	shuō	to speak, to say, to explain — To speak, say, explain, and persuade: the character at the center of all communication.
思	sī	to think, to miss, thought — The field of the mind above the heart: thinking as cultivation, longing as thought turned inward.
死	sǐ	death, to die, dead, fixed — A figure kneeling beside a corpse. The character behind the strongest verb of finality in Chinese, the bedrock of every taboo, and the intensifier that turns 累 into 累死了.
四	sì	four; the four directions; the homophone of death — Four organizes the Chinese world into quarters, the four directions, four seas, four seasons, yet its near-homophony with 死 sǐ (death) makes it the most avoided number in modern China.
他	tā	he, him; another — 他 was gender-neutral for a millennium until 她 was coined in 1917 to solve a translation problem that Classical Chinese had never needed to solve.
它	tā	it; the non-human third person — 它 began as a picture of a snake and once meant other; in the twentieth century it was reassigned to the non-human third person, completing the homophonous 他/她/它 triad that exists only in writing.
她	tā	she, her — 她 is the youngest character in everyday Chinese, invented in 1917 to translate "she," and the cause of one of the loudest grammatical fights in modern Chinese letters.
太	tài	too, extremely, highest, grand — The character 大 (big) with one extra dot, pushing big past its limit into too-much. From that excess grow the everyday too, the highest and grandest, the sun, and the supreme pole of Taiji.
题	tí	topic, question, problem, to inscribe — The head-radical character behind every exam question and discussion topic. From the leaf or head of a thing grows the question to solve, the theme to discuss, and the act of inscribing a poem.
体	tǐ	body, substance, to experience — The person beside the root: the body as a person's base. From the physical body grow substance and system, the whole as against the part, and the act of grasping a thing by living through it.
天	tiān	sky, heaven, day — Heaven as moral authority, weather, time, and the ground of 天命 the Mandate of Heaven.
听	tīng	to listen, to hear, to obey — The simplified 听 = mouth + axe, but the traditional 聽 encodes whole-body Confucian listening: ear, eye, heart, and upright virtue arrayed together in a single glyph.
通	tōng	to go through; to connect; general; fluent — The walk-movement radical drives a path through every obstacle, giving 通 a single character that covers passage, communication, expertise, and the ordinary general state of things.
同	tóng	same, together, with — Voices joined under one cover: the character for sameness and shared ground. From it grow the words for being alike, doing things together, and the people who share your role, your class, your cause.
头	tóu	head, top, end, chief, noun-suffix — The head, and from it the top of a hill, the end of a street, the start of a thing, the chief of a group, and the toneless tag on a hundred everyday nouns. The simplified 头 stands for traditional 頭.
图	tú	map, diagram, to plan, to seek — A character for diagrams, maps, intentions, and plots: anything that represents the world at one remove, or seeks to reshape it.
土	tǔ	earth, soil, local, native — A mound of soil with a base: the earth phase of the Five Elements, and the root of hundreds of characters for land, construction, and cultural belonging.
外	wài	outside, outer, foreign, besides — A diviner reading the shells at the wrong hour, off the normal schedule. From that image grows everything beyond the boundary: the outside, the outer surface, the foreign land, and the relatives on the mother's side.
完	wán	to finish; to be whole — 完 means both whole and finished, and the bridge between them is intact completeness; its most important job is as a result complement, the 完 that ends 吃完, 做完, and 看完.
万	wàn	ten thousand; myriad; all — 万 is the Chinese hyperbole unit. Where English reaches for millions, Chinese reaches for 万 to express the immeasurable, because the written number 10,000 originally meant scorpion.
王	wáng	king — Three horizontals joined by one vertical: the ruler who connects heaven, earth, and humanity. Also the most common Chinese surname, and a radical that is secretly 玉 (jade) in disguise.
望	wàng	to gaze afar, to hope, reputation — A person on a mound looking into the distance, with the moon above: from that raised gaze grow three senses. To gaze and look afar (眺望, 望远镜, 张望), to hope and expect (希望, 期望, 盼望, 失望), and reputation or prestige (名望, 威望, 声望). As a coverb it can even mean toward, like 朝 or 向.
危	wēi	danger, peril, precarious, lofty height — A kneeling figure pinned beneath a cliff overhang spells danger, where the same overhang also carries the sound.
为	wéi	to do; to act; for; because of — In oracle bone script, a hand leading an elephant (to guide an immense force through skillful agency), and the character at the center of the Daoist concept of non-action.
文	wén	writing, culture, civil — A tattooed figure: writing as marks on the body, culture as the accumulated markings of civilization.
问	wèn	to ask, to inquire, to ask after — A mouth at the gate, asking. From that image grow the everyday verb to ask, the noun for a question or problem, the warm act of asking after someone, and the stern act of holding to account.
我	wǒ	I, me, myself — The character for 'I' began as a toothed bronze halberd: the assertive grip of a weapon became the grammatical mark of a self.
屋	wū	room, house, dwelling — Room, dwelling, and the human need for shelter: the character at home.
无	wú	nothing, without, non-being — A dancer with arms outstretched, holding nothing. The character behind every Chinese negation of having, every Daoist non-doing, and the philosophical pole that pairs with 有 to frame the whole cosmos.
五	wǔ	five; the organizing number of correlative cosmology — Five is the center plus the four directions, the number that completes the Chinese cosmos: five phases, five colors, five tones, five flavors, five viscera, all keyed to one master grid.
武	wǔ	martial, military, valiant — The foot radical 止 carrying a dagger-axe 戈: the soldier on the march, and the older reading that to wield arms well is finally to halt them. From 武力 armed force to 武术 martial arts to 威武 powerful and dignified, the character holds the whole world of strength wielded with discipline.
物	wù	thing, object, matter, being — An ox under a phonetic, once the word for varicolored cattle, that broadened into the whole world of things: every object, all matter, the myriad creatures, and even a person as a figure.
西	xī	west; western — A bird settling into its nest at sunset: the direction that gave Chinese the word for "things" when it paired with east.
喜	xǐ	joy, to like, happy events — A festive drum sounding above an open mouth: the sound of joy and the smiling mouth that answers it. From this image grow the words for liking, delight, and every happy occasion, above all the wedding.
系	xì	to connect, system, department — A hand holding silk threads. From that image of holding strands together come the great connecting words of modern Chinese: the system, the relationship, the link, the university department, and the colloquial act of tying a knot.
下	xià	below, down, to descend, to issue — The mirror image of 上: a notch below the line that governs everything from going downstairs to issuing imperial decrees.
现	xiàn	to appear, present, now — The luster of jade coming into view. From that glint of something becoming visible grows the whole vocabulary of appearing, of the present moment, and of what is ready and on hand.
相	xiāng	mutual, appearance, to examine, minister — An eye examining a tree. From that act of looking grow two readings: level-tone xiang, the mutual that binds two sides face to face, and falling-tone xiang, the appearance you look at, the looking itself, and the minister who looks over the realm.
香	xiāng	fragrant, delicious, incense, popular — Grain stacked over a mouth of sweetness: 香 is the smell of ripe rice, and the word still means both perfume and a hot meal worth eating.
想	xiǎng	to think, to want, to miss — 想 = 相 (mutual, facing) + 心 (heart): the heart turned toward something, carrying the full range from thought to desire to longing in a single gesture.
向	xiàng	direction, toward, to face, all along — A window opening in a wall, facing one way. From that image grows the whole field of orientation: the direction a thing points, the preposition toward a target, the inclination of a heart, and the sense of all along, up to now.
小	xiǎo	small, little; humble — Three minimal strokes that became the Confucian axis of moral character: the 小人 (petty person) and the 君子 (exemplary person) are the two poles every learner of classical Chinese must know.
笑	xiào	to laugh, to smile, to mock — Bamboo over a bending person: a form that has split scholars since the Song over whether laughter or rustling reeds came first.
写	xiě	to write; to describe — 写 simplified from 寫, which shows a magpie under a roof: the bird that mimics and transmits, giving its voice to the written mark.
心	xīn	heart, mind — The radical at the root of all feeling, and the Chinese conception of thought itself.
新	xīn	new; fresh; recently; novel — Chopping fresh wood with an axe: the character whose compounds span New Year greetings, fresh produce, marriage, and the great question of what counts as modern.
信	xìn	trust, faith, credibility — A person standing by their word: one of the most visually transparent characters in the language, and the virtue that holds every contract, conversation, and relationship together.
行	xíng	to walk, to act, a row, a trade — One of the great polyphonic characters: xíng "to walk; to act; conduct" and háng "row; profession; firm." A pictograph of a four-way crossroads, the place where walking and the world are organized.
形	xíng	shape, form, to appear, to compare — The character for visible form: shape and figure, the body as form, the act of taking shape and being described, and the lay of a situation. A pictophonetic compound built on the pattern-strokes radical 彡.
性	xìng	nature, character, sex/gender, the -ness/-ity suffix — The heart radical carrying the sense of innate nature: what a thing is born to be. From that core grow the four faces of 性: a persons disposition or character, sex and gender, the property or quality of a thing, and the productive abstract suffix that turns adjectives into -ness and -ity nouns.
学	xu é	to study, to learn, learning — Hands guiding a child beneath a roof: the character for learning as both action and institution.
雪	xu ě	snow, whiteness, and vengeance — Rain over a sweeping hand: snow is the precipitation you can hold. The same character means dazzling white and, by a buried metaphor, to wipe clean an old grievance.
言	yán	speech, word, language — The root of every word about words: the mouth open, the tongue moving, the speech rising.
眼	yǎn	eye, glance — The everyday word for eye, and by extension any small opening, any moment of looking, any judgment made through sight. The body-part counterpart to literary 目.
阳	yáng	sun, bright, the yang principle — A sunlit mound: the simplified 阳 sets a hill beside the sun. From that bright south slope grow the sun itself, the active masculine half of the cosmos, and the place names that face the light.
样	yàng	shape, kind, manner, sample — A word once rooted in wood that grew into the language of appearance and kind: how a thing looks, what type it is, the sample held up as a model, and the everyday this-way and how of manner words.
要	yào	to want, must, essential — Want, need, must, and will: the grammar engine disguised as a single character.
也	yě	also; too; classical particle — A pictograph that lost its original image entirely and became one of the most load-bearing particles in classical Chinese: the gavel at the end of every verdict.
业	yè	occupation, industry, undertaking, study — A notched rack for hanging bells and chimes, then the work hung upon it. From that frame grows the whole field of doing: the trade one practices, the industry one builds, the cause one devotes a life to, the coursework one masters, and in Buddhist thought the karma one accrues.
一	yī	one; unified; as soon as — The simplest mark a brush can make carries the heaviest philosophical weight in Chinese civilization: 道生一，一生二，the Dao generates One, and One generates everything.
以	yǐ	by means of; using; because; in order to — A classical Chinese preposition and conjunction so versatile that it anchors half the grammar of formal Chinese: the word that turns one thing into the means, standard, or cause of another.
义	yì	righteousness, duty, justice — The Confucian virtue of doing what is morally right, and the simplification that erased a sheep, a self, and the entire picture of what righteous form looks like.
意	yì	meaning, intention, the wish of the mind — Sound over heart: 意 is what the heart means before it is spoken. From that image grows meaning and significance, intention and willingness, and the notions of attention, satisfaction, and the unexpected.
因	yīn	cause, reason, because of, to follow — A person resting inside an enclosure, the mat one lies on. From that image of lying-upon comes the basis one builds on, then the cause behind an effect and the because that links the two.
阴	yīn	the shaded slope, the lunar, the covert — The shadow half of the cosmos and the everyday word for an overcast day. From the dark side of a hill grew the lunar calendar, the hidden plot, and time itself as light and shade.
音	yīn	sound, tone, news — A mouth with a sound marked inside it. From that image grow the words for sound and music, for the way a syllable is pronounced, and for tidings carried from afar.
应	yīng	ought, should; (yìng) to respond, to answer, to handle — A high-frequency polyphone with two readings. As yīng it means ought to or should; as yìng it means to respond, to answer, to adapt, and to handle. The tone tells you whether the sense is obligation or response.
用	yòng	to use; function; expense — 用 began as a pictograph of a bucket or bell (a vessel put to work) and became the character that Zhuangzi inverted in his doctrine of 无用之用: the use of uselessness.
有	yǒu	to have, to exist — Possession, existence, and the have/be distinction that reshapes grammar.
右	yòu	right; to assist; to favor — A right hand raised to a mouth: the hand that feeds and speaks, whose authority in Chinese extended to favoring, protecting, and placing the highest things to the right of the seat.
于	yú	at, in, to, than (the classical preposition) — The workhorse preposition of formal Chinese. 于 marks where, when, toward whom, and against what a thing is measured, and it is bound into a long list of everyday disyllables like 由于, 终于, and 等于.
雨	yǔ	rain — The oracle bone shows rain falling from the sky: one of the most transparent pictographs in the script, and the radical that organizes all Chinese weather.
语	yǔ	language, speech, words — Speech radical plus the phonetic 吾 (I, me): the character for language carries the self within it, encoding the idea that language is the medium through which the self speaks to the world.
远	yuǎn	far, distant, remote — Long robes trailing as you walk away: distance encoded in motion, and the ambivalence of far-off places in Chinese thought.
月	yu è	moon, month — A crescent moon in profile, and one of the most poetically charged characters in Chinese literature.
越	yu è	to cross over, to surpass — The character of crossing-and-exceeding: a foot-radical verb that travels from "stride over a barrier" to "the more, the more" to a Chinese state name and a national poetic gesture.
云	yún	cloud — The simplified 云 is older than the traditional 雲, a rare case where the modern form recovers the ancient one.
再	zài	again, then, further — The adverb that sequences and repeats: again, one more time, and the subtle art of "and then."
在	zài	to be at, located, in progress — The character that locates: a seedling rooted in earth, expanded into location, preposition, and progressive aspect.
站	zhàn	to stand, to halt, a station, a stop — A person planted upright on the ground, holding their place: from the body that will not move grows the station, the stop, and the website you log on to.
长	cháng	long; to grow; chief — A person with flowing long hair. One character, two readings: cháng (long) and zhǎng (to grow, elder), the richest dual-reading in Mandarin.
真	zhēn	real, true, genuine — Truth not as accuracy but as authenticity: the unadorned thing itself, prized in Daoist self-cultivation and in everyday emphasis.
正	zhèng	upright, correct, exactly, in the midst of — A foot halting squarely on the target line. From that image grows everything straight and correct: the upright, the standard, the exact moment, and the ongoing right-now.
知	zhī	to know; knowledge — 知 = arrow + mouth: knowledge as the swift, target-hitting utterance, and in Wang Yangming, the act of knowing is inseparable from the act of doing.
智	zhì	wisdom, moral intelligence — The Confucian virtue of knowing what is right to do; separately, the Buddhist prajñā that sees through the nature of things altogether.
中	zhōng	middle, China, to hit the mark — An arrow piercing through the center of a target: the character that names China as the Middle Kingdom and encodes the Confucian virtue of equilibrium.
重	zhòng	heavy, important; again, to repeat — A figure bearing a heavy load. From that weight grows everything that carries gravity: the heavy, the important, the grave. Read again as chóng, the same load comes back, layered and repeated.
主	zhǔ	master, host, main, to advocate — A lamp flame steady on its stand: the central light that grew into the master of a household, the main thing among many, the one who decides, and the Lord of a faith.
子	zǐ	child, seed, Master — A picture of a swaddled infant that grew into one of the busiest characters in the language: a child and a son, the honorific for a Master, the empty noun-suffix on a thousand words, and the seed of a thing.
字	zì	character, written word — The character that names itself, and the whole system of 汉字.
自	zì	self, from, naturally — A picture of a nose, the thing an ancient speaker pointed at to mean me. From that gesture grows the self, the source a thing comes from, and the sense of a thing acting of its own accord.
走	zǒu	to walk, to go, to leave — A figure in motion with feet below: the basic verb of movement that Chinese uses where English uses dozens of different words.
足	zú	foot; sufficient; enough — The foot that became the word for sufficiency, and the radical anchoring every character involving legs, running, and roads.
最	zuì	most; the superlative marker — 最 is the single word that pushes any adjective to its limit: the -est of English, the most of comparison, the marker that turns 好 into 最好 and 后 into 最后.
左	zuǒ	left; unorthodox; to assist — A left hand holding a carpenter's square: the assisting hand at the ruler, and by extension the word for unorthodox, heterodox, and everything that deviates from the orthodox path.
