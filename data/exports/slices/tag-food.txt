//角落書屋/Slices/Food
饱	bǎo	full, satisfied, to eat one's fill — The food radical 饣 wraps the sound of 包 (bāo), so the glyph itself is a stomach packed full like a dumpling.
菜	cài	vegetable, dish, cuisine — Plants gathered to be eaten. From the picked greens grows a whole table: the vegetable, the cooked dish, the regional cuisine, and the slangy put-down for anyone weak at a task.
吃	chī	to eat — To eat, and the extraordinary range of Chinese life encoded in a single food verb.
蛋	dàn	egg, egg-shaped thing, scoundrel — A pictophonetic egg: the 虫 radical for small creatures sits under a sound element, and in slang it turns people into scoundrels.
点	diǎn	dot, point, a little, o'clock — From a single dot of ink the character spreads into points and decimals, into the hours of the clock, into ordering food and lighting a flame, and into the small quantity that softens a sentence.
馆	guǎn	a public building, hall, shop, establishment — A food-radical building: the eating-house that grew into the library, the museum, and the embassy.
喝	hē	to drink; (hè) to shout, to cry out — A mouth radical paired with the phonetic 曷, splitting cleanly into the everyday hē you drink with and the older hè you shout with.
鸡	jī	chicken, fowl, rooster — The simplified 鸡 swaps a chicken-coop hand 又 in where the traditional 雞 kept the sound-bearer 奚 beside the bird.
苦	kǔ	bitter, hardship, suffering, painstaking — The grass radical over 古 names a bitter herb on the tongue, then stretches to every flavor of hardship and effort.
面	miàn	face, surface, side — A pictograph of a face inside its outline that opened outward into surfaces, sides, and aspects, became a measure word for flat things, and in simplified script swallowed the separate character for flour and noodles.
牛	niú	ox, cow, cattle; (slang) awesome — A four-stroke pictograph of an ox head seen straight on, the horns still visible; in modern slang it means impressively good.
肉	ròu	meat, flesh, the pulp of fruit — A pictograph of a meat slab with its marbling drawn inside; squeezed onto the left of a character it becomes 月, the flesh-moon of the body radicals.
香	xiāng	fragrant, delicious, incense, popular — Grain stacked over a mouth of sweetness: 香 is the smell of ripe rice, and the word still means both perfume and a hot meal worth eating.
饱经风霜	bǎo jīng fēng shuāng	to have weathered wind and frost: hardened by hardship — 饱 here means having had a full share of something. The image is a face lined by years of exposure; used of people who have lived through long adversity. Common in literary and journalistic prose.
饱食终日	bǎo shí zhōng rì	to eat one's fill all day: to idle in comfort — From the Analects (论语), where Confucius reproaches those who eat their fill the whole day and apply their minds to nothing. A pointed criticism of idleness; carries a moral edge.
饱学之士	bǎo xu é zhī shì	a scholar full of learning: an erudite person — 饱 extends from a full stomach to a full mind. 饱学 means saturated with learning; a respectful term for a deeply read scholar. Formal register.
饱以老拳	bǎo yǐ lǎo quán	to treat to a full helping of fists: to give a sound beating — A grim joke on the eating sense: instead of a meal, the recipient gets a generous serving of punches. From the Jìn Shū (晋书). Vivid and slightly humorous in tone.
别馆离宫	bié guǎn lí gōng	separate lodges and detached palaces: the same scattered residences, with the lodge named first — The attested inversion of 离宫别馆, listed as its own form in idiom dictionaries. Putting 馆 first foregrounds the lodging over the palace.
茶馆酒肆	chá guǎn jiǔ sì	teahouses and wine-shops: the everyday public houses of a town — A four-character set phrase pairing 茶馆 with 酒肆 (the wine-shop) to stand for the street-level places where ordinary people gathered, drank, and talked.
吃喝玩乐	chī hē wán lè	eat, drink, play, be merry: a life of indulgence — Four verbs of pleasure strung together, with 喝 as the drinking. Usually mildly disapproving, said of someone who does nothing but enjoy themselves.
吃苦耐劳	chī kǔ nài láo	to endure hardship and persevere in labor — a core work-ethic virtue — The defining work-ethic phrase in Chinese culture. Applied to individuals, generations, and national character. Eating bitterness + tolerating labor = the formula for achievement.
吃亏是福	chī kuī shì fú	suffering loss is a blessing — short-term disadvantage leads to long-term benefit — A proverb attributed to Zheng Banqiao (Qing dynasty). The Daoist-inflected wisdom: accepting being at a disadvantage cultivates humility and patience, which eventually bring greater good. Widely quoted in Chinese business and parenting contexts.
大吃大喝	dà chī dà hē	to eat and drink lavishly: feast extravagantly — The doubled 大 stresses excess. Modern usage often flags wasteful banqueting or living beyond one's means.
大喝一声	dà hè yī shēng	to give a great shout: bellow once, loudly — Here 喝 takes its hè reading, "to shout". A stock phrase in classical fiction and storytelling, the warrior or judge who freezes a room with a single roar.
点石成金	diǎn shí chéng jīn	to touch stone and turn it to gold: to transform the ordinary into the priceless — Lit: touch-stone-become-gold. From Daoist immortal lore, the touch that turns base rock to gold. Now used of an editor, teacher, or craftsman who makes something dull suddenly shine.
对牛弹琴	duì niú tán qín	playing the qin to a cow — speaking to someone incapable of understanding — From a Han dynasty text attributed to 牟融 Móu Róng. A musician plays the most refined music for a cow; the cow goes on eating grass. The problem is not the cow's intelligence but the category mismatch: the music is addressed to the wrong kind of listener.
古色古香	gǔ sè gǔ xiāng	of antique flavor: redolent of the past in look and feel — Literally "ancient color, ancient scent." Praises a building, room, or object that has a tasteful, antique character, steeped in the atmosphere of an older age. A favorite for describing a classical garden, a tea house, or an old street that has kept its period charm.
骨肉相连	gǔ ròu xiāng lián	bone and flesh joined together: bound by blood kinship — The image is that bone and flesh cannot be pulled apart. Used for the inseparable bond between family members, and figuratively for a homeland and its people.
含辛茹苦	hán xīn rú kǔ	to hold pungency and swallow bitterness: to endure every hardship — 茹 (rú) means to eat. Almost always said of parents, especially mothers, who suffer silently for years to raise their children.
汗牛充栋	hàn niú chōng dòng	to sweat the oxen and fill the rafters: a vast number of books — Coined by the Tang writer Liǔ Zōngyuán. So many volumes that hauling them makes the oxen sweat and stacking them fills the house to the roofbeams. Praise for a great library or prolific output.
喝西北风	hē xī běi fēng	to drink the northwest wind: have nothing to eat, go hungry — A vivid colloquial idiom: with no food, all you can swallow is the cold wind. Said wryly of poverty or a failed livelihood, "我们都去喝西北风了".
鹤立鸡群	hè lì jī qún	a crane standing among chickens: head and shoulders above the rest — The tall white crane towering over a yard of squat fowl. Said of a person whose talent or bearing makes them stand out from an ordinary crowd.
画龙点睛	huà lóng diǎn jīng	drawing a dragon and dotting the eye — the small finishing touch that brings the whole alive — From a Tang anecdote: the painter Zhang Sengyou painted dragons but refused to add eyes; when pressed, he dotted the eyes of two , and they immediately flew off the wall. The dot is what brings the form to life. Used today for the small but decisive move that makes a piece of work click.
鸡蛋里挑骨头	jī dàn lǐ tiāo gǔ tou	to pick bones out of an egg: to nitpick, to find fault where there is none — A vivid colloquial saying: an egg has no bones, so hunting for one means inventing flaws. Used of a critic or boss who complains about everything.
鸡飞蛋打	jī fēi dàn dǎ	the hen flies off and the eggs break: to lose everything, ending up empty-handed — A botched scheme where both the chicken and its eggs are lost. Said when an attempt to grab more leaves you with nothing at all.
鸡毛蒜皮	jī máo suàn pí	chicken feathers and garlic skins: trivial, petty matters — The kitchen sweepings nobody bothers with. Used to dismiss a quarrel or complaint as not worth the fuss: 别为这点鸡毛蒜皮的事吵架.
九牛一毛	jiǔ niú yī máo	"one hair from nine oxen" — a trifling amount; a drop in the bucket — 九牛 (nine oxen) and 一毛 (a single hair). A lone hair plucked from nine whole oxen is utterly negligible against the mass it comes from. The phrase comes from Sima Qian, who wrote that his own death would weigh on the world no more than one hair lost from nine oxen.
苦尽甘来	kǔ jìn gān lái	bitterness ends, sweetness comes: hardship gives way to better days — 苦 (bitter) and 甘 (sweet) frame a full turn of fortune. The standard consolation offered to anyone enduring a long hard stretch; the reward is built into the saying.
苦口婆心	kǔ kǒu pó xīn	bitter mouth, grandmotherly heart: to urge earnestly and patiently — Describes someone who repeats hard advice tirelessly out of genuine care, the way an old woman nags her grandchild for their own good.
狼吞虎咽	láng tūn hǔ yàn	to wolf down food — to eat ravenously like a wolf and tiger — Lit: wolf-swallow tiger-gulp. Vivid image of eating without manners or restraint. Used humorously for someone who is very hungry or eating too fast.
离宫别馆	lí gōng bié guǎn	detached palaces and separate lodges: the many residences a ruler kept beyond the main palace — A classical 成语 for the scattered country retreats and travelling-lodges of an emperor or great house. Here 馆 carries its oldest sense, the official lodge where the powerful stayed on the road.
怜香惜玉	lián xiāng xī yù	pity the fragrance, cherish the jade: to be tender toward women — 香 (fragrance) and 玉 (jade) both stand for a beautiful woman. To treat women with gentleness and care. Literary, sometimes ironic.
良药苦口	liáng yào kǔ kǒu	good medicine is bitter to the mouth: honest advice is hard to hear — From the Kǒngzǐ Jiāyǔ. Often completed by 忠言逆耳 (loyal words grate on the ear). Used to soften unwelcome but useful criticism.
满面春风	mǎn miàn chūn fēng	a face full of spring breeze: beaming, radiant with joy — Lit: full-face-spring-wind. The whole face glowing like a warm spring day, said of someone visibly pleased, content, or flush with good fortune.
面不改色	miàn bù gǎi sè	"face doesn't change color" — unruffled; keeping composure under pressure; poker-faced — Lit: face-not-change-color. The mark of composure under pressure , your face doesn't betray fear, surprise, or discomfort. 他面不改色地接受了批评，继续进行演讲。"He received the criticism without changing expression and continued his speech." Often admired as a sign of courage or self-discipline.
面红耳赤	miàn hóng ěr chì	face red, ears crimson — flushed with anger, embarrassment, or the heat of argument — 面 miàn (face) + 红 hóng (red) + 耳 ěr (ears) + 赤 chì (the deeper crimson). The face and ears flush red from a surge of emotion, most often a heated argument or acute embarrassment. 两个人争得面红耳赤，谁也不让谁。
面面俱到	miàn miàn jù dào	every aspect covered; thorough in all directions — 面 miàn (face; surface; aspect) + 面 + 俱 jù (all; together) + 到 (arrived at). Arrival has reached every face, every surface, every angle of the matter. Used to praise comprehensive coverage , a plan that misses nothing, a speech that addresses every concern, an analysis that leaves no gap.
面有菜色	miàn yǒu cài sè	a face with the color of greens: gaunt and sallow from hunger — Lit: face-has-vegetable-color. 菜色 is the wan, greenish pallor of someone who has eaten nothing but wild greens, with no grain or meat. The phrase paints a face worn down by famine or hardship.
鸟语花香	niǎo yǔ huā xiāng	birds singing, flowers fragrant: a beautiful spring scene — A stock phrase for an idyllic countryside in spring, the birds talking and the blossoms in scent. Warm and common in description.
牛刀小试	niú dāo xiǎo shì	a small test of the ox-cleaver: great talent on a minor task — From the cook Páo Dīng of the Zhuāngzǐ, whose ox-carving blade glides through every joint. A heavy cleaver lightly tried, said when a capable person handles an easy job and shows the skill in reserve.
杀鸡儆猴	shā jī jǐng hóu	kill the chicken to warn the monkey: punish one to frighten the rest — Make an example of a minor offender so the watching crowd falls into line. Common in talk of office discipline and political crackdowns.
书香门第	shū xiāng mén dì	a family with a fragrance of books — a family of scholars and learning — Lit: book-fragrance-door-lineage. A family where books are the tradition and education runs through the generations. High cultural prestige in Chinese society. 书香 shūxiāng "the fragrance of books" is itself a vivid compound.
完蛋	wán dàn	finished egg: to be done for, ruined, all over — Not a four-character chengyu but a fixed colloquial expression. 完 (wán, to finish) + 蛋 marks total collapse: 完蛋了 (wándàn le) means we're finished, it's all over.
闻鸡起舞	wén jī qǐ wǔ	hear the cock and rise to practice swordplay: diligent self-discipline — From the Jin Shu account of Zǔ Tì (祖逖), who woke at the rooster's first crow to drill with his sword. A praise word for someone who studies or trains relentlessly.
小菜一碟	xiǎo cài yī dié	a small dish on a plate: a piece of cake, an easy matter — Lit: small-dish-one-saucer. A 小菜 is a minor side dish, trivial next to the main course, so 小菜一碟 means a task so easy it is hardly worth mentioning. 这对我来说是小菜一碟 "this is a piece of cake for me."
行尸走肉	xíng shī zǒu ròu	a walking corpse, a moving piece of flesh — alive in body but dead in spirit — Lit: walking-corpse, walking-flesh. Said of someone who has lost the inner life that makes a person alive in any meaningful sense , going through motions without thought, feeling, or purpose. Sharp, almost cruel; reserved for those judged to have given up entirely.
血肉相连	xu è ròu xiāng lián	blood and flesh linked: tied as closely as flesh to blood — A near-twin of 骨肉相连, stressing the living, circulating closeness between people, or between an individual and a cause they cannot separate from.
一点一滴	yì diǎn yì dī	a dot and a drop: bit by bit, little by little — Lit: one-dot-one-drop. The small increments by which patient effort accumulates. 一点一滴地积累 "to build up bit by bit."
以卵击石	yǐ luǎn jī shí	to throw an egg at a rock: to attempt the hopelessly outmatched — Here the egg is 卵 (luǎn), the classical word for egg, but the image is the same fragile shell shattering on stone. Describes a doomed fight against overwhelming force.
鱼肉百姓	yú ròu bǎi xìng	to make fish-meat of the common people: to oppress and exploit them — Here 鱼肉 is a verb: to treat people as meat to be carved up. A classical condemnation of tyrannical officials, traceable to the Shǐjì. Still used to denounce abuse of power.
指指点点	zhǐ zhǐ diǎn diǎn	to point and gesture: to criticize or gossip about others behind their backs — The reduplicated 指点 zhǐdiǎn (to point out, to instruct) turns sour here: to keep pointing fingers and passing judgment on what is not one's business.
艾草	ài cǎo	mugwort; wormwood (Artemisia argyi) — 艾 (ài, mugwort) + 草 (cǎo, herb, grass). Hung over doorways on the Double Fifth to repel evil and insects, owing to its strong fragrance. Also used in traditional Chinese medicine (艾灸 àijiǔ, moxibustion) and in cooking (青团 qīngtuán, green rice cakes flavored with mugwort, eaten at Qīngmíng).
鹌鹑蛋	ān chún dàn	quail egg
八大菜系	bā dà cài xì	the Eight Great Cuisines — every regional banquet runs on baijiu
白菜	bái cài	Chinese cabbage; bok choy family — 白 bái (white) + 菜 cài (vegetable). The pale-stalked cabbage that anchors northern winter cooking. So cheap and plentiful that 白菜价 "cabbage price" means dirt cheap.
白酒	bái jiǔ	baijiu — the spirit most often paired with a hotpot meal
白切鸡	bái qiē jī	White-Cut Chicken
包	bāo	to wrap, bundle — To wrap, to enclose , the verb for making dumplings (包饺子), as well as the root of 包子 (stuffed bun). Also means "to guarantee" or "to take responsibility for."
饱餐	bǎo cān	to eat a hearty meal — 饱 (full) + 餐 (cān, meal): to eat heartily, to feast. Often appears as 饱餐一顿 (bǎocān yí dùn, to have a good filling meal), with a faint literary warmth.
饱含	bǎo hán	to be brimming with — 饱 (full) + 含 (hán, to hold inside): to be charged with, brimming with. Used for tears, feeling, or meaning, as in 饱含深情 (bǎohán shēnqíng, brimming with deep feeling).
饱和	bǎo hé	saturated, saturation — 饱 (full) + 和 (hé, to mix, to combine): saturation. A technical term for a solution, market, or air that can absorb no more, as in 市场已经饱和 (shìchǎng yǐjīng bǎohé, the market is already saturated).
饱满	bǎo mǎn	full, plump; brimming — 饱 (full) + 满 (mǎn, full to the brim): plump and rounded, or figuratively brimming with vigor. Describes grain, fruit, and spirits alike, as in 精神饱满 (jīngshén bǎomǎn, full of energy).
笨蛋	bèn dàn	idiot, fool — 笨 (bèn, stupid, clumsy) + 蛋: a fool or dimwit. Among close friends it softens into teasing, even affection, but aimed at a stranger it lands as a real insult.
表面	biǎo miàn	surface; outside; outward appearance — 表 biǎo (outer; to show) + 面 miàn (face). The outer surface of a thing, and by extension the surface meaning as opposed to the truth beneath: 表面上 biǎomiàn shàng "on the surface, ostensibly."
宾馆	bīng uǎn	hotel, guesthouse — 宾 bīn (guest) + 馆. A house for guests, that is, a hotel. The word keeps the old hospitality sense of 馆 right on its sleeve.
博物馆	bó wù guǎn	museum — 博 bó (broad, extensive) + 物 wù (things) + 馆. The hall of broad things, where relics and specimens are kept and shown. 故宫博物院 uses 院 instead, but the everyday word is 博物馆.
菜单	cài dān	menu; (computing) a menu — 菜 cài (dish) + 单 dān (list; slip). The list of dishes a restaurant offers, and by extension the drop-down 菜单 of a computer program. 看菜单 "to look at the menu."
菜鸟	cài niǎo	(slang) novice; newbie; noob — 菜 cài (here: weak, lousy) + 鸟 niǎo (bird). A fledgling, hence a beginner who is still bad at something, especially in gaming and the workplace. Self-deprecating or teasing: 我是个菜鸟 "I'm a total newbie."
菜系	cài xì	cuisine school; culinary lineage — Cuisine system , the term for a recognized regional cooking tradition. The 八大菜系 (eight great cuisine systems) are the canonical eight, though culinary scholars recognize anywhere from four to dozens of regional traditions.
餐厅	cān tīng	restaurant; dining hall
叉烧	chā shāo	Char Siu — Cantonese BBQ Pork
茶馆	chá guǎn	Teahouse — Teahouse: a social institution especially important in Sichuan and Guangdong, where one goes not just to drink tea but to play mahjong, listen to storytelling, or simply spend a morning reading the paper.
茶香	chá xiāng	aroma of tea
茶叶蛋	chá yè dàn	tea egg
差不多	chà bu duō	almost; about the same
场	chǎng	open ground, venue
炒	chǎo	to stir-fry; the wok toss
炒蛋	chǎo dàn	scrambled eggs — 炒 (chǎo, to stir-fry) + 蛋: eggs broken into a hot wok and pushed around until they set in soft curds. The same 炒 powers most Chinese cooking, so 炒蛋 reads as plainly as English "scrambled eggs."
撑	chēng	stuffed, overfull
成都	Chéng dū	Chengdu city
吃饱	chī bǎo	to eat one's fill — 吃 (chī, eat) + 饱 (full) as a resultative pair: to eat until satisfied. The negative is 没吃饱 (méi chī bǎo, did not eat enough), and a question often takes 吃饱了吗 (chī bǎo le ma).
吃醋	chī cù	to be jealous (in love); to eat vinegar — 吃 chī + 醋 cù (vinegar). Eating vinegar: romantic jealousy. The origin story: a Tang dynasty concubine had to drink vinegar as punishment when she defied orders, giving rise to this vivid metaphor. Exclusively romantic jealousy (not professional envy, which is 嫉妒 jídù).
吃饭	chī fàn	To eat a meal , literally "eat cooked grain — To eat a meal , literally "eat cooked grain." Used as a general term for eating any meal regardless of whether rice is served. 吃 means "to eat"; 饭 means cooked grain or, by extension, any meal.
吃苦	chī kǔ	to endure hardship; to eat bitterness — 吃 chī (eat) + 苦 kǔ (bitter; hardship). To eat bitterness , to endure difficulty without complaint. One of the most culturally loaded compounds in Chinese. 能吃苦 (able to endure hardship) is a major virtue. 吃苦耐劳 = endure hardship and labor without complaint.
吃亏	chī kuī	to suffer a loss; to be at a disadvantage; to get the short end — 吃 chī + 亏 kuī (deficit; shortfall). To eat a shortfall , to come out worse, to be cheated or disadvantaged. 吃亏是福 chī kuī shì fú (suffering loss is a blessing) is a common proverb: the Daoist wisdom that accepting disadvantage builds character.
吃了吗	chī le ma	have you eaten
吃香	chī xiāng	to be popular, sought-after
充足	chōng zú	ample; abundant; fully sufficient — 充 (chōng, to fill; to be full) + 足 (sufficient). Filled to sufficiency , not merely enough but abundantly enough. 阳光充足 (ample sunlight); 准备充足 (fully prepared); 充足的理由 (compelling reasons). Stronger than 足够 zúgòu (merely enough).
重庆小面	Chóng qìng xiǎo miàn	Chongqing Spicy Noodles
臭	chòu	smelly, stinking
臭豆腐	chòu dòu fu	Stinky Tofu; Fermented Tofu
厨房	chú fáng	kitchen — 厨 (chú, cooking, the cook) plus 房. The room where food is prepared. One of the most frequent 房 compounds in daily life, and usually among the first room words a learner needs.
楚辞	Chǔ Cí	Songs of Chu
川菜	chuān cài	Sichuan cuisine — 川 chuān (Sichuan) + 菜 cài (cuisine). The cooking of Sichuan, famous for the numbing-spicy 麻辣 málà of Sichuan peppercorn and chili. 麻婆豆腐 and 回锅肉 are classic 川菜 dishes.
吹牛	chuī niú	to brag, to talk big — 吹 (chuī, to blow) + 牛 (ox). From the full phrase 吹牛皮 (chuī niúpí, blow up an ox-hide), once said of herders inflating a hide skin-raft by lung power: big puff, little substance.
葱	cōng	scallion; green onion; Chinese leek
葱烧海参	cōng shāo hǎi shēn	Scallion-Braised Sea Cucumber
大使馆	dà shǐ guǎn	embassy — 大使 dàshǐ (ambassador, lit. great envoy) + 馆. The ambassador's official house abroad. A smaller mission led by a consul is a 领事馆 lǐngshìguǎn, a consulate.
担担面	dān dān miàn	Dan Dan Noodles
蛋白	dàn bái	egg white; protein — 蛋 (egg) + 白 (bái, white): literally the white of the egg. In science it extends to protein in general, and 蛋白质 (dànbáizhì) is the standard word for dietary protein.
蛋糕	dàng āo	cake — 蛋 (egg) + 糕 (gāo, a steamed or baked flour cake): a cake gets its name from the eggs whipped into the batter. The word covers birthday cakes, sponge cakes, and the slice you buy at a café.
蛋黄	dàn huáng	egg yolk — 蛋 (egg) + 黄 (huáng, yellow): the yolk. It pairs neatly with 蛋白, the two halves of every egg named by their color. Salted yolk, 咸蛋黄 (xián dànhuáng), is a prized flavor in mooncakes and pastries.
蛋壳	dàn ké	eggshell
蛋挞	dàn tà	egg tart
刀工	dāo gōng	knife technique; knife skills
刀削面	dāo xiāo miàn	Knife-Shaved Noodles
捣蛋	dǎo dàn	to make trouble, to be naughty
德州扒鸡	Dé zhōu pá jī	Dezhou Braised Chicken
地点	dì diǎn	place; location; site; venue — 地 dì (ground) + 点 diǎn (point). A specific point on the ground, the precise spot where something happens: a meeting place, an event venue, the scene of an incident. More exact than 地方 dìfang.

图书馆	tú shū guǎn	library — 图 tú (drawing; map; illustration) + 书 shū (book) + 馆 guǎn (hall; building for a function). A building for maps and books. The word 图书 túshū , "illustrated books" , is now the formal term for library holdings in general.
外面	wài miàn	outside; the outside; outdoors — 外 wài (outside) + 面 miàn (side; surface). The everyday word for the area beyond an enclosed space, or the outer surface of a thing. Its pair is 里面 lǐmiàn (inside).
味道	wèi dào	taste; flavor
温饱	wēn bǎo	warmth and food; subsistence — 温 (wēn, warm) + 饱 (fed): warm enough and fed enough, the baseline of a decent life. 温饱 names the threshold of basic livelihood in economic and political language, as in 解决温饱问题 (jiějué wēnbǎo wèntí, to solve the subsistence problem).
蜗牛	wō niú	snail — 蜗 (wō, the snail's shell, with the 虫 insect radical) + 牛. The little horns of a snail earned it the ox; the word doubles as a byword for anything slow.
五味	wǔ wèi	the five flavors — sour, bitter, sweet, pungent, salty — 五 wǔ (five) + 味 wèi (flavor, taste). Sour 酸 suān (Wood/Liver), bitter 苦 kǔ (Fire/Heart), sweet 甘 gān (Earth/Spleen), pungent/spicy 辛 xīn (Metal/Lung), salty 咸 xián (Water/Kidney). Each flavor enters and supports its corresponding organ system in moderate amounts; in excess, it taxes it.

馅儿	xiàn r	Filling — Filling: the stuffing inside the wrapper. The -r suffix is a Beijing-dialect feature that appears frequently in food vocabulary.
香蕉	xiāng jiāo	banana
香气	xiāng qì	aroma, sweet smell
香水	xiāng shuǐ	perfume, cologne — 香 (fragrant) + 水 (water). Literally fragrant water, the modern word for bottled perfume. Measured with 瓶 (píng, bottle) or 滴 (dī, drop).
香甜	xiāng tián	sweet-smelling and tasty; sound (of sleep) — 香 (fragrant) + 甜 (sweet). Of food, fragrant and sweet at once. By extension, of sleep, deep and contented, the way you sleep after a good meal.
香味	xiāng wèi	aroma, fragrance, pleasant smell — 香 (fragrant) + 味 (smell, flavor). The scent something gives off, especially of food or flowers. The everyday word for a good aroma.
香烟	xiāng yān	cigarette — 香 (fragrant) + 烟 (smoke). The everyday word for a cigarette, named for the scented smoke it gives off. Counted with 支 (zhī, stick) or 包 (bāo, pack).
香皂	xiāng zào	scented soap

小鸡	xiǎo jī	chick, baby chicken — 小 (xiǎo, small) + 鸡 (chicken). The fluffy yellow chick just out of the shell, a favorite in children's songs and picture books.
辛	xīn	pungent; laborious
辛苦	xīn kǔ	hard, toilsome; thanks for your trouble — 辛 (xīn), pungent or laborious, pairs with 苦 (kǔ), bitter, to name effort that wears you down. As a set phrase it doubles as warm thanks: telling someone 辛苦了 acknowledges the toil they just put in for you.

鸭蛋	yā dàn	duck egg; a zero (slang) — 鸭 (yā, duck) + 蛋: a duck egg, larger and richer than a hen's. Among students it means a score of zero, since the round 0 on a test paper looks like an egg sitting there.
羊	yáng	sheep, goat
羊肉	yáng ròu	mutton, lamb — 羊 (yáng, sheep/goat) + 肉 (meat). The meat of choice in the northwest, central to hotpot and skewers.
摇头	yáo tóu	to shake the head
一次性筷子	yī cì xìng kuài zi	Disposable chopsticks — Disposable chopsticks: the individually wrapped bamboo or wood pairs given at most restaurants. Billions are used annually; their environmental cost is a topic of ongoing public debate.
一点儿	yì diǎn r	a little; a bit (neutral, often comparative) — After an adjective, 一点儿 makes a neutral comparison, "a little more X": 大一点儿 (a bit bigger), 快一点儿 (a bit faster), 便宜一点儿 (a little cheaper). After a verb, it means "a little / some": 喝一点儿水 (drink a little water), 说一点儿 (say a bit).
一面	yī miàn	one side; one surface; (measure) one flat object — 一 yī (one) + 面 miàn (measure word). 面 counts flat, surface-like objects: 一面镜子 (a mirror), 一面墙 (a wall), 一面旗 (a flag). As a noun 一面 also means "one side" of an argument or "one aspect."
以茶代酒	yǐ chá dài jiǔ	Substitute tea for spirits — Substitute tea for spirits: the accepted formula for toasting without drinking alcohol. The phrase is spoken while raising a cup of tea, acknowledging the substitution explicitly.
饮	yǐn	to drink (literary)
饮茶	yǐn chá	yum cha; going for dim sum; drinking tea
饮料	yǐn liào	beverage
饮食	yǐn shí	diet; food and drink
优点	yōu diǎn	strong point; merit; advantage — 优 yōu (excellent) + 点 diǎn (point). A good point, a strength. Its exact opposite is 缺点 quēdiǎn, a shortcoming. Together they are the standard pair for weighing a person, plan, or product.
有点儿	yǒu diǎn r	a bit; somewhat (often "a bit too…") — Placed before an adjective (or a feeling-verb) to say something is "a bit / somewhat" that way, usually with a tinge of complaint or an unwanted state. 有点儿 + 累 / 冷 / 贵 / 难 = a bit tired / cold / expensive / hard. It describes a current condition the speaker finds slightly undesirable.
圆桌	yuán zhuō	round table — Round table , the standard configuration for Chinese group dining. 圆 means "round" or "complete"; 桌 means "table." The round form eliminates a linear head seat and places all diners in a radial relationship to the shared dishes at the center.
院	yuàn	courtyard, institution
粤菜	yu è cài	Cantonese cuisine — 粤 yuè (Guangdong; Canton) + 菜 cài (cuisine). The cooking of Guangdong, prized for lightness, freshness, and steaming that keeps each ingredient tasting of itself. Dim sum (点心) belongs to this tradition.
炸鸡	zhá jī	fried chicken — 炸 (zhá, to deep-fry) + 鸡 (chicken). The crisp street-food and fast-food staple, often eaten with 啤酒 (píjiǔ, beer) in the Korean-style 炸鸡啤酒 pairing.
展览馆	zhǎn lǎng uǎn	exhibition hall — 展览 zhǎnlǎn (to exhibit) + 馆. The hall where exhibitions are mounted, cousin to the 博物馆 but temporary in what it shows.
蘸	zhàn	To dip, as in dipping a dumpling into sauce (蘸酱 zhàn jiàng) — To dip, as in dipping a dumpling into sauce (蘸酱 zhàn jiàng). The correct word for the motion; 沾 (zhān) is a common error for this usage.
蘸料	zhàn liào	dipping sauce; the condiment mix
长江	Cháng Jiāng	the Yangtze River — lit. "the long river" — 长 cháng (long) + 江 jiāng (large river; river south of Huai River). The Yangtze, Asia's longest river at 6,300 km, is simply "the Long River." Compare: 黄河 Huáng Hé (Yellow River) is named for its color; 长江 is named for its length.

重点	zhòng diǎn	key point; focus; emphasis; priority — 重 zhòng (heavy; important) + 点 diǎn (point). The heavy point, the part that matters most. 重点大学 "key universities," 划重点 "to mark the key points," the things to underline.
猪肉	zhū ròu	pork — 猪 (zhū, pig) + 肉 (meat). The most-eaten meat in China, so common that 肉 alone often means pork by default in recipes.
主食	zhǔ shí	Staple food — Staple food: the rice, noodles, or dumplings served at the end of a Chinese meal. The late arrival of the staple signals that the banquet was generous enough that no one needed to fill up on carbohydrates.
煮蛋	zhǔ dàn	boiled egg
粽子	zòng zi	rice dumpling wrapped in leaves — Sticky rice dumplings wrapped in bamboo or reed leaves, traditionally triangular or cylindrical. Made with various fillings: red bean paste, salted egg yolk, cured pork. The characteristic food of the Dragon Boat Festival, eaten to commemorate Qu Yuan.
做菜	zuò cài	to cook; to prepare dishes — 做 zuò (to make) + 菜 cài (dish). To cook, in the sense of making the dishes for a meal. 他很会做菜 "he is a good cook." Its near-neighbor 做饭 zuò fàn frames the same act around the staple rice.
