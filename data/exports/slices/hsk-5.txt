佛	fó	Buddha, the awakened — The character that carried an entire worldview across the Silk Road: etymology, Buddhism, the 佛-family of compounds.
雷	léi	thunder, the Arousing — Rain over fields struck by lightning: the ancient pictograph of thunder that became a deity, a Yijing trigram, and a metaphor for decisive force.
落	luò	to fall, to settle, to land — Falling, settling, and the beauty of things arriving at their place.
仁	rén	humaneness, the kernel of human relation — The character that anchors Confucian ethics: 亻 + 二, the self in relation to another. Etymology, the virtue-cluster compounds, and the seed-kernel sense that links moral and botanical 仁.
之	zhī	of, going, this: the classical connective — The most-written character in classical Chinese: a possessive particle, pronoun, and verb fossilized into pure grammar.
阿弥陀佛	Ē mí tuó fó	Amitābha Buddha — 阿弥陀佛 is the Buddha of Infinite Light and Life who presides over the Western Pure Land, and his name is itself the central practice: to recite 南无阿弥陀佛 is the simple path of Pure Land devotion, open to everyone.
仁	rén	humaneness, benevolence — The supreme Confucian virtue: the quality of genuine care for other people that holds civilization together.
丝绸之路	sī chóu zhī lù	the Silk Road — The term Silk Road was coined by a German geographer in 1877: the Chinese themselves had no single name for the trade network that defined their contact with the world for a millennium.
爱美之心，人皆有之	ài měi zhī xīn , rén jiē yǒu zhī	the love of beauty is universal — everyone has a heart for beauty — Classical proverb affirming the universality of aesthetic appreciation. Often used to gently excuse vanity or the appreciation of beautiful people and things.
饱学之士	bǎo xu é zhī shì	a scholar full of learning: an erudite person — 饱 extends from a full stomach to a full mind. 饱学 means saturated with learning; a respectful term for a deeply read scholar. Formal register.
必由之路	bì yóu zhī lù	the road one must take; the only viable path forward — 必 (must) + 由 yóu (to pass through; to travel by way of) + 之路 (the road). The path through which one must pass, with no alternative route. Used when describing the single viable way forward in a difficult situation: 改革是必由之路 (Reform is the only path forward).
不解之缘	bù jiě zhī yuán	a bond that cannot be untied: an indissoluble tie — Lit: a connection 缘 that cannot 不 be untied 解. An unbreakable bond or lifelong affinity, often with a person, a place, or a pursuit. 他和音乐结下了不解之缘 = he formed a lifelong bond with music. Here 解 is "to untie," and the negation makes the tie permanent.
不了了之	bù liǎo liǎo zhī	to let a matter fade away unresolved; to end without proper conclusion — Lit: not-conclude-conclude-it. Uses 了 liǎo as a verb twice , "to not conclude [a matter], to conclude [it anyway]." A set phrase for when a problem or investigation is allowed to dissolve without resolution , bureaucratic negligence, diplomatic evasion, quiet abandonment. 这件事就这样不了了之了。
操之过急	cāo zhī guò jí	to act on it too hastily: to rush a matter — A gentle warning that someone moved too fast and forced a result before its time. Often used to advise patience: 这件事不能操之过急 (don't rush this).
恻隐之心，人皆有之。	cè yǐn zhī xīn , rén jiē yǒu zhī .	The heart of compassion — every person has it. — Mencius 6A:6. The full passage lists all four hearts (恻隐、羞恶、恭敬、是非之心) and asserts that all four are universal: "every person has them." This is the foundation of the 性善论 argument: if the moral emotions are universal, they are innate, not learned.
尘埃落定	chén āi luò dìng	the dust has settled; a situation has finally reached resolution — Lit: dust-settle-fixed. Evokes the moment after turmoil when everything finally comes to rest and the outcome is clear. Title of Alai's celebrated Tibetan novel.
沉鱼落雁	chén yú luò yàn	"sinking fish and falling geese" — describing a woman so beautiful that fish dive and birds fall when she passes — From the Zhuangzi, originally a wry critique of relativism in beauty (the fish do not actually find her beautiful , they hide because they are afraid of humans).
大起大落	dà qǐ dà luò	dramatic rises and falls — extreme volatility and turbulence — Lit: great-rise great-fall. For lives, markets, or dynasties marked by dramatic swings: the roller-coaster image. Common in financial commentary and biographical writing.
多事之秋	duō shì zhī qiū	an eventful autumn: a troubled time, full of upheavals — Lit: many-matters' autumn. Autumn was the season of harvest reckoning and of war, so a "many-matters autumn" came to mean a period crowded with crises and disturbances, a turbulent, eventful time for a state or a person.
方寸之地	fāng cùn zhī dì	a space of mere inches; a tiny place — also: one's heart and mind — Lit: square-inch-of-ground. 方寸 = one square cun (about an inch). Used literally for extremely small physical spaces, and metaphorically for the heart/mind (one's inner space).
放下屠刀，立地成佛	fàng xià tú dāo , lì dì chéng fó	lay down the butcher's knife and become a Buddha on the spot — radical redemption is always possible — The full form of 立地成佛. Rooted in the Chan teaching that Buddha nature is present in all beings, even the most hardened. Used both seriously (in discussions of rehabilitation, forgiveness, religious conversion) and ironically (with skepticism about a villain's sudden change of heart). 只要放下屠刀，立地成佛.
瓜熟蒂落	guā shú dì luò	when the melon is ripe, the stem falls — things happen at the right natural moment — Lit: melon-ripe-stem-fall. Things should not be forced; they will happen naturally when conditions are right. The Daoist principle of naturalness encoded in a harvest image.
害群之马	hài qún zhī mǎ	a horse that harms the herd: one bad apple — From the Zhuāngzǐ, where the Yellow Emperor is told to govern by removing whatever harms the herd. Now the person who spoils a group or organization.
好自为之	hǎo zì wéi zhī	take good care of yourself; do well for yourself — A formal farewell or parting injunction: "do well in your own conduct." Can be warm (a mentor to a student) or pointed (a warning to behave). Context determines whether it is affectionate or admonishing.
后顾之忧	hòu gù zhī yōu	"the worry of looking back" — concern for what you leave behind; anxiety about what is happening at the rear — 后 (behind; the rear) + 顾 (to look back; to attend to) + 之 (classical possessive particle) + 忧 (worry; anxiety).
花甲之年	huā jiǎ zhī nián	the year of the flowery cycle — age sixty
花开见佛	huā kāi jiàn fó	when the flower blooms, the Buddha is seen — sudden illumination after patient waiting — Originally a Pure Land image (the lotus flower opening in the Pure Land to reveal the practitioner reborn there, seeing Amitābha), adopted by Chan as a metaphor for sudden enlightenment: the moment when all the conditions are right and understanding blooms.
患难之交	huàn nàn zhī jiāo	a friend made in times of hardship — a friend in adversity — A friendship forged through shared difficulty, prized in Chinese culture as the truest kind. 患难 huànnàn means hardship and danger; 交 jiāo here is the noun "friendship, bond." The related proverb is 患难见真情 ("adversity reveals true feeling"): you learn who your real friends are when times are hard.
借花献佛	jiè huā xiàn fó	to offer flowers borrowed from another — presenting something that isn't yours — Used with self-deprecating humor when giving a gift that wasn't originally yours , passing along a recommendation, re-gifting, or presenting another person's work as an offering. The image: lacking flowers of your own to offer at the Buddhist altar, you borrow someone else's.
靖康之耻	Jǐng kāng zhī Chǐ	the Jingkang Humiliation — national shame that demands redemption — The capture of the Song imperial family by the Jurchen Jin dynasty in 1127 CE. Invoked by Yue Fei and every subsequent patriot as the wound that must be avenged. Still appears in modern Chinese political rhetoric when national pride or territorial integrity is at stake.
君子之交	jūn zǐ zhī jiāo	the friendship of noble people: plain as water, lasting and unselfish — Lit: noble-person's friendship. From the saying 君子之交淡如水, "the friendship of the noble is bland as water," meaning a true bond needs no flattery or gifts and so never sours.
雷电交加	léi diàn jiāo jiā	thunder and lightning together — a violent storm; used of chaotic, intense situations — Lit: thunder-lightning-together-intensify. Describes a fierce storm where both thunder (雷 léi) and lightning (电 diàn) are striking simultaneously. Used metaphorically for any situation of simultaneous, intense upheaval: a chaotic meeting, an emotional confrontation, a turbulent period.
雷厉风行	léi lì fēng xíng	as fierce as thunder, as swift as wind — to act with decisive, energetic speed — Lit: thunder-fierce-wind-moving. 厉 means severe, sharp, intense; 行 means to move, to act. The phrase describes a person or policy that executes with the speed and force of a storm: no hesitation, no dragging of feet. Used to praise decisive leadership or swift implementation.
雷声大，雨点小	léi shēng dà , yǔ diǎn xiǎo	thunder loud, raindrops small — all noise, no substance — A folk proverb elevated to proverbial status. The contrast is between the dramatic announcement and the meager follow-through: the thunder promised a deluge, but only a few drops fell.
立地成佛	lì dì chéng fó	to become a Buddha on the spot — Usually in the full proverb 放下屠刀，立地成佛: lay down the butcher's knife and become a Buddha where you stand. Chan's radical claim that awakening is instantaneous and open even to the worst, now the standard phrase for the possibility of immediate reform.
邻里之间	lín lǐ zhī jiān	"between neighbors and the neighborhood" — community bonds; relations among people living nearby — 邻 (lín, neighbor) + 里 (the neighborhood unit) + 之间 (between). Uses 里 in its oldest sense , the bounded residential community of the Zhou system.
临时抱佛脚	lín shí bào fó jiǎo	clasping the Buddha's feet at the last minute — cramming — From a proverb about neglecting incense in ordinary times and embracing the statue's feet only when disaster looms (平时不烧香，临时抱佛脚). The universal student condition, named without mercy. Usable for any deferred preparation: visas, taxes, wedding speeches.
落后就要挨打	luò hòu jiù yào ái dǎ	if you fall behind, you will be beaten — the fundamental lesson of the century of humiliation — The defining phrase of modern Chinese nationalist historiography. Attributed to various leaders; popularized by Deng Xiaoping. The phrase frames national development not as aspiration but as existential necessity: weakness invites predation, therefore modernization is survival.
落花流水	luò huā liú shuǐ	fallen blossoms and flowing water — spring's end; utter defeat — Lit: falling-flowers flowing-water. A classical image from Tang and Song poetry for the beauty of spring's end. Also used for utter defeat: 打得落花流水 "beaten to pieces."
落井下石	luò jǐng xià shí	to throw stones at someone who has fallen in a well — to kick someone when they're down — Lit: fall-well-down-stone. The act of throwing rocks on top of someone already trapped in a well. Vivid idiom for betrayal or cruelty toward someone already in misfortune.
落叶归根	luò yè guī gēn	fallen leaves return to the root — everyone returns to their origins — Lit: fallen-leaves-return-root. The universal Chinese sentiment about homeland: no matter how far one has traveled or how long one has been away, the natural ending is to return to one's place of origin.
麻木不仁	má mù bù rén	numb and inhumane; deadened to feeling — both physical and moral — 麻木 mámù (numb; without sensation , 麻 hemp, suggesting the prickling of nerves; 木 wood, suggesting deadness) + 不仁 bùrén (without humaneness; without feeling). Originally a medical term for physical numbness , limbs that have lost sensation.
靡靡之音	mǐ mǐ zhī yīn	enervating music: sounds thought to soften the will and sap the spirit — Lit: dispirited music. A classical term of disapproval for music seen as decadent and weakening, the kind moralists blamed for a ruler's downfall. Still used, half-seriously, of overly sentimental songs.
名落孙山	míng luò Sūn Shān	one's name fell behind Sun Shan's — to fail an examination — Sun Shan, a Song-dynasty wit, passed the provincial exam in last place. Asked by a neighbor whether the neighbor's son had passed, he answered in verse: "The last name on the list was Sun Shan; your worthy son came after Sun Shan." The gentlest failure notice in the language, still the standard lite…
匹夫之勇	pǐ fū zhī yǒng	the courage of a common man: reckless bravery without strategy — 匹夫 pǐfū is an ordinary fellow of no rank. The phrase names the brute, headlong courage of one who rushes in to fight without thought or plan, contrasted with the cool, calculated valor of a true leader. It is a gentle put-down: brave, but foolishly so.
七零八落	qī líng bā luò	"seven scattered, eight fallen" — scattered and broken up; in pieces — 七零 (sevens scattered) and 八落 (eights fallen): things strewn about, broken apart, no longer whole. Used of an army routed and dispersed, of belongings scattered, or of a once-unified group now fragmented.
千里之行，始于足下	qiān lǐ zhī xíng , shǐ yú zú xià	"a thousand-li journey begins beneath the feet" — every great endeavor starts with a single step — From the Daodejing, chapter 64. 千里 (a thousand li) + 之 (connecting particle) + 行 (journey, travel) + 始 (to begin) + 于 (at, from) + 足下 (beneath the feet).
千里之行，始于足下。	qiān lǐ zhī xíng , shǐ yú zú xià .	A journey of a thousand li begins beneath one's feet. — From chapter 64, now a chengyu used worldwide. The surrounding passage teaches that great things grow from small beginnings and that the wise act early, while problems are still tiny and easy to handle: the tree that fills an armspan grew from a sprout, the nine-storey tower rose from a heap of eart…
前车之鉴	qián chē zhī jiàn	"the lesson of the overturned cart ahead" — learn from the mistakes of those who came before — 前 (in front) + 车 (cart) + 之 (possessive) + 鉴 (mirror; lesson; to take as a warning). A cart that has already overturned lies visible on the road ahead of you , it is the past made legible, a warning you can see precisely because the past stands in front.
取而代之	qǔ ér dài zhī	to take over and replace it: to supplant another and assume their place — Lit: take it, and replace it. From the Records of the Grand Historian, where the young Xiang Yu, seeing the First Emperor's procession, vows that he could take the emperor's place. Now used for any decisive supplanting of one thing by another.
仁义道德	rén yì dào dé	humaneness, righteousness, the Way, and virtue — the full Confucian moral vocabulary — The four great concepts of Confucian ethics strung together: 仁 rén (humaneness), 义 yì (righteousness), 道 dào (the Way , the correct path of conduct), 德 dé (virtue , moral power accumulated through right conduct). Together they describe the ideal moral life.
仁者爱人	rén zhě ài rén	the benevolent person loves others — the most concise definition of 仁 — From Mencius (4B:28). The simplest, most direct statement of the first virtue. 仁 is not an abstract principle but an orientation toward actual people. Love here (爱 ài) means caring about the genuine wellbeing of others, not sentimental attachment.
仁者乐山，智者乐水	rén zhě yào shān , zhì zhě yào shuǐ	"the benevolent love mountains, the wise love water" — The companion form of the Analects couplet, often quoted with the order reversed. 仁 (benevolence) and 智 (wisdom) are matched to mountain (immobile, supporting) and water (mobile, responsive) respectively.
仁者无敌	rén zhě wú dí	the humane have no enemies — From Mencius (孟子). The political claim that follows from 仁 as a virtue of rule: a ruler who genuinely cares for the people will have no one willing to make war against them, because no one wants to fight a benefactor.
仁至义尽	rén zhì yì jìn	humaneness to the utmost, righteousness to its limit — to do everything one possibly can — Said of someone who has discharged every moral obligation, exhausted every avenue of help or forgiveness, usually before walking away. Often used right before delivering a hard refusal: "I have done everything humanly possible; the rest is not on me."
如雷贯耳	rú léi guàn ěr	"like thunder piercing the ear" — a name of thunderous fame — 如 (like) + 雷 (thunder) + 贯 (to pierce, to pass through) + 耳 (ear). A famous name entering the ear with the force of thunder.
三代之治	sān dài zhī zhì	the governance of the Three Dynasties — the Confucian golden age — The Three Dynasties (Xia, Shang, Zhou) collectively were idealized by Confucians as the golden age of moral government, a standard against which all later dynasties were measured and found wanting. 三代 remains a byword for ideal antiquity in Chinese intellectual discourse.
手足之情	shǒu zú zhī qíng	the affection of hands and feet: the bond between brothers and sisters — Lit: hand-foot's-feeling. 手足 (hands and feet) is a classical image for siblings, who are as inseparable as one's own limbs. The phrase names the deep tie between brothers and sisters.
送佛送到西	sòng fó sòng dào xī	if you escort the Buddha, escort him all the way west: finish what you start, see help through to the end — A colloquial saying drawing on the westward journey of Buddhist pilgrimage. Urges you not to abandon a kindness or a task halfway, but to carry it to completion.
他山之石	tā shān zhī shí	stones from other hills — outside perspectives can polish your own jade — From the Book of Songs (诗经): "stones from other hills can be used to grind jade." External viewpoints, foreign models, and outside criticism all carry value that self-regard misses. 他山 (other hills) + 之 (possessive particle) + 石 (stones).
天下无不散之筵席。	tiān xià wú bù sàn zhī yán xí .	No feast under heaven goes on forever; every banquet must end. — A proverb woven through the novel and epitomising its theme of impermanence: every gathering, however joyful, must break up, just as the splendour of the Jia family must pass. Used widely in Chinese to console at partings and to acknowledge that good times, like all things, come to an end.
天之骄子	tiān zhī jiāo zǐ	a favored child of Heaven: one singularly blessed by fortune — Lit: Heaven's-spoiled-child. Originally said of the Xiongnu nomads who boasted Heaven's favor; now used of anyone who seems destined for success, sometimes with a touch of envy.
天作之合	tiān zuò zhī hé	a union made by Heaven: a match made in heaven — Lit: Heaven-made union. A classical blessing for a marriage, suggesting the couple was destined to come together. Still used today on wedding scrolls and congratulations.
土木之变	Tǔ mù zhī Biàn	the Tumu Crisis — the catastrophic defeat in which a Chinese emperor was captured by nomads — The 1449 capture of Emperor Yingzong at Tumu Fortress (土木堡 Tǔmùbǎo) by Oirat Mongols under Esen Khan. A turning point that ended Ming offensive posture against the steppe and initiated the Great Wall rebuilding program. Used as a metonym for military disaster born of imperial overreach.
亡国之音	wáng guó zhī yīn	the music of a doomed state — decadent art that signals decline — From the Liji 礼记: licentious music signals a state in moral collapse. Used today to criticize art deemed nihilistic or culturally destructive.
无价之宝	wú jià zhī bǎo	a treasure beyond price: a priceless thing — 无价 (without price) marks what no sum could buy. Used for a rare heirloom, a one-of-a-kind artwork, or, warmly, for a person or a friendship.
弦外之音	xián wài zhī yīn	the note beyond the strings: an unspoken meaning, the overtone behind the words — Lit: outside-the-string's sound. The sense a speaker implies without saying it outright, like the overtones a plucked string sets ringing. To catch the 弦外之音 is to hear what someone really means.
星星之火，可以燎原	xīng xing zhī huǒ , kě yǐ liáo yuán	a single spark can set the prairie ablaze — 星星 here is the spark sense of 星. The image of fire racing across a plain is ancient (the Book of Documents already warns of fire on the grassland), but the phrase's modern fame comes from Mao Zedong's 1930 essay taking it as a title, arguing a small revolutionary force could ignite the country.
学而时习之	xu é ér shí xí zhī	to study and constantly practice what one has learned — the joy of learning — The opening phrase of the Analects (1.1). 时 shí means "at the right times" or "constantly/regularly"; Confucius is describing not cramming but sustained, rhythmic practice. 习 xí originally depicted a young bird practicing flight: iteration until mastery.
学而时习之，不亦说乎？	xu é ér shí xí zhī , bù yì yu è hū ?	Is it not a pleasure to learn and repeatedly practice what you have learned? — Opening line of the Analects (1.1). The word 说 here is read yuè, an archaic form of 悦 "pleased, delighted." The passage continues: "Is it not a joy to have friends come from afar? Is it not a mark of the noble person not to be resentful when others do not recognize him?" Three questions that establi…
一念之差	yí niàn zhī chā	the difference of a single thought — a momentary lapse that changes everything — 一念 (one thought-moment) + 之差 (the difference of). The idea that a whole fate can turn on a single passing impulse , one wrong thought acted on, one good one suppressed.
一言以蔽之	yī yán yǐ bì zhī	to cover it all in one word — in a word; to sum up — From the Analects II.2, where Confucius summarizes the 300 poems of the Shijing: 诗三百，一言以蔽之，曰思无邪。 — "The three hundred poems can be covered in one phrase: 'no depraved thoughts.'" 以 marks the instrument (one word) used to accomplish the action (cover/sum up).
一以贯之	yī yǐ guàn zhī	one thread running through all — a single principle governing everything — Source: Analects 4.15. Confucius tells Zengzi 吾道一以贯之 , "My way is threaded through by one." When Zengzi explains to other disciples, he names that one thing as 忠恕 zhōngshù (loyalty and reciprocity).
以小人之心，度君子之腹	yǐ xiǎo rén zhī xīn , duó jūn zǐ zhī fù	measuring a gentleman's belly with a petty man's heart — projecting one's own baseness — Adapted from a Zuozhuan phrase: assuming others' motives are as small as one's own. The polite deployment is self-accusation: 是我以小人之心度君子之腹了, "I misjudged you by my own petty standard," the graceful way to withdraw a suspicion that proved unfair.
右文之治	yòu wén zhī zhì	"governance that favors the literary" — an administration that prioritizes civil culture over military force — 右文 (to favor the literary arts: to place the civil over the martial) + 之 (possessive particle) + 治 (governance; rule).
贞观之治	Zhēng uān zhī Zhì	the Well-Governed Era of Zhenguan — the Confucian ideal of enlightened imperial rule — Taizong's reign era (627–649 CE), celebrated as the model of good governance in Chinese political tradition. The formula "[reign-era name] 之治" became the standard way to name historical periods of exceptional governance, as in 文景之治 and 开元盛世.
知之为知之，不知为不知。	zhī zhī wéi zhī zhī , bù zhī wéi bù zhī .	To know what you know and know what you don't know — that is real knowledge. — From the Analects of Confucius. The full saying ends 是知也 ("this is wisdom"). It teaches intellectual honesty: genuine knowledge includes a clear sense of the limits of one's knowledge.
制天命而用之	zhì tiān mìng ér yòng zhī	Master the decrees of Heaven and use them — human agency as the response to natural necessity — From 天论 (On Heaven). Contrasted with the alternative: 大天而思之, "exalting Heaven and longing for it." Xunzi argues that the latter is passive and futile; the former is the correct response to a world governed by regular, impersonal natural processes.
治世之能臣，乱世之奸雄	zhì shì zhī néng chén , luàn shì zhī jiān xióng	a capable minister in peace, a villainous hero in chaos — The famous double-edged appraisal of Cao Cao, conceding his talent while condemning his ambition. The phrase is quoted as a character type in itself: the gifted person whose abilities turn dangerous when the rules break down. 奸雄 jiānxióng, "villain-hero," is the role Cao Cao defines.
子非鱼，安知鱼之乐	zǐ fēi yú , ān zhī yú zhī lè	You are not a fish — how can you know the happiness of fish? — From the 秋水 chapter. Used in modern Chinese as a rhetorical challenge when someone claims to understand an experience or perspective that is not their own: "You haven't been through what I've been through; how can you know how I feel?" 之	zhī	classical genitive, nominalizer, and pronoun — The single character that does the work of three English words (of, it, and the one who) and still shows up in modern Chinese every day.
百分之	bǎi fēn zhī	percent; out of a hundred parts — 百 (hundred) + 分 fēn (parts, divide) + 之 zhī (of, possessive particle). Literally: "out of a hundred parts." Chinese writes percentages as 百分之X , 百分之五十 (50%), 百分之百 (100%).
避雷针	bì léi zhēn	lightning rod — lit. "thunder-avoiding needle" — 避 bì (to avoid; to deflect) + 雷 léi (thunder/lightning) + 针 zhēn (needle; pin). Lightning rod. Literally: the needle that deflects the thunder. Metaphorically, a 避雷针 is a person or thing that absorbs blame or controversy on behalf of someone else , a lightning rod in the political sense.
不仁	bù rén	inhumane; callous; morally numb — also: physical numbness (limbs without sensation) — 不 bù (not; without) + 仁 rén (humaneness). The negation that reveals the concept's full weight: to be without 仁 is not merely to be unkind , it is to be morally deadened, incapable of feeling for others.
部落	bù luò	tribe; clan
恻隐之心	cè yǐn zhī xīn	the heart of compassion / commiseration — sprout of 仁 humaneness — 恻隐 cèyǐn: the feeling of alarm and pity , specifically the involuntary distress one feels at another's suffering. The child-at-the-well example demonstrates this sprout. It is the first beginning of 仁 rén (humaneness/benevolence), the master virtue.
赤壁之战	Chì bì zhī Zhàn	the Battle of Red Cliffs
辞让之心	cí ràng zhī xīn	the heart of deference and yielding — sprout of 礼 ritual propriety — 辞让 cí ràng: declining (an honor) and yielding (precedence to another). The capacity to defer , to put another's needs or claim before one's own in appropriate situations. The sprout of 礼 lǐ (ritual propriety).
地雷	dì léi	landmine — lit. "earth thunder" — 地 dì (earth; ground) + 雷 léi (thunder). Landmine , an explosive buried in the ground that detonates on contact. The compound is viscerally accurate: a mine hidden in the earth that erupts like thunder when triggered.
低落	dī luò	low; down; dispirited; fallen in spirit — 低 (low) + 落 (to fall, to drop, to settle down). Mood or morale that has fallen and settled at a low point. 情绪低落 (qíngxù dīluò, spirits low) is the standard phrase for feeling deflated, discouraged, or quietly unhappy without necessarily being in crisis.
跌落	diē luò	to plummet; to tumble down sharply — 跌 diē (to stumble, to fall sharply) + 落 luò. A more violent or dramatic fall than 落 alone , prices plummeting, a person tumbling. Common in financial and news contexts.
段落	duàn luò	paragraph
仿佛	fǎng fú	as if; seemingly; as though — 仿 fǎng (to imitate; resembling) + 佛 fú (phonetic only; here read fú, not fó, with no Buddhist meaning). A high-frequency literary and spoken adverb meaning "as if; seemingly; as though." Used to describe perceptions, memories, or states that have a dreamlike or approximate quality.
佛法	fó fǎ	the Dharma; Buddhist teaching; the law of the Buddha — 佛 + 法 fǎ (law; method; dharma). The teachings of the Buddha as a whole, understood as both doctrine and practice. Also the specific rendering of Sanskrit dharma, which encompasses cosmic law, the teachings themselves, individual phenomena, and moral truth.
佛教	fó jiào	Buddhism (the religion) — 佛 (Buddha) + 教 jiào (teaching; religion; to teach). The standard modern term for Buddhism as a religion or institutional tradition. Entered China officially during the Han dynasty (around the 1st century CE), though oral transmission and merchant routes likely brought it earlier.
佛教节日	fó jiào jié rì	Buddhist festivals — integrated into the Chinese calendar — 浴佛节 Yùfó Jié (Buddha's Birthday, 4th lunar month, 8th day): ritual bathing of the infant Buddha statue with scented water, commemorating his birth.
佛经	fó jīng	Buddhist scripture; sutra — 佛 fó (Buddha) + 经 jīng (scripture). The sutras, the recorded discourses of the Buddha. Reciting and copying 佛经 is a long-standing devotional practice, as with the much-loved 心经 xīnjīng (Heart Sutra).
佛说阿弥陀经	Fó shuō Ā mí tuó jīng	the Smaller Sukhāvatīvyūha · ~1,800 characters · Kumārajīva's 402 CE translation — The short Amitabha Sutra. The daily-recitation text of Pure Land practice. The Buddha describes the Pure Land's adornments to Śāriputra, names the practice (single-minded recollection of Amitābha), and is supported by the buddhas of the six directions affirming the teaching.
佛系	fó xì	Buddhist-style; detached; low-key; unbothered (modern slang) — 佛 + 系 xì (style; type; lineage, as in 日系 Japanese-style). Emerged in Chinese internet culture around 2017 to describe a mentality of deliberate non-striving: going with the flow, not competing, indifferent to outcomes.
佛性	fó xìng	Buddha nature
果仁	guǒ rén	fruit kernel; nut meat (general term) — 果 guǒ (fruit) + 仁. The general category term: any kernel or "nutmeat." Wang Yangming and other Neo-Confucians explicitly used this image as the metaphor for moral 仁: a 果仁 is what gives the fruit its capacity to generate the next tree, just as 仁 is what gives a person the capacity to relate fully to…

活佛	huó fó	living Buddha; reincarnated lama — Living Buddha , the Chinese term for a recognized reincarnation (tulku in Tibetan). Not precisely "Buddha" in the doctrinal sense but a lama whose successive rebirths are identified and confirmed by the tradition. The Dalai Lama and Panchen Lama are the most senior examples.
降落	jiàng lùo	to land; to descend (aircraft); to come down — 降 jiàng (to descend, to lower) + 落 luò (to land). The standard word for an aircraft landing. Also used for anything descending to earth: 降落伞 jiànglùosǎn = parachute.
角落	jiǎo luò	corner; nook; out-of-the-way place — 角 jiǎo (corner) + 落 luò (to settle, to land). The place where things come to rest in the corner, a nook. Both literally (a room's corner) and figuratively (a quiet, tucked-away place in the world). The name of this site: 角落書屋.
靖难之役	Jīng nán zhī Yì	Jingnan Campaign (Yongle's usurpation)
九层之台	jiǔ céng zhī tái	a nine-story platform
九五之尊	jiǔ wǔ zhī zūn	the supreme position; the imperial throne — 九五 (the Nine-Five, the fifth yang line of the Qian hexagram, the "flying dragon in the heavens") + 之尊 (the honored, the exalted). The throne itself, the supreme position of the emperor. To 登上九五之尊 is to ascend the throne.
雷达	léi dá	radar — phonetic loanword — Phonetic transliteration of the English "radar" (Radio Detection And Ranging). 雷 léi (thunder) + 达 dá (to reach; to arrive). A felicitous loanword: the chosen characters evoke the technology's function , a signal (like thunder, striking out from a source) that reaches and returns.
雷公	Léi gōng	Duke of Thunder — the thunder deity — 雷 léi (thunder) + 公 gōng (duke; honorific for male deities and elder men). The personified god of thunder, depicted with a bird beak, wings, and ring of drums. Works alongside 电母 (Diàn Mǔ, Mother of Lightning).
雷声	léi shēng	the sound of thunder; a thunderclap — 雷 léi (thunder) + 声 shēng (sound). The rumble or crack of thunder. Often used in the phrase 雷声大，雨点小 (léishēng dà, yǔdiǎn xiǎo) , "thunder loud, raindrops small" , meaning all bluster and announcement with little actual follow-through.
雷雨	léi yǔ	thunderstorm — 雷 léi (thunder) + 雨 yǔ (rain). A thunderstorm , rain accompanied by thunder and lightning. Also the title of one of the most important plays in modern Chinese literature: 雷雨 (Léiyǔ, 1934) by Cao Yu (曹禺), a family tragedy of suppressed desire and social constraint.
零落	líng luò	to wither and fall; scattered; declined and broken up — 零 líng (to fall in drops) + 落 luò (to fall; to drop). Closest to the character's root: things falling and scattering. Of flowers and leaves, to wither and drop; of people, families, or fortunes, to scatter and decline (家道零落, the household fallen on hard times).
落成	luò chéng	to be completed (of a building); inauguration — 落 luò (to settle) + 成 chéng (to complete, to become). Used specifically for the completion of a building or major construction project. 落成典礼 = inauguration ceremony.
落第	luò dì	to fail the examination; to be rejected from the list — Literally "to fall from the notice board": 第 refers to the ranked list of successful candidates posted publicly after each examination. To fail was to have your name absent from that list.
落后	luò hòu	to fall behind; backward; underdeveloped — 落 luò + 后 hòu (behind). To lag behind others in a race, in development, or in progress. Used in sports, economics, and social commentary. Carries a normative weight , something that has fallen behind should catch up.
落脚	luò jiǎo	to settle in (a place); to find lodging; to come to rest — 落 luò (to fall; to land) + 脚. To set the foot down after travel , to find a place to stay, to settle in temporarily or permanently. 在北京落脚 "to settle in Beijing" (especially as a newcomer); 落脚点 luòjiǎodiǎn "a place to settle, a foothold."
落实	luò shí	to implement; to pin down; to make concrete — 落 luò (to land) + 实 shí (solid, real, concrete). To take something from the abstract and make it real , to land on a decision, confirm a plan, carry out a policy. One of the most important governance and management words in Chinese.
落下	luò xià / là xia	to fall; to drop behind; to be left behind — Two distinct pronunciations with different meanings. luò xià = to fall downward (rain falling, price falling). là·xia (neutral tone) = to fall behind, to leave something behind by accident: 把钥匙落下了 "left the keys behind."
南无阿弥陀佛	Ná mó Ē mí tuó fó	I take refuge in Amitābha Buddha
念佛	niàn fó	Buddha-recitation — the primary and most accessible practice — 念 niàn (to recite; to think of; to hold in mind) + 佛 fó (Buddha). The recitation of Amitābha's name, 南无阿弥陀佛 Nāmó Āmítuófó ("homage to Amitābha Buddha"), as a continuous practice.
千里之行	qiān lǐ zhī xíng	a journey of a thousand li
人间佛教	rén jiān fó jiào	humanistic Buddhism; Buddhism for the human world — The 20th-century reformist movement, associated above all with Master Taixu (太虚, 1890–1947), that repositioned Buddhist practice away from funeral rites and other-worldly concerns toward engagement with society, education, and the relief of present suffering.
仁爱	rén ài	benevolence and love; humanitarian care; genuine concern for others — 仁 rén (humaneness) + 爱 ài (love; affection). The compound that captures 仁's essential motion: active, outward-reaching care for others. 仁 without 爱 is a concept; 仁爱 is what it looks like in practice.
仁慈	rén cí	compassionate; merciful; kind-hearted — 仁 rén + 慈 cí (tender care; the warmth of a mother for a child). The quality of someone whose inner humaneness produces visibly tender conduct. Warmer and more personal than 仁爱: 仁爱 can describe a principle, 仁慈 always describes a quality felt by the recipient.
仁义	rén yì	humaneness and righteousness; moral conduct; the Confucian ethical pairing — 仁 rén (humaneness) + 义 yì (righteousness; moral rightness). The foundational pairing in Confucian ethics: 仁 is the inward quality of genuine care; 义 is the outward expression of doing what is morally right. Together they describe the full ethical person.
仁义之师	rén yì zhī shī	an army of benevolence and righteousness; a just and humane military force — 仁义 (benevolence and righteousness) + 之 (attributive particle) + 师 (army; force; teacher).
仁者	rén zhě	the humane person; one who possesses 仁 (classical) — 仁 rén + 者 zhě (the one who…; nominalizer in classical Chinese). The classical term for a person who has cultivated 仁.

山水之间	shān shuǐ zhī jiān	amid mountains and waters; in the landscape — A literary phrase widely used in modern essays, song lyrics, and travel writing. 之间 zhī jiān = between; amid. The phrase carries the Daoist resonance of being in the landscape rather than looking at it. Common in tea-house names, hotel names, and book titles aimed at evoking refinement.

The sprout of 智 zhì (moral wisdom , knowing how to act rightly in complex situations).
