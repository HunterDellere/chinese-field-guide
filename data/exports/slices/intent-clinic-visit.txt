//角落書屋/Slices/Clinic Visit
饱	bǎo	full, satisfied, to eat one's fill — The food radical 饣 wraps the sound of 包 (bāo), so the glyph itself is a stomach packed full like a dumpling.
病	bìng	illness, disease, to fall ill, fault — A person sweating on a sickbed with the phonetic 丙 inside: the radical 疒 frames every Chinese word for being unwell.
穿	chuān	to pierce, to bore through, to wear, to put on — A tooth gnawing through the roof of a cave: one image for boring a hole and for the body slipping into clothing.
耳	ěr	ear; to hear; handle — The pictograph of an ear that anchors Chinese words for listening, fame, and the furred handles on bronze vessels.
发	fā	to send out, to develop, hair — One simplified glyph that swallowed two traditional characters. 发 carries the fa reading of sending, emitting, and developing (traditional 發) and the fa reading of hair on the head (traditional 髮).
脚	jiǎo	foot, base — The everyday word for foot, and by extension the base of any standing thing: a mountain, a wall, a script. The flesh-and-bone counterpart to literary 足.
口	kǒu	mouth, opening, entrance — A simple rectangle for an open mouth: one of the most productive radicals in the script, present in hundreds of characters for speaking, eating, and openings.
脸	liǎn	face, cheeks, social face, reputation — The flesh-radical face that doubles as a ledger of dignity: in Chinese you can lose it, save it, or tear it apart.
目	mù	eye; to look at; section heading — The pictograph of an eye that became the Chinese radical for everything seen, watched, and classified.
女	nǚ	woman, female — A kneeling figure with crossed arms: one of the most productive radicals in the script, present in characters for family relationships, feelings, and cultural concepts.
跑	pǎo	to run; to flee; to operate — The everyday verb of running: a foot-radical character whose semantic range stretches from "the legs in motion" to "running an errand" to "the engine is running" to a small but productive family of escape-and-flee compounds.
气	qì	breath, vital energy — From weather to anger to the cosmic force underlying TCM and martial arts.
肉	ròu	meat, flesh, the pulp of fruit — A pictograph of a meat slab with its marbling drawn inside; squeezed onto the left of a character it becomes 月, the flesh-moon of the body radicals.
手	shǒu	hand — A five-fingered hand drawn from life: one of the most productive radicals in the script, present in hundreds of action characters.
首	shǒu	head, first, chief, a measure word for songs and poems — A drawn head, with hair above an eye, and from it the senses of first, foremost, chief, leader, and the classifier you count songs and poems with. 首 is its own radical.
体	tǐ	body, substance, to experience — The person beside the root: the body as a person's base. From the physical body grow substance and system, the whole as against the part, and the act of grasping a thing by living through it.
头	tóu	head, top, end, chief, noun-suffix — The head, and from it the top of a hill, the end of a street, the start of a thing, the chief of a group, and the toneless tag on a hundred everyday nouns. The simplified 头 stands for traditional 頭.
眼	yǎn	eye, glance — The everyday word for eye, and by extension any small opening, any moment of looking, any judgment made through sight. The body-part counterpart to literary 目.
站	zhàn	to stand, to halt, a station, a stop — A person planted upright on the ground, holding their place: from the body that will not move grows the station, the stop, and the website you log on to.
足	zú	foot; sufficient; enough — The foot that became the word for sufficiency, and the radical anchoring every character involving legs, running, and roads.
节气	jié qì	the 24 solar terms — China's ancient agricultural calendar: 24 precise moments that mark the turning of the year, still shaping food, medicine, and daily life.
气功	qì gōng	qigong — Cultivating the vital breath: China's ancient practice of aligning body, breath, and mind.
身体	shēn tǐ	body; health — The word for body doubles as the word for health, asking about it is a greeting, and its second character got luckier in simplification than any other.
武术	wǔ shù	martial arts — The art of stopping weapons: how China systematized combat into a living philosophy of the body.
医院	yī yuàn	hospital — The healing courtyard: how an arrow wound, a wine jar, and the institutional suffix 院 assembled the word for hospital.
中医	zhōng yī	Traditional Chinese Medicine — Two thousand years of systematic medical thought: qi, yin-yang, the Five Elements, acupuncture, and the living tradition that treats the person, not the disease.
爱不释手	ài bù shì shǒu	love it so much you cannot put it down — Lit: love-not-release-hand. The standard phrase for something you cannot stop using, reading, or holding: so beloved that your hand refuses to let go. Used for books, gadgets, food, art. 这本小说写得太好了，让人爱不释手 "This novel is so well-written; you can't put it down."
白发苍苍	bái fà cāng cāng	hair gone hoary white: aged, in advanced old age — Lit: white-hair-grey-grey. A vivid picture of a head of grizzled white hair, used affectionately or respectfully of the elderly. Again the fà reading, since the phrase is literally about hair.
饱经风霜	bǎo jīng fēng shuāng	to have weathered wind and frost: hardened by hardship — 饱 here means having had a full share of something. The image is a face lined by years of exposure; used of people who have lived through long adversity. Common in literary and journalistic prose.
饱食终日	bǎo shí zhōng rì	to eat one's fill all day: to idle in comfort — From the Analects (论语), where Confucius reproaches those who eat their fill the whole day and apply their minds to nothing. A pointed criticism of idleness; carries a moral edge.
饱学之士	bǎo xu é zhī shì	a scholar full of learning: an erudite person — 饱 extends from a full stomach to a full mind. 饱学 means saturated with learning; a respectful term for a deeply read scholar. Formal register.
饱以老拳	bǎo yǐ lǎo quán	to treat to a full helping of fists: to give a sound beating — A grim joke on the eating sense: instead of a meal, the recipient gets a generous serving of punches. From the Jìn Shū (晋书). Vivid and slightly humorous in tone.
病急乱投医	bìng jí luàn tóu yī	when the illness is desperate, one runs to any doctor — A proverb rather than a classical chengyu: desperation abandons judgment. Used far beyond medicine, for panicked job hunts, failing companies grasping at consultants, or students trying every cram school at once. The tone is sympathetic but critical; the flailing rarely works.
病入膏肓	bìng rù gāo huāng	the illness has entered the vitals: beyond cure — From the Zuǒ Zhuàn: a duke dreams the disease hides in the 膏肓, the region between heart and diaphragm that no needle or medicine can reach. Used today for any situation, not only medical, that has decayed past saving.
不胫而走	bù jìng ér zǒu	"to travel without legs" — (of news) to spread rapidly on its own — A classical chengyu (using 走 in its classical "to run" sense, before 跑 took over). 胫 jìng = the lower leg; the image is of news that has no legs but somehow runs everywhere by itself. Used today for rumors, viral information, and news that spreads without effort.
不要脸	bú yào liǎn	to want no face: shameless, brazen — Not a classical four-character chengyu but a fixed colloquial phrase: to want no face is to discard all sense of shame. A sharp insult, stronger than it looks in English.
充耳不闻	chōng ěr bù wén	"stuff the ear and not hear" — deliberately ignoring what is said — 充 (to fill, to stuff) + 耳 (ear) + 不闻 (not listening). Plugging the ear on purpose , willful deafness to advice, complaint, or truth. From the Shijing (詩經, Classic of Poetry): the earliest citation describes a ruler who stopped his ears to his ministers' counsel.
愁眉苦脸	chóu méi kǔ liǎn	knitted brows and a bitter face: a picture of gloom and worry — 愁 (worry) eyebrows over a 苦 (bitter) face. The whole head sags with anxiety. A vivid, everyday way to say someone looks miserable.
穿针引线	chuān zhēn yǐn xiàn	thread the needle and lead the thread: to act as go-between — Literally the seamstress's first move. Figuratively, to broker a connection between two parties, matchmaking a deal, a marriage, a meeting.
大开眼界	dà kāi yǎn jiè	to greatly open one's horizons — to have one's eyes opened — Lit: greatly-open-eye-boundary. The standard phrase for the experience of suddenly seeing something new and broadening one's outlook. Said after a trip, a museum visit, a striking piece of work.
大手大脚	dà shǒu dà jiǎo	big hands and big feet — extravagant; throwing money around — Lit: big-hand-big-foot. Said of someone who spends lavishly without thinking, makes large gestures, lives beyond their means. Mildly disapproving, the opposite of 节俭 jiéjiǎn (frugal).
滴水穿石	dī shuǐ chuān shí	dripping water bores through stone: persistence wins — The image of a single drip, repeated for years, drilling a hole in rock. The standard idiom for patient, steady effort overcoming a hard obstacle.
东奔西跑	dōng bēn xī pǎo	"running east and dashing west" — to rush around in every direction — A four-character formula combining 奔 and 跑 across the directional pair 东 dōng / 西 xī. Used for someone constantly on the move, often in service of obligations or in pursuit of a livelihood.
对症下药	duì zhèng xià yào	prescribe according to the symptoms; tailor the remedy to the problem — 对 (match) + 症 (symptoms) + 下药 (administer medicine). From the case lore of the Han physician Hua Tuo, who gave two patients with identical headaches different prescriptions because the causes differed.
耳目一新	ěr mù yī xīn	"ears and eyes completely renewed" — a refreshingly new impression — 耳 (ear) + 目 (eye) + 一 (completely) + 新 (new). All your senses reset , everything seems fresh and surprising. Used for places recently renovated, new presentations, or any experience that resets one's perception. The compound treats ears and eyes as a pair, the full perceptual system.
耳濡目染	ěr rú mù rǎn	"ears soaked, eyes stained" — influenced by constant exposure — 耳濡 (ear soaked/wetted) + 目染 (eye stained). The effect of growing up surrounded by something , gradually absorbing it through the senses without formal instruction. Often used for children who learn by immersion: 耳濡目染，自然就会了 ("soaked by ears and eyes, one naturally learns").
耳闻目睹	ěr wén mù dǔ	"heard with the ear, seen with the eye" — witnessed firsthand — 耳 (ear) + 闻 (heard) + 目 (eye) + 睹 (seen). Both the ear and the eye were present and active. A marker of direct personal testimony, as opposed to hearsay. Used in legal testimony, journalism, memoir, and any context where the authority of a claim depends on personal presence.
发扬光大	fā yáng guāng dà	to carry forward and make flourish: to develop a good thing to its fullest — Lit: send-forth-raise-bright-great. Said of carrying on a tradition, virtue, or fine quality and making it grow even greater. The fā reading: to bring something forth and develop it. 把好传统发扬光大 "to carry a good tradition forward."
感同身受	gǎn tóng shēn shòu	to feel it as if one's own body received it; deep empathy — Originally a formula of thanks on another's behalf (I feel your kindness to them as if I received it myself); modern usage has shifted it to empathy generally, feeling someone's pain as one's own.
骨肉相连	gǔ ròu xiāng lián	bone and flesh joined together: bound by blood kinship — The image is that bone and flesh cannot be pulled apart. Used for the inseparable bond between family members, and figuratively for a homeland and its people.
画龙点睛	huà lóng diǎn jīng	drawing a dragon and dotting the eye — the small finishing touch that brings the whole alive — From a Tang anecdote: the painter Zhang Sengyou painted dragons but refused to add eyes; when pressed, he dotted the eyes of two , and they immediately flew off the wall. The dot is what brings the form to life. Used today for the small but decisive move that makes a piece of work click.
巾帼英雄	jīn guó yīng xióng	heroine — a woman of exceptional courage and achievement — Lit: kerchief-hood hero. 巾帼 jīnguó is the traditional women's headdress, used by extension to mean women collectively. A 巾帼英雄 is a female hero, a woman who has achieved what is extraordinary even by the standards of men. Hua Mulan 花木兰 is the archetype. Used to praise exceptional women in any field.
久病成医	jiǔ bìng chéng yī	long illness makes the patient a doctor — Chronic experience becomes expertise. Said of patients who know their condition better than junior doctors, and by extension of anyone whose long exposure to a problem has made them its reluctant expert: the veteran user who knows the software's bugs better than the developers.
口是心非	kǒu shì xīn fēi	the mouth says yes but the heart says no — saying one thing while thinking another — Lit: mouth-yes-heart-no. The classic expression for hypocrisy or false agreement: when outward words and inner feelings are opposites. Used for people who agree to your face while disagreeing inside, or who say what they think you want to hear rather than what they actually believe.
埋头苦干	mái tóu kǔ gàn	to bury the head and toil hard: to work diligently without distraction — Lit: bury-head-bitter-work. To lower one's head and labor away, absorbed in the task and indifferent to praise or notice. The picture is of someone bent over their work, head down.
妙手回春	miào shǒu huí chūn	miraculous hands bring back the spring; masterly healing — 妙手 (wondrous hands) + 回春 (return spring, restore life's season). The stock phrase of gratitude to a doctor, embroidered on the silk banners (锦旗 jǐnqí) that patients present to hospital departments, a living custom: the walls of a respected clinic hang red with them.
明哲保身	míng zhé bǎo shēn	the wise protect themselves; prudent self-preservation — From the Book of Odes, where it praised wise self-conduct. Two millennia of court politics turned it ambivalent: today it usually criticizes the colleague who sees the problem and says nothing, keeping their own 身 safe. A compliment in the Zhou dynasty, a workplace accusation now.
目不转睛	mù bù zhuǎn jīng	"eyes not turning from the pupil" — to stare fixedly, riveted — 目 (eye) + 不转 (not turning) + 睛 (pupil). Eyes so fixed on something that even the pupils don't move. Used for complete absorption in watching a performance, a fight, or any gripping sight.
目中无人	mù zhōng wú rén	"no people in the eye" — arrogant; treating no one as worthy of notice — 目 (eye) + 中 (within) + 无 (without) + 人 (people). Looking out from one's eye and seeing no one worth registering , arrogance so total that others become invisible. A criticism of conceit and contempt. 目空一切 (eye-empty-everything) intensifies this further: not even things are worth looking at.
跑了和尚跑不了庙	pǎo le hé shàng pǎo bu liǎo miào	"the monk may run, but the temple cannot run" — even if the person flees, the institution stays put — A folk saying, not a classical chengyu, but functionally fixed. The proverb captures a specific reasoning about accountability: a debtor or wrongdoer may try to flee, but the property, family, or institution they are tied to will remain, and can be held responsible.
跑龙套	pǎo lóng tào	"to run the dragon costume" — to play a minor role; to be a bit-part player — From traditional Chinese opera. 龙套 lóngtào is the costume worn by the chorus of attendants (soldiers, servants, courtiers) who fill out a scene without lines. To 跑龙套 is to be one of these background figures: present, busy, but never central.
劈头盖脸	pī tóu gài liǎn	splitting the head and covering the face: full in the face, without holding back — Describes a torrent that lands straight on you, a downpour of rain or a barrage of scolding, leaving no chance to dodge.
评头论足	píng tóu lùn zú	to judge the head and discuss the feet: to nitpick and find fault — Lit: appraise-head-discuss-feet. Originally about appraising a person's looks head to toe; now it means to carp over trifles and make petty, unwanted criticisms of others.
气吞山河	qì tūn shān hé	spirit that could swallow mountains and rivers — magnificent, all-encompassing force — Lit: qi-swallow-mountain-river. A classical idiom for heroic grandeur: the qi of a great army, a historical figure, or a piece of calligraphy or poetry so powerful it seems to contain all of nature within it. Often used in literary and historical commentary.
气壮山河	qì zhuàng shān hé	magnificent spirit that fills the mountains and rivers — Lit: qi-strong-mountain-river. Similar to 气吞山河 but with a sense of steadiness rather than swallowing, the qi standing as firm and vast as mountains and rivers. Used for patriotic spirit, heroic resolve, or great art. A phrase from Wen Tianxiang's 正气歌 tradition.
千钧一发	qiān jūn yī fà	"a thousand jun hanging by one hair" — a moment of extreme peril; hanging by a thread — 千钧 (a thousand jun, an enormous weight, the 钧 being an ancient heavy measure) suspended by 一发 (a single hair). The image is of a crushing weight held by the thinnest possible thread, an instant of utmost danger in which the slightest thing decides catastrophe or escape.
群龙无首	qún lóng wú shǒu	a flock of dragons with no head: a leaderless, directionless group — Lit: flock-dragons-without-head. A phrase from the Book of Changes: many capable members but no one to lead them, so the group drifts. Used of an organization left rudderless after its leader is gone.
如雷贯耳	rú léi guàn ěr	"like thunder piercing the ear" — a name of thunderous fame — 如 (like) + 雷 (thunder) + 贯 (to pierce, to pass through) + 耳 (ear). A famous name entering the ear with the force of thunder.
身体力行	shēn tǐ lì xíng	to practice with one's own body and strength; to live what one preaches — 身 and 体 here are both verbs in the classical grammar: to embody personally (身) and to enact (体力行, carry out with strength). The Confucian ideal of the teacher whose conduct is the curriculum. Modern usage praises leaders who do rather than direct: 环保不能只喊口号，要身体力行。
手到擒来	shǒu dào qín lái	the hand arrives and the capture is made — easily accomplished — Lit: hand-arrives-capture-comes. Used to describe a task accomplished with ease and confidence , the expert reaches out and the thing is done. An expression of mastery making difficulty look effortless. 对他来说，这件事手到擒来 "For him, this was a cinch."
手忙脚乱	shǒu máng jiǎo luàn	hands busy, feet disordered — flustered and disorganized — Lit: hands-busy-feet-chaotic. The image of someone scrambling in a panic, hands and feet moving in disordered haste. Used to describe being overwhelmed and disorganized: the opposite of calm competence. 他一到过年就手忙脚乱 "Around New Year, he's always running around in a flustered state."
手足无措	shǒu zú wú cuò	"hands and feet without placement" — at a complete loss, not knowing what to do — 手 (hands) + 足 (feet) + 无措 (nowhere to place them). So flustered that your hands and feet don't know where to go. From the Analects (Book XIII): Confucius warns that without ritual propriety, the people will be 手足无措.
首屈一指	shǒu qū yī zhǐ	the first finger you bend to count: second to none, the very best — Lit: first-bend-one-finger. When you count off the best on your fingers, this is the one you bend down first. Used to rank a person or thing as the foremost in its field. 他在这个行业首屈一指 "He is second to none in this industry."
水滴石穿	shuǐ dī shí chuān	water drips and the stone is bored through: small force, long time — The same image reordered, traced to the Han text Hàn Shū. Stresses the result, the worn-through stone, as proof that small efforts accumulate.
体大思精	tǐ dà sī jīng	grand in structure and fine in thought: a work both vast and meticulous — Lit: body-large-thought-fine. Praise for a major work, a book or system, whose overall framework is grand while its detailed reasoning is precise. Here 体 is the structure or framework of the whole.
体无完肤	tǐ wú wán fū	the body without a patch of whole skin: wounded all over, or torn apart in criticism — Lit: body-without-intact-skin. Literally beaten until no skin is unbroken; figuratively, an argument or piece of work refuted or criticized so thoroughly that nothing is left standing.
同病相怜	tóng bìng xiāng lián	sharing the same sickness, they pity each other: fellow sufferers sympathize — From the Wú Yuè Chūnqiū. People who endure the same hardship feel a natural bond, the way two patients in one ward understand each other without explanation.
痛心疾首	tòng xīn jí shǒu	pain in the heart and ache in the head: bitterly grieved and resentful — Lit: pain-heart-ache-head. To feel grief and indignation so deep that heart and head both ache. Used for bitter regret or anger over a wrong or a loss. 对这种浪费，他感到痛心疾首 "He was bitterly grieved at such waste."
头头是道	tóu tóu shì dào	clear and logical at every point: well-reasoned and articulate — Lit: every-head-is-the-way. Said of speech or analysis that is orderly and convincing from start to finish, with each point landing soundly. 说得头头是道 "to argue every point convincingly."
头重脚轻	tóu zhòng jiǎo qīng	heavy on top and light on the feet: top-heavy and unstable — Lit: head-heavy-feet-light. Used of anything badly balanced, with too much weight or emphasis at the top: a wobbling structure, a lopsided plan, or a dizzy, unsteady feeling.
望女成凤	wàng nǚ chéng fèng	hoping your daughter will become a phoenix — parental ambition for daughters — The female counterpart to 望子成龙 wàng zǐ chéng lóng (hoping your son will become a dragon). 凤 fèng (phoenix) is the female auspicious creature paired with 龙 (dragon). The phoenix (凤凰 fènghuáng) represents feminine grace, beauty, and virtue.
望眼欲穿	wàng yǎn yù chuān	gazing till the eyes would bore through: aching with anticipation — Eyes straining at the road so hard they might pierce the distance. Describes longing for someone's return or news that will not come.
无病呻吟	wú bìng shēn yín	to groan without being sick: to complain for no reason — A literary critic's jab at writers who manufacture sorrow they do not feel. Now applied to anyone who dramatizes trivial troubles or affects suffering for effect.
五体投地	wǔ tǐ tóu dì	"five limbs to the ground" — to prostrate fully; to admire with complete humility — 五体 (the five parts of the body: the two knees, the two elbows or hands, and the head) plus 投地 (cast to the ground). It names the Buddhist full prostration in which all five touch the floor, the deepest bodily gesture of reverence.
嬉皮笑脸	xī pí xiào liǎn	grinning cheekily: to behave with a flippant, unserious grin — A face stuck in a smirk, used to describe someone who refuses to take a moment seriously. Often a parent's rebuke to a child who jokes through a scolding.
行尸走肉	xíng shī zǒu ròu	a walking corpse, a moving piece of flesh — alive in body but dead in spirit — Lit: walking-corpse, walking-flesh. Said of someone who has lost the inner life that makes a person alive in any meaningful sense , going through motions without thought, feeling, or purpose. Sharp, almost cruel; reserved for those judged to have given up entirely.
血肉相连	xu è ròu xiāng lián	blood and flesh linked: tied as closely as flesh to blood — A near-twin of 骨肉相连, stressing the living, circulating closeness between people, or between an individual and a cause they cannot separate from.
眼花缭乱	yǎn huā liáo luàn	eyes blurring, vision tangling — dazzled, overwhelmed by abundance — Lit: eye-flower-tangle-disorder. Used for the sensory overload of a busy market, a fireworks display, a dazzling performance: too much beauty or activity to take in at once. Almost always positive in tone (the overload of richness, not chaos).
扬眉吐气	yáng méi tǔ qì	to feel proud and elated; to finally breathe out after long suffering — Lit: raise-eyebrows-exhale-qi. The physical sensation of relief and pride after enduring hardship or injustice. The eyebrows lift, the breath that was held in tension is finally released. Used for vindication, promotion after years of struggle, or national pride after historical humiliation.
药到病除	yào dào bìng chú	the medicine arrives, the illness is gone: an instant, effective cure — A praise phrase for a skilled doctor or a decisive remedy. Extended to any solution that fixes a problem cleanly and at once.
一步一个脚印	yī bù yī gè jiǎo yìn	"one step, one footprint" — steady, methodical progress — Lit: each step leaves exactly one footprint; nothing rushed, nothing skipped. A modern proverb for diligent, unhurried work that builds on itself. Often used in career advice, study guidance, and praise for craftsmen.
一触即发	yī chù jí fā	touch and it ignites — a situation on the verge of explosion — Lit: one-touch-immediately-fire. A crisis situation where the slightest action will trigger an explosion: political tensions, a fragile negotiation, volatile emotions. Originally describing hair-trigger mechanisms, now widely applied to any situation of extreme tension.
一口气	yī kǒu qì	in one breath; without stopping; in one go — Lit: one-mouth-breath. Describes doing something continuously, without pause, in one uninterrupted exhalation of effort. 他一口气跑了十公里 "He ran ten kilometers without stopping." 一口气读完这本书 "Read the whole book in one sitting." The most common and versatile 气 idiom in everyday speech.
一目了然	yī mù liǎo rán	"one glance makes it clear" — obvious at a single look — 一 (one) + 目 (eye-glance) + 了然 (clearly understood). Describes things immediately comprehensible , a diagram that explains itself, an argument whose logic is visible. The phrase appears in Qing dynasty texts and entered general usage by the early Republic period.
一眼万年	yī yǎn wàn nián	a single glance, ten thousand years — a look that lasts forever — Lit: one-glance-ten-thousand-years. Modern romantic register, especially in song lyrics and pop fiction: the glance at first meeting that settles a lifetime. Builds on 眼 as the measure word for a single act of looking.
以身作则	yǐ shēn zuò zé	to set a standard through personal conduct — lead by example — 以 (by means of) + 身 (one whole person; conduct) + 作则 (set a rule/standard). 身 is the entire person understood as embodied conduct , the leader is not separate from the example set. Among the most-cited leadership principles in Chinese management and parenting discourse. 作为领导者，必须以身作则。
异口同声	yì kǒu tóng shēng	different mouths, the same sound — unanimous — Everyone says the same thing at once: the committee that answers in chorus, the witnesses whose accounts match. Neutral to positive in tone; suspicious unanimity would be phrased differently. The mirror image of 众说纷纭, where every mouth says something else.
有眼无珠	yǒu yǎn wú zhū	to have eyes but no pupils — to fail to recognize what is right in front of you — Lit: have-eyes-without-pearls (珠 = pearl, here pupil). Said of someone who has missed an obvious quality or opportunity: failed to spot a talent, dismissed a worthy person. Often self-deprecating: 我真是有眼无珠 "I had eyes but couldn't see."
鱼肉百姓	yú ròu bǎi xìng	to make fish-meat of the common people: to oppress and exploit them — Here 鱼肉 is a verb: to treat people as meat to be carved up. A classical condemnation of tyrannical officials, traceable to the Shǐjì. Still used to denounce abuse of power.
站不住脚	zhàn bù zhù jiǎo	cannot stand on its feet: untenable — Literally "the feet cannot hold their stand." Used of an argument, excuse, or theory that collapses under scrutiny. Colloquial and very frequent.
站稳脚跟	zhàn wěn jiǎo gēn	to stand firm; to gain a foothold — Literally "to stand steady on one's heels." A common set phrase, not a classical four-character idiom: to establish oneself securely in a place, job, or market before making a move. Everyday modern usage.
站在风口浪尖	zhàn zài fēng kǒu làng jiān	to stand where the wind and waves meet: at the eye of the storm — To be exposed at the most dangerous, contested forefront of events, where pressure and scrutiny are fiercest. Modern journalistic register, common in commentary.
知足常乐	zhī zú cháng lè	knowing sufficiency brings lasting happiness — contentment is the source of joy — From Laozi (老子·道德经, Chapter 33): 知足者富 (zhī zú zhě fù) , "Those who know sufficiency are wealthy." The fuller proverb 知足常乐 is not directly from the Daodejing text but is a Taoist-derived commonplace built on that chapter's logic.
指手画脚	zhǐ shǒu huà jiǎo	pointing with hands and gesturing with feet — bossing others around with showy gestures — Lit: point-hand-draw-foot. Said of someone who criticises and orders others about while making theatrical gestures. Carries a strong note of irritation: the speaker is performing authority rather than exercising it.
罪魁祸首	zuì kuí huò shǒu	the ringleader of the crime and the chief of the disaster: the main culprit — Lit: crime-chief-disaster-head. The principal wrongdoer, the one most to blame for an offense or calamity. Here 首 is the head, the ringleader. 他是这场事故的罪魁祸首 "He is the chief culprit behind this accident."
阿是穴	ā shì xu é	Ashi points — Ashi points: tender spots with no fixed location, needled wherever the patient says "Ah, yes, that is it!" when pressed. Not on any meridian map, but recognized in classical texts as clinically valid.
艾灸	ài jiǔ	Moxibustion , burning dried mugwort at or near acupoints to warm and tonify. Paired with needling in the term 针灸 (zhēnjiǔ), which names the two techniques together. Used specifically for cold-type and deficiency-type conditions where warming stimulation is indicated.
昂首	áng shǒu	to hold the head high; head raised — 昂 áng (to raise, to lift up) + 首 shǒu (head). To carry the head high, in pride or defiance. 昂首挺胸 "head high and chest out" is the stock image of someone walking with proud, confident bearing. Literary in tone; the spoken equivalent uses 头 tóu.
八卦	bā guà	the eight trigrams; gossip — 八 bā + 卦 guà (trigram; divinatory figure). The eight three-line figures of yin and yang at the foundation of the Yijing, used in divination, feng shui, and cosmology. In colloquial modern usage the same word means gossip: 八卦新闻 (tabloid news), 别八卦了 (stop gossiping).
拔罐	bá guàn	cupping therapy — 拔 bá (to pull; to extract) + 罐 guàn (jar; can). Cupping: placing glass or bamboo cups on the skin after creating a vacuum (by heat or suction) to draw up the skin and underlying tissues, promoting local blood and qi circulation.
白茶	bái chá	white tea — minimally processed; wither and dry
绊脚石	bàn jiǎo shí	stumbling block; obstacle — 绊 bàn (to trip) + 脚 + 石 shí (stone). The foot-tripping stone. Both literal (a stone in the path) and figurative (an obstacle to progress).
帮手	bāng shǒu	helper; assistant — 帮 bāng (to help) + 手 shǒu (person; hand). A helper , someone who lends a hand. Also 帮忙 bāng máng (to help out , the common spoken form) and 帮助 bāngzhù (to assist , more formal).
包	bāo	to wrap, bundle — To wrap, to enclose , the verb for making dumplings (包饺子), as well as the root of 包子 (stuffed bun). Also means "to guarantee" or "to take responsibility for."
饱餐	bǎo cān	to eat a hearty meal — 饱 (full) + 餐 (cān, meal): to eat heartily, to feast. Often appears as 饱餐一顿 (bǎocān yí dùn, to have a good filling meal), with a faint literary warmth.
饱含	bǎo hán	to be brimming with — 饱 (full) + 含 (hán, to hold inside): to be charged with, brimming with. Used for tears, feeling, or meaning, as in 饱含深情 (bǎohán shēnqíng, brimming with deep feeling).
饱和	bǎo hé	saturated, saturation — 饱 (full) + 和 (hé, to mix, to combine): saturation. A technical term for a solution, market, or air that can absorb no more, as in 市场已经饱和 (shìchǎng yǐjīng bǎohé, the market is already saturated).
饱满	bǎo mǎn	full, plump; brimming — 饱 (full) + 满 (mǎn, full to the brim): plump and rounded, or figuratively brimming with vigor. Describes grain, fruit, and spirits alike, as in 精神饱满 (jīngshén bǎomǎn, full of energy).
奔跑	bēn pǎo	to dash; to run hard; to gallop — 奔 (bēn, to rush; to gallop) + 跑. A more vivid and literary form than plain 跑. 奔 originally described the gallop of a horse; 奔跑 carries that sense of full-speed exertion.
本草	běn cǎo	Materia medica — Materia medica: the traditional term for the pharmacological literature and its subject matter.
本草纲目	běn cǎo gāng mù	the materia medica
鼻	bí	nose
闭	bì	to close, to shut (formal)
病毒	bìng dú	virus — 病 + 毒 (dú, poison): a "sickness poison," the standard word for a biological virus and, by extension, a computer virus. The same term covers the flu and the malware on your hard drive.
病房	bìng fáng	hospital ward, sickroom — 病 + 房 (fáng, room): the room where patients are kept, a hospital ward. It pairs naturally with 病床 (bìngchuáng, hospital bed), bringing the original sickbed of the radical 疒 full circle.
病假	bìng jià	sick leave
病菌	bìng jūn	germ, pathogenic bacterium — 病 + 菌 (jūn, bacterium, fungus): a disease-causing germ. Where 病毒 names a virus, 病菌 names the bacterial culprit, the everyday word a parent uses when telling a child to wash their hands.
病情	bìng qíng	condition of an illness — 病 + 情 (qíng, situation, state): the state of a sickness, how the illness stands. Doctors use it to report whether a patient is stable, worsening, or improving.
病人	bìng rén	patient, sick person — 病 + 人 (rén, person): a sick person, the general word for a patient in a hospital or clinic. It names the person by their condition rather than their role, unlike 患者 (huànzhě), which is the more clinical register used in medical writing.
不舒服	bù shū fu	Unwell — Unwell: "not comfortable." The all-purpose symptom, the polite sick-day excuse, and the doctor's opening question in negative form: 哪里不舒服?
不足	bù zú	insufficient; not enough; shortcoming — 不 (not) + 足 (sufficient). The negation of sufficiency. 时间不足 (insufficient time); 不足为奇 (not enough to be surprising , meaning "nothing to be surprised about"); 美中不足 (a flaw in an otherwise perfect situation , literally "not enough in the beautiful center").
部分	bù fen	part; section; portion — 部 bù (section) + 分 fen (a share). A part of something larger, the everyday word for a portion: 大部分 dàbùfen "the greater part, most of," 一部分 yí bùfen "a portion." Note the second syllable goes neutral.
部首	bù shǒu	radical (the section head of a character) — 部 bù (section, division) + 首 shǒu (head). The radical of a character: the head-element under which a dictionary groups it. 首 itself is a 部首. To look a character up in a paper dictionary, you find its 部首 first, then count the remaining strokes.
插一脚	chā yī jiǎo	to butt in; to insert oneself into someone else's affair — 插 chā (to insert) + 一 yī (one) + 脚. To stick a foot in where it wasn't invited , usually meddlesome but sometimes opportunistic. Cousin of English "to put one's foot in." Often pejorative.
车站	chē zhàn	station; bus or train stop — 车 (chē, vehicle) + 站 (stop): the place where buses or trains halt and passengers board. The base word for any ground-transit station; specify with 火车站 huǒchēzhàn (train station) or 汽车站 qìchēzhàn (bus station).
撑	chēng	stuffed, overfull
吃饱	chī bǎo	to eat one's fill — 吃 (chī, eat) + 饱 (full) as a resultative pair: to eat until satisfied. The negative is 没吃饱 (méi chī bǎo, did not eat enough), and a question often takes 吃饱了吗 (chī bǎo le ma).
赤脚	chì jiǎo	barefoot — 赤 chì (red; bare; naked) + 脚. Bare-footed. Famously the term in 赤脚医生 chìjiǎo yīshēng, the "barefoot doctors" of the Mao-era rural healthcare program , village paramedics with minimal training who made primary care available across the countryside.
充足	chōng zú	ample; abundant; fully sufficient — 充 (chōng, to fill; to be full) + 足 (sufficient). Filled to sufficiency , not merely enough but abundantly enough. 阳光充足 (ample sunlight); 准备充足 (fully prepared); 充足的理由 (compelling reasons). Stronger than 足够 zúgòu (merely enough).
出口	chū kǒu	to export; an exit; to speak — 出 chū (exit; emerge) + 口 kǒu (mouth; opening; port). Three interlocking meanings from the same compound. As trade: to export , goods going out through the port. As architecture: the exit of a building or tunnel. As speech: 出口成章 (words emerge perfectly formed , to speak with eloquence).
穿过	chuāng uò	to pass through; to cross — 穿 (to pass through) + 过 (guò, across, a directional complement). The verb of moving through and out the other side, a crowd, a forest, a tunnel. 过 marks the full traverse, in one side and out the far side.
穿上	chuān shàng	to put on (and have it on) — 穿 (to thread on) + 上 (shàng, up/onto, here a result complement). The complement 上 marks the garment as now in place. 穿上 stresses the completed act of getting it on, against bare 穿 which can describe the habit of wearing.
穿梭	chuān suō	to shuttle back and forth
穿透	chuān tòu	to penetrate; to pierce through — 穿 (to bore through) + 透 (tòu, thoroughly/through). Two synonyms stacked for force, used for light, sound, water, or radiation breaking all the way through a barrier. The technical word in physics and medicine for penetration.
穿衣	chuān yī	to put on clothes; to dress — 穿 (to thread through) + 衣 (yī, clothing). A verb-object pair, the plainest way to say "get dressed." 衣 is the general word for what covers the upper body, so 穿衣 frames dressing as passing the body into a garment.
穿越	chuān yu è	to pass through; to cut across; (popular fiction) to time-travel — Cutting through a region. Also the standard verb for popular time-travel fiction (穿越剧, 穿越小说) where a modern character is thrown into a past dynasty.
穿着	chuān zhe	wearing; dressed in (current state) — 穿 (to put on clothing) + 着 = the dressed state is in force. This is among the most natural uses of durative 着, because clothing occupies a position continuously. The same pattern applies to 戴着 (wearing a hat/glasses), 拿着 (holding), 带着 (carrying/bringing).
传染病	chuán rǎn bìng	infectious disease — 传染 (chuánrǎn, to infect, to pass on) + 病: a disease that spreads from one person to another. It is the headline word during any epidemic and the category label on public-health notices.
春分	Chūn fēn	Spring Equinox — balance of yin and yang — 春 chūn (spring) + 分 fēn (to divide; to share equally). The Spring Equinox: the day of perfect balance, when day and night are equal. In traditional cosmology, the moment when yin and yang are in exact equilibrium before yang takes the lead toward summer.
聪明	cōng míng	clever; intelligent; sharp-minded — 聪 (cōng, sharp-eared; quick-witted , 耳 radical) + 明 (bright, clear-sighted , 日 sun + 月 moon). Intelligence as a compound of keen hearing and clear sight. 聪 alone originally meant "sharp of ear" in classical texts; combined with 明 (bright), it became the standard adjective for intelligence.
戴	dài	to wear (accessories)
丹田	dān tián	the elixir field — the body's energy center — 丹 dān (cinnabar; red; elixir) + 田 tián (field). The "elixir field," the body's primary energy reservoir. Three locations: 下丹田 xià dāntián (lower, 3 inches below the navel, the primary center and focus of most practice) · 中丹田 zhōng dāntián (middle, the chest) · 上丹田 shàng dāntián (upper, the head).
得气	dé qì	Arrival of qi, the sensation confirming correct needling — Arrival of qi, the sensation confirming correct needling: heaviness, distension, or a spreading warmth at the needled point. Classical and modern acupuncturists both treat déqì as the essential marker of effective treatment.
低头	dī tóu	to lower one's head; to bow; to yield — 低 (low, lower) + 头 (head). The single most loaded compound on this page. Physically, it is simply to look down , at a phone, at work, at the ground while walking.
点头	diǎn tóu	to nod (the head) — 点 diǎn (to touch lightly, to dip) + 头 tóu (head). To dip the head: to nod in agreement or greeting. 点头同意 "to nod in assent." Its opposite gesture is 摇头 yáotóu, to shake the head.
调理	tiáo lǐ	to regulate; to tonify
丢脸	diū liǎn	to lose moral face; to be shamed; disgraceful — More severe than 丢面子. 丢脸 involves an element of moral failure , betrayal, cheating, acting in ways that contradict one's stated principles. Can affect not just the individual but their family and group. The scope is broader and the damage more lasting than simple social embarrassment.
冬至	dōng zhì	Winter Solstice — the turning of yin into yang — Winter Solstice, the most important of the twenty-four solar terms, celebrated as a major festival throughout Chinese history. Marks the shortest day and the nadir of the yin/yang cycle, from which point yang begins its return. Dumplings are eaten in the north; tangyuan in the south.
动功	dòng gōng	moving qigong; forms with coordinated movement and breath — 动 dòng (moving; active) + 功 gōng (practice). Qigong with movement, from slow and gentle forms like 八段锦 Bāduànjǐn (Eight Brocades) and 五禽戏 Wǔqínxì (Five Animals) to more vigorous practices. Movement is synchronized with breath and guided by awareness.
动气	dòng qì	to get riled up; to let anger stir (colloquial) — 动 dòng (to move; to stir) + 气 qì. For qi to be "moved," to let irritation become active anger. Often used in negative advice: 别动气 "don't get worked up." The qi-as-fluid logic is explicit: something causes the qi to shift and agitate.
洞	dòng	hole
毒性	dú xìng	Toxicity, one of the key properties documented for each drug in the Bencao Gangmu. Li distinguished between non-toxic (无毒), slightly toxic (小毒), moderately toxic (有毒), and highly toxic (大毒) substances, with corresponding cautions about dosage, preparation, and contraindications.
肚子	dù zi	belly, stomach
饿	è	hungry
耳朵	ěr duo	ear (everyday word) — 耳 (ear) + 朵 (duǒ, flower; measure word for flowers, ears, and clouds). 朵 is the measure word for ear-shaped objects; 耳朵 is the unmarked everyday word for the ear as a physical body part. 一只耳朵 or 一个耳朵 are both acceptable, but 耳朵 alone typically refers to the ear without a number.
耳机	ěr jī	earphones; headphones — 耳 (ear) + 机 (machine; device). The device for the ear. 入耳式耳机 (in-ear earphones), 头戴式耳机 (over-ear headphones). A modern compound that keeps 耳 as its anchor, illustrating how the character's body-part meaning extends naturally to technology.
耳目	ěr mù	ears and eyes; spies; awareness — 耳 (ear) + 目 (eye). The two organs of alert perception together , the paired sentinels. Used figuratively for one's information network, or for spies and informants: 皇帝的耳目 (the emperor's ears and eyes). Also in 耳目一新 ěrmù yīxīn (a completely fresh impression, as if new eyes and ears).
耳闻	ěr wén	to have heard of; auditory knowledge — 耳 (ear) + 闻 (to hear; to be known). To have heard of something, often at a distance or through others.
发表	fā biǎo	to publish; to issue; to express (publicly) — 发 fā (to issue) + 表 biǎo (to express; surface). To put a piece of writing or an opinion out into the public sphere: 发表文章 "to publish an article," 发表意见 "to voice an opinion."
发出	fā chū	to give off; to emit; to issue (a sound, light, order) — 发 fā (to emit) + 出 chū (out). To let something out from a source: a sound, a smell, a light, a command. 发出声音 "to make a sound," 发出命令 "to issue an order."
发火	fā huǒ	to lose one's temper; to flare up (contrast) — 发 fā (to emit; to break out) + 火 huǒ (fire). Not a 气 compound, but essential for contrast. Where 生气 is quiet simmering anger, 发火 is the eruption: raised voice, visible emotion, the moment qi becomes fire. Colloquial. Very common in domestic and workplace situations.
发劲	fā jìn	issuing force — explosive power release
发明	fā míng	to invent
发烧	fā shāo	To run a fever — To run a fever: emit burn. 量体温 is to take a temperature; 退烧 is the fever breaking, and 退烧药 the medicine that breaks it.
发生	fā shēng	to happen; to occur; to take place — 发 fā (to send forth) + 生 shēng (life; to arise). For an event to come into being: 发生事故 "an accident happens," 发生变化 "a change occurs." Used of events, not of people deliberately doing things.
发送	fā sòng	to send; to transmit; to dispatch — 发 fā (to send out) + 送 sòng (to deliver). To send a message, signal, or shipment outward to a recipient: 发送邮件 "to send an email." The everyday verb for transmitting in the digital age.
发现	fā xiàn	to discover; to find; to realize — 发 fā (to bring forth) + 现 xiàn (to appear; manifest). To bring something previously hidden into view, whether a fact, an object, or a realization: 发现问题 "to spot a problem," 科学发现 "a scientific discovery."
发展	fā zhǎn	to develop; to grow; development — 发 fā (to bring forth) + 展 zhǎn (to unfold; spread). To grow and expand from a smaller or earlier state: 经济发展 "economic development," 发展中国家 "a developing country." Works as both verb and noun.
翻脸	fān liǎn	to turn hostile; to fall out with someone — 翻 (to flip, turn over) + 脸 (face). To flip the face from friendly to cold in an instant, breaking off goodwill. The vivid version is 翻脸不认人 (fānliǎn bú rèn rén), to turn cold and pretend you never knew the person.
俯首	fǔ shǒu	to bow the head; to submit — 俯 fǔ (to bend down, to lower) + 首 shǒu (head). To lower the head, and by extension to yield or submit. 俯首听命 "to bow the head and take orders" describes obedient submission. The direct opposite of 昂首.
感冒	gǎn mào	cold; flu; to catch a cold — 感 gǎn + 冒 mào (to emit; to brave recklessly). In traditional Chinese medicine, illness = pathogenic qì 气 entering the body. 感冒 = receiving and being overcome by wind-cold 风寒.
歌手	gē shǒu	singer — a skilled vocalist — 歌 gē (song) + 手 shǒu (skilled person). The standard word for a professional singer. This -手 suffix pattern for skilled persons: 歌手 singer, 选手 athlete/contestant, 鼓手 drummer, 射手 archer/shooter, 旗手 flag-bearer.
根	gēn	roots; origin
枸杞	gǒu qǐ	wolfberry / goji berry — the ubiquitous health supplement
骨	gǔ	bone
挂号	guà hào	To register at a hospital — To register at a hospital: hang a number. 挂个专家号 is booking the senior specialist; 挂不上号 is the national complaint about famous hospitals.
观察	guān chá	to observe; to watch closely; observation — 观 guān (to look) + 察 chá (to examine). To watch with attention in order to learn something: a scientist, a doctor, or a detective 观察 their subject. Stronger and more deliberate than simply watching.
贯穿	guàn chuān	to run through, to permeate
果肉	guǒ ròu	fruit pulp, flesh of fruit — 果 (guǒ, fruit) + 肉 (flesh). The soft edible part between the skin and the pit, the meat of the fruit.
咳嗽	ké sou	to cough
红茶	hóng chá	black tea (literally "red tea") — 红 hóng (red) + 茶 chá (tea). Fully oxidized tea, the category English calls black tea. The Chinese name describes the amber-red color of the infusion in the cup; the English name describes the near-black color of the oxidized leaf.
红脸	hóng liǎn	to blush; to flush red (with anger or embarrassment) — 红 (red) + 脸 (face). The cheeks going red, whether from shyness or from a flare of temper. 脸红 (liǎnhóng) reverses the order for the same blush; 红脸 also names the stern, blunt role someone plays in a good-cop bad-cop pairing.
呼吸	hū xī	to breathe; breathing
护士	hù shi	nurse — 护 (protect, the 护 of 保护) + 士 (officer, the scholar-gentleman suffix of 战士 soldier and 博士 PhD). Coined when modern nursing arrived in the late Qing; the profession's day, 护士节, falls on Florence Nightingale's birthday, May 12.
黄帝内经	Huáng dì Nèi jīng	Yellow Emperor's Classic of Internal Medicine
婚礼	hūn lǐ	wedding ceremony — 婚 hūn (marriage) + 礼 lǐ (rite; ceremony). Wedding as ritual. The 礼 suffix creates ceremony words for life's great transitions: 婚礼 wedding · 葬礼 zànglǐ funeral · 成人礼 chéngrénlǐ coming-of-age ceremony · 开幕礼 kāimùlǐ opening ceremony.
婚姻	hūn yīn	marriage; matrimony — 婚 hūn (marriage, 女 woman + 昏 dusk, from the tradition of evening weddings) + 姻 yīn (marriage-alliance; matrimony, 女 woman + 因 cause/connection). The formal compound for marriage. 婚礼 hūnlǐ = wedding ceremony. 结婚 jiéhūn = to get married. 离婚 líhūn = to divorce.
活力	huó lì	vitality; vigor
肌肉	jī ròu	muscle — 肌 (jī, muscle tissue) + 肉 (flesh). The fibrous flesh that moves the body; both characters carry the 月 flesh-radical.
鸡肉	jī ròu	chicken (meat) — 鸡 (chicken) + 肉 (ròu, meat). The flesh on the menu, as opposed to the live bird. Pair it with 牛肉 (niúròu, beef) and 猪肉 (zhūròu, pork) to name the everyday meats.
基础	jī chǔ	foundation; base
急诊	jí zhěn	emergency medical treatment, the ER — 急 (urgent) + 诊 (zhěn, to diagnose). Emergency consultation. 急诊 is the hospital department you go to when something cannot wait, the Chinese counterpart of the emergency room. 看急诊 (kàn jízhěn) means to go to the ER.
疾	jí	ailment, swift
疾病	jí bìng	disease, illness (formal) — 疾 (jí) and 病 are near-synonyms paired into one formal noun. In classical usage 疾 was the slight ailment and 病 the serious one; fused together, 疾病 is the neutral cover term for disease in medical and official language.
集体	jí tǐ	collective; group; the community — 集 jí (to gather) + 体 tǐ (body). A gathered body of people acting as one: 集体活动 "a group activity," 集体主义 "collectivism." Set against the individual, 个人 gèrén.
加油站	jiā yóu zhàn	petrol station — 加油 (jiāyóu, to add fuel) + 站 (station): the place a car stops to refuel. Note that 加油 doubles as the cheer "let's go! keep it up!", literally "add oil," but here it is strictly the fuel sense.
健康	jiàn kāng	health; healthy — 健 (robust) + 康 (well, at ease). The formal health word: 健康码 was the pandemic-era health code, 健康证 the food-worker's health certificate, and 亚健康 ("sub-health") the popular term for run-down-but-not-sick, a category Chinese wellness culture takes seriously.
健身	jiàn shēn	to work out; fitness — 健 (strengthen) + 身 (body). The gym era's verb: 健身房 is the gym, 健身教练 the personal trainer. The park's older exercise culture keeps its own verb, 锻炼, so the generations can be told apart by which word they use.
饺子	jiǎo zi	dumplings — the northern New Year food — The quintessential northern Chinese New Year food, eaten at midnight on New Year's Eve in many northern households. The shape resembles the 元宝 yuánbǎo (ancient sycee gold ingots), making them symbols of wealth.
脚本	jiǎo běn	script (theatrical, film); (computing) script (programmatic) — 脚 jiǎo + 本 běn (root; book; basis). The script for a play, film, or shoot , and, in the modern technical vocabulary, a programming script (a JavaScript file is a JS 脚本). 经络	jīng luò	meridians; the qi channel system — 经 jīng (warp thread; main channel) + 络 luò (network thread; connecting channel). 口气	kǒu qì	manner of speaking; breath smell; implied attitude — 口 kǒu (mouth) + 气 qì. Operates on two levels: (1) the qi from the mouth, literally breath smell (口气重 "bad breath"); (2) the implied attitude and tone in what someone says, what their words reveal about how they feel. 你这个口气 "That tone of yours…" carries reproach.
口味	kǒu wèi	flavor; taste preference; palate — 口 kǒu (mouth) + 味 wèi (flavor; taste). Both the objective flavor of food and the subjective preference of a taster. 这不合我的口味 "This doesn't suit my taste." 口味重 kǒuwèi zhòng = "strong-flavored preferences" , liking bold, heavy flavors.
口吻	kǒu wěn	manner of speech; implied standpoint (literary) — 口 kǒu (mouth) + 吻 wěn (lips; to kiss, the form of the mouth). 气候	qì hòu	climate; the long-term atmospheric pattern — 气 qì + 候 hòu (season; to wait; signs). 候 originally referred to the five-day intervals used in classical Chinese to track seasonal changes. 气候 is the patterned behavior of atmospheric qi over time: climate, not weather. 气色	qì sè	complexion; appearance of health (lit. "qi-color") — 气 qì + 色 sè (color; appearance). 气势	qì shì	momentum; imposing presence; force of spirit — 气 qì + 势 shì (power; momentum; force, the potential energy of a situation). 人口	rén kǒu	population — lit. "people-mouths" — 人 rén (person) + 口 kǒu (mouth). Population , the number of mouths to feed, the count of human presences. 中国人口约14亿 "China's population is approximately 1.4 billion." 人口众多 rénkǒu zhòngduō = "large population" (a standard description of China).

题目	tí mù	exam question; title; subject (of an essay) — 题 tí (heading) + 目 mù (item; entry). The item set at the head of a test, or the title given to a composition: 考试题目 "exam questions," 作文题目 "the essay prompt." It points at the specific thing to be answered or written about.
体会	tǐ huì	to grasp through experience; the understanding so gained — 体 tǐ (body) + 会 huì (to understand). To come to understand a thing by living it, and the insight that results. 深刻的体会 "a deep understanding (won from experience)."
体贴	tǐ tiē	considerate; thoughtful; attentive to others' needs — 体 tǐ (to feel in the body) + 贴 tiē (to stick close). To feel another's situation closely and act with care: 体贴的丈夫 "a considerate husband." It is warmth shown through attention to small needs.
体验	tǐ yàn	to experience; to live through; an experience — 体 tǐ (body) + 验 yàn (to test, to verify). To know something by passing through it in person: 体验生活 "to experience real life." As a noun, the experience itself: 难忘的体验 "an unforgettable experience."
体育	tǐ yù	physical education; sports — 体 tǐ (body) + 育 yù (to nurture, to educate). The cultivation of the body: school PE, and sport in general. 体育馆 tǐyùguǎn is a gymnasium; 体育课 is a PE class.
体质	tǐ zhì	Constitution — Constitution: one's inherent physical and energetic type, which determines predispositions to certain diseases and guides appropriate diet, lifestyle, and treatment choices. Nine constitutional types are defined in modern TCM standardization.
体重	tǐ zhòng	body weight — 体 tǐ (body) + 重 zhòng (weight). How much a person weighs: 量体重 "to weigh oneself." The weight that the body itself carries.
天气	tiān qì	weather — 天 tiān (sky) + 气 qì (breath; air; vital force). Literally: the sky's breath. In traditional Chinese cosmology, qì 气 is the invisible force that animates all phenomena; weather is the sky breathing. HSK 1, but it carries this depth beneath the surface.
跳	tiào	to jump
停	tíng	to stop, to park
通过	tōng guò	to pass through; to get past; by means of; via — 通 (to go through) + 过 guò (to cross; to pass). The everyday verb for getting through something: passing through a gate, passing an exam, a motion passing a vote.
铜人	tóng rén	Bronze man — Bronze man: the life-sized bronze figure commissioned in 1026 CE with channel lines and point locations engraved on the surface and a hollow interior. Filled with water and wax for examination purposes, it was the world's first standardized anatomical teaching model for acupuncture instruction.
痛	tòng	pain, to ache
头发	tóu fa	hair (on the head) — 头 tóu (head) + 发 fà (hair). The hair on a person's head, as a mass. Note that 发 here is the fà / 髮 reading; in casual speech the syllable often softens toward neutral tone. Distinct from 毛 máo, the word for body hair and fur.
推手	tuī shǒu	pushing hands — the partner practice of Tai Chi

脱	tuō	to take off
袜子	wà zi	socks
网站	wǎng zhàn	website — 网 (wǎng, net) + 站 (station): a website, a fixed point on the internet. The everyday word for any site you visit, from a shop to a search engine.
望闻问切	wàng wén wèn qiē	The four examinations , looking (observation of complexion, tongue, posture), listening/smelling (voice, breath), asking (history, symptoms), and touching (pulse diagnosis). The complete TCM diagnostic protocol compressed into four characters.
味道	wèi dào	taste; flavor
温饱	wēn bǎo	warmth and food; subsistence — 温 (wēn, warm) + 饱 (fed): warm enough and fed enough, the baseline of a decent life. 温饱 names the threshold of basic livelihood in economic and political language, as in 解决温饱问题 (jiějué wēnbǎo wèntí, to solve the subsistence problem).
握手	wò shǒu	to shake hands — 握 wò (to grasp) + 手 shǒu (hand). To shake hands , the standard formal greeting gesture in modern Chinese business and official contexts. Traditional Chinese greeting was bowing or clasping one's own hands together (拱手 gǒngshǒu) rather than hand-to-hand contact.
五色	wǔ sè	the five colors — green-blue, red, yellow, white, black — 五 wǔ (five) + 色 sè (color, appearance). The five phase-colors: 青 qīng (green-blue, Wood), 红 hóng (red, Fire), 黄 huáng (yellow, Earth), 白 bái (white, Metal), 黑 hēi (black, Water). These are cosmological categories before they are aesthetic ones.
五味	wǔ wèi	the five flavors — sour, bitter, sweet, pungent, salty — 五 wǔ (five) + 味 wèi (flavor, taste). Sour 酸 suān (Wood/Liver), bitter 苦 kǔ (Fire/Heart), sweet 甘 gān (Earth/Spleen), pungent/spicy 辛 xīn (Metal/Lung), salty 咸 xián (Water/Kidney). Each flavor enters and supports its corresponding organ system in moderate amounts; in excess, it taxes it.
五行	wǔ xíng	the five phases; the five agents — 五 wǔ + 行 xíng (to move; phase; agent). Wood, Fire, Earth, Metal, Water, understood as five phases of cyclical change rather than fixed substances. The generating cycle (相生) runs Wood feeds Fire, Fire makes Earth (ash), Earth bears Metal, Metal carries Water, Water nourishes Wood.
五脏	wǔ zàng	the five yin organ systems — heart, liver, spleen, lung, kidney — 五 wǔ (five) + 脏 zàng (solid, yin, storing organ). In TCM, 五脏 refers to five functional systems, not five anatomical organs in the Western sense. Heart (心 xīn) , Fire; Liver (肝 gān) , Wood; Spleen (脾 pí) , Earth; Lung (肺 fèi) , Metal; Kidney (肾 shèn) , Water.
西医	xī yī	Western medicine
洗脸	xǐ liǎn	to wash one's face — 洗 (to wash) + 脸 (face). The first ritual of the morning. A verb-object pair, so you can split it: 洗个脸 (xǐ ge liǎn), to give the face a quick wash.
瞎	xiā	blind
吓跑	xià pǎo	to scare away; to frighten off — 吓 (xià, to frighten) + 跑. A V+跑 resultative: the action of frightening produces the result of running off. Highly productive pattern: 赶跑 gǎnpǎo (chase off), 撵跑 niǎnpǎo (drive off), 骂跑 màpǎo (scold off). The construction packages cause and escape into one verb.
相克	xiāng kè	mutual conquest; the control cycle — 相 xiāng (mutual) + 克 kè (to conquer, to regulate, to check). In the Five Phases context, 克 means disciplined control rather than destruction , the river bank that contains the flood, not the dam that annihilates the water.
相生	xiāng shēng	mutual generation; the creative cycle — 相 xiāng (mutual, reciprocal) + 生 shēng (to give birth, to generate, to produce). Each phase nourishes and gives rise to the next; each is simultaneously "mother" to the phase it generates and "child" to the phase that generates it.
笑脸	xiào liǎn	smiling face; a smile worn for others — 笑 (laugh, smile) + 脸 (face). A face shaped into a smile, especially the welcoming one you put on for guests or customers. The opposite expression is 板着脸 (bǎnzhe liǎn), to hold the face stiff and stern.
歇脚	xiē jiǎo	to rest one's feet (during a journey); to take a break en route — 歇 xiē (to rest) + 脚. The classic verb for stopping during a long walk or trip , at a tea-house, an inn, a roadside pavilion. Carries the texture of pre-modern travel; still used for any pause on a long journey.
鞋	xié	shoes
心眼	xīn yǎn ( r )	wits; smarts; (also) intent, motive of the heart — 心 xīn (heart) + 眼. The eye of the heart , the inner faculty of judgment and quick-wittedness. 心眼多 xīnyǎn duō "to have many wits" (clever, calculating, or scheming, depending on tone). 心眼好 xīnyǎn hǎo "to have a good heart-eye" (kind-hearted, well-intentioned).
星宿	xīng xiù	the lunar mansions
姓	xìng	surname; clan name
姓名	xìng míng	full name — surname + given name — 姓 xìng (surname) + 名 míng (given name). The formal term for a full name. Note: Chinese names are surname-first, given-name-second, the reverse of Western convention. 你叫什么名字？= "What is your name?" (informal, just the name). 请问您的姓名？= "May I ask your full name?" (formal).
穴位	xu é wèi	acupoint — Acupoint , a specific location on a channel where qi gathers close to the surface and is most accessible to needling or moxa stimulation. Also called 腧穴 shùxué in classical texts. The body has 361 standard points on the 14 main channels.
学院	xu é yuàn	college, institute
血	xu è	blood
眼光	yǎng uāng	vision; taste; judgment; the discerning eye — 眼 yǎn + 光 guāng (light). Literally eye-light. The kind of seeing a person has , their taste in art, their judgment in business, their ability to spot a good investment or a good partner. 有眼光 yǒu yǎnguāng "to have a good eye"; 没眼光 "to have poor judgment"; 长远的眼光 "long-term vision."
眼界	yǎn jiè	horizon (of experience); breadth of one's outlook — 眼 yǎn + 界 jiè (boundary; realm). The boundary of what one's eye has taken in , how widely one has seen, traveled, read, lived. 开阔眼界 kāikuò yǎnjiè "to broaden one's horizons" is one of the standard reasons given in Chinese for travel, study abroad, and reading widely.
眼睛	yǎn jing	eye(s); the eyes as organ — 眼 yǎn + 睛 jīng (eyeball; pupil). The standard everyday word for "eyes" in spoken Chinese. Single 眼 alone almost never serves this role; for "her eyes are beautiful" you say 她的眼睛很美 not 她的眼很美. The 睛 is light and unstressed in pronunciation.
眼泪	yǎn lèi	tears — 眼 yǎn + 泪 lèi (tear). Standard word for tears. 流眼泪 liú yǎnlèi "to shed tears"; 擦眼泪 cā yǎnlèi "to wipe away tears." The classical/literary equivalent is just 泪 alone or 泪水 lèishuǐ.
眼力	yǎn lì	perceptiveness; discerning power; visual acuity — 眼 yǎn + 力 lì (strength). The strength of one's seeing , both literally (sharpness of vision) and figuratively (judgment, ability to spot quality or fakery). The skilled antique-dealer is praised for 眼力 in detecting forgeries.
眼皮	yǎn pí	eyelid — 眼 yǎn + 皮 pí (skin). Eyelid. The colloquial idiom 眼皮底下 yǎnpí dǐxià "right under one's eyelids" means "right in front of one, in plain sight" , usually used to describe something missed or stolen despite being right there.
眼神	yǎn shén	the look in one's eyes; gaze; expression — 眼 yǎn + 神 shén (spirit; expression). Not the eyes themselves but the spirit visible in them , the look one gives, the expression of feeling that the eyes carry. A high-frequency word in fiction and dialogue: 他的眼神很温柔 "the look in his eyes was very gentle"; 用眼神告诉我 "tell me with your eyes."
羊肉	yáng ròu	mutton, lamb — 羊 (yáng, sheep/goat) + 肉 (meat). The meat of choice in the northwest, central to hotpot and skewers.
阳历	yáng lì	the solar (Gregorian) calendar — 阳 yáng (the sun; the yang principle) + 历 lì (calendar). The sun-based calendar, in modern usage the Gregorian calendar used for official and international dates. Also called 公历 gōnglì (the "common," that is internationally standard, calendar).
阳气	yáng qì	yang energy: warm, active vital force — 阳 yáng (active; bright) + 气 qì (vital energy). The warm, rising, animating energy of the body in traditional medicine, set against 阴气 yīnqì. A person low on 阳气 is thought to feel cold and sluggish.
阳虚	yáng xū	yang deficiency — the yang warmth is insufficient; cold dominates — Classic 阳虚 symptoms: aversion to cold (怕冷 pà lěng), cold hands and feet, pale complexion, fatigue and low energy, frequent clear urination, low libido, loose stools.
养生	yǎng shēng	health cultivation; nourishing life
摇头	yáo tóu	to shake the head
药	yào	Medicine — Medicine. 吃药 is taking it, 开药 is prescribing it, 中药 is the herbal kind, and 药店 the pharmacy. The grass radical 艹 on top remembers that medicine began as herbs.
药食同源	yào shí tóng yuán	food and medicine share an origin
一首歌	yī shǒu gē	one song; a song — 一 yī (one) + 首 shǒu (measure word) + 歌 gē (song). 首 is the classifier for songs and poems: 一首歌 a song, 一首诗 a poem, 一首词 a lyric. Each piece is counted as one self-contained "head." 唱一首歌 "to sing a song."
一首诗	yī shǒu shī	one poem; a poem — 一 yī (one) + 首 shǒu (measure word) + 诗 shī (poem). The same classifier counts poems. 写一首诗 "to write a poem," 背一首诗 "to recite a poem from memory." A classical anthology is described as so many 首 of verse.
一头牛	yī tóu niú	one cow; a head of cattle — 一 yī (one) + 头 tóu (measure word) + 牛 niú (cow). Here 头 keeps its full tone as the classifier for large livestock: cattle, pigs, sheep, elephants. You count beasts by the head.
衣服	yī fu	clothes
医生	yī shēng	doctor; physician — 医 yī (medicine; to heal) + 生 shēng (practitioner). The standard word for doctor in mainland China. Note the 生 suffix , it originally meant "one who practices/studies" (compare 学生, 先生). 看医生 = "to see a doctor."
阴气	yīn qì	yin energy; cool, receptive qi
阴虚	yīn xū	yin deficiency — the yin coolant is insufficient; yang heat dominates — 虚 xū = deficient; empty; insufficient. Classic 阴虚 symptoms: heat sensations in the palms and soles of feet (五心烦热), night sweats (盗汗 dào hàn), dry mouth and throat, redness of the cheeks in the afternoon, irritability, insomnia.
阴阳五行	yīn yáng wǔ xíng	yin-yang and the Five Phases — the paired cosmological framework — The two systems developed in parallel during the Warring States period and were synthesized into a unified framework by the Han dynasty, particularly in the 黄帝内经 (Yellow Emperor's Classic, compiled ca. 200 BCE–200 CE).
窨花	xùn huā	scenting — the process of infusing tea with flower fragrance
语气	yǔ qì	tone of voice; grammatical mood; modal register — 语 yǔ (language; speech) + 气 qì. The qi of language, how something is said rather than what. In grammatical analysis, 语气 = mood (indicative, imperative, interrogative).
元气	yuán qì	primordial qi; vitality; the reserves of life force — 元 yuán (origin; primary; first) + 气 qì. In cosmology: the undifferentiated qi before heaven and earth separated, the primordial substrate of existence. In medicine: the constitutional qi inherited from one's parents, stored in the kidneys, spent by illness and restored by rest.
元首	yuán shǒu	head of state
站点	zhàn diǎn	site; node; stop — 站 (station) + 点 (diǎn, point): a designated point or node, whether a physical service point or a website. Broad and slightly technical, common in logistics, networks, and field operations.
站岗	zhàng ǎng	to stand guard — 站 (to stand) + 岗 (gǎng, a post or sentry position): to take up a guard post. A verb-object phrase, literally "to stand a post," used of soldiers, guards, and watchmen holding their station.
站立	zhàn lì	to stand erect — 站 (stand) + 立 (stand): two near-synonyms stacked for emphasis, a written-register word for standing upright. More formal than plain 站, common in instructions, ceremonies, and description.
站起来	zhàn qǐ lái	to stand up — 站 + 起来 (the directional complement "up"): to rise from sitting to standing. The 起来 makes the verb a completed upward motion rather than a static posture.
站台	zhàn tái	platform — 站 (station) + 台 (tái, raised platform): the boarding platform at a station, the raised edge alongside the track. By extension, 站台 also means to publicly back someone, to "stand on the platform" for them.
站长	zhàn zhǎng	station chief; webmaster — 站 (station) + 长 (zhǎng, head, chief): the person in charge of a station. Originally a stationmaster; now also the administrator of a 网站, a webmaster.
针灸	zhēn jiǔ	acupuncture and moxibustion — 针 zhēn (needle) + 灸 jiǔ (moxibustion, burning dried mugwort at acupoints to warm qi). The compound covers both therapies, which are always taught together. Acupuncture inserts fine needles at specific points (穴位 xuéwèi, acupoints) along the meridians to regulate the flow of qi.
针线	zhēn xiàn	needle and thread
针眼	zhēn yǎn	the eye of a needle; (medical) sty — 针 zhēn (needle) + 眼. The hole in a sewing needle , the same metaphor English uses. Also a colloquial term for a sty on the eyelid (the small swelling looking like a tiny eye).
阵脚	zhèn jiǎo	battle-line foundation; one's footing in a contest — 阵 zhèn (battle formation) + 脚. The foot of the battle line , the structural integrity of a position. The common idiom 乱了阵脚 luàn le zhènjiǎo "to lose one's footing" describes the moment a debater, athlete, or general is rattled and the formation collapses.
睁	zhēng	to open (the eyes)
整体	zhěng tǐ	the whole; the entirety; as a whole — 整 zhěng (whole, complete) + 体 tǐ (body). A thing taken as one integrated body rather than as separate parts. Its standing contrast is 部分 bùfen, a part: 整体利益 "the interests of the whole."
正气	zhèng qì	upright qi; righteous energy; moral integrity — 正 zhèng (upright; correct; proper) + 气 qì. In TCM: the body's defensive qi that resists pathogenic invasion. In moral discourse: the upright character and courage that resists corruption and injustice.
治疗	zhì liáo	to treat, therapy
中药	zhōng yào	Chinese herbal medicine — 中 zhōng (Chinese) + 药 yào (medicine; drug). Chinese herbal medicine: the pharmacological branch of TCM. The classical formulary contains thousands of substances: plant-based (ginseng 人参 rénshēn, astragalus 黄芪 huángqí, wolfberry 枸杞 gǒuqǐ), mineral (cinnabar 朱砂 zhūshā), and animal-derived.
终点站	zhōng diǎn zhàn	terminus; last stop — 终点 (zhōngdiǎn, end point) + 站 (stop): the final stop on a route, where the line ends and everyone gets off. Its opposite is 起点站 qǐdiǎnzhàn, the first stop.
猪肉	zhū ròu	pork — 猪 (zhū, pig) + 肉 (meat). The most-eaten meat in China, so common that 肉 alone often means pork by default in recipes.
住院	zhù yuàn	to be hospitalized — 住 (live, stay) + 院 (the hospital courtyard). Separable: 住了三天院 is three days in the hospital. Its exit is 出院 (be discharged), the phone call every family waits for.
注脚	zhù jiǎo	footnote; gloss; annotation — 注 zhù (to annotate; to gloss) + 脚. Annotation at the foot of the page. A more elegant alternative to the modern compound 脚注 jiǎozhù (which has the same components in reverse order).
注目	zhù mù	to fix one's gaze; to give attention to — 注 (to pour into; to concentrate) + 目 (eye). To concentrate the eye on something. A formal register word for focused attention: 引人注目 yǐn rén zhùmù means "eye-catching" or "attention-drawing."
注意	zhù yì	to pay attention; to take note; to watch out — 注 zhù (to pour; to fix) + 意 yì (notion; attention). To pour the mind onto something, that is, to pay attention: 注意安全 "mind your safety," 请注意 "please take note."
字眼	zì yǎn	the key word; the word one fastens on — 字 zì (character; word) + 眼. The eye of the phrase , the single word that carries the weight, that you pivot a sentence on, that an opponent picks at. 抠字眼 kōu zìyǎn "to pick at the wording" (often pejoratively, like English "splitting hairs over phrasing").
自首	zì shǒu	to turn oneself in; to confess to the authorities — 自 zì (oneself) + 首 shǒu (to bow the head, to confess). To give oneself up to the law of one's own accord, before being caught. A legal term: voluntary surrender that often earns a lighter sentence. The head bows itself before justice.
足迹	zú jī	footprint; trace; tracks — 足 (foot) + 迹 (trace, mark). The imprint a foot leaves , both physical footprints and the metaphorical traces left by a life or a civilization. 留下足迹 (to leave one's footprints); 遍布全国的足迹 (footprints spread across the whole country).
足球	zú qiú	football; soccer — 足 (foot) + 球 (ball). The foot-ball: soccer in China and Taiwan. The Chinese Football Association (中国足球协会) governs 足球 nationally.
