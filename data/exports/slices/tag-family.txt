//角落書屋/Slices/Family
夫	fū	husband, a grown man, laborer; the literary particle fú — A grown man pictured with a hairpin marking adulthood: the husband and the married couple (丈夫 夫妻 夫人 夫妇), the adult man and laborer (农夫 渔夫 大夫), skill and effort (功夫 工夫), and the classical literary particle 夫 fú that opens a sentence.
父	fù	father, male elder, household head — A hand gripping a stone axe: the tool of the one who heads the household, drawn as the figure of the father himself.
哥	gē	elder brother, a familiar term for an older young man — Two 可 stacked into one glyph; the doubled phonetic came to carry the warmest word in the family, elder brother.
孩	hái	baby, child, children — A child-radical fused with the earthly branch 亥: 子 carries the meaning, 亥 lends the rising sound hái.
家	jiā	home, family — House, family, school of thought, nation (国家): the fractal of belonging.
姐	jiě	elder sister, miss, young lady — A woman radical fused to the sound-lender 且, 姐 names the elder sister and stretches to cover every miss and young lady in modern address.
老	lǎo	old, venerable, experienced — A stooped elder with long hair: age as wisdom, seniority as moral authority, and the Daoist sage who wrote the Daodejing.
女	nǚ	woman, female — A kneeling figure with crossed arms: one of the most productive radicals in the script, present in characters for family relationships, feelings, and cultural concepts.
外	wài	outside, outer, foreign, besides — A diviner reading the shells at the wrong hour, off the normal schedule. From that image grows everything beyond the boundary: the outside, the outer surface, the foreign land, and the relatives on the mother's side.
喜	xǐ	joy, to like, happy events — A festive drum sounding above an open mouth: the sound of joy and the smiling mouth that answers it. From this image grow the words for liking, delight, and every happy occasion, above all the wedding.
长	cháng	long; to grow; chief — A person with flowing long hair. One character, two readings: cháng (long) and zhǎng (to grow, elder), the richest dual-reading in Mandarin.
子	zǐ	child, seed, Master — A picture of a swaddled infant that grew into one of the busiest characters in the language: a child and a son, the honorific for a Master, the empty noun-suffix on a thousand words, and the seed of a thing.
春节	Chūn jié	Spring Festival — The most important holiday in the Chinese calendar: customs, foods, taboos, the reunion dinner, and the cultural weight of the Spring Festival.
孝	xiào	filial piety — An elder supported by a child's hands: the cardinal Confucian virtue that placed care for parents at the foundation of all moral life.
祖先	zǔ xiān	ancestors; forebears — 祖先 sits at the center of Chinese religious life: ancestor veneration is a living practice through which families maintain active, reciprocal continuity with the dead.
成家立业	chéng jiā lì yè	start a family and establish a career — the two milestones of adult life — 立业 = to establish one's career or livelihood. The traditional sequence of adult life goals: first you build a home; then you build a livelihood. 他终于成家立业了。(He has finally started a family and established himself.) The phrase encodes a whole theory of the proper human life cycle.
大哥别说二哥	dà gē bié shuō èr gē	the eldest shouldn't mock the second: pot calling the kettle black — A proverb on shared flaws: 大哥 and 二哥 are equally at fault, so neither has standing to criticize the other. The Mandarin take on glass houses.
大姐大	dà jiě dà	top sister: a commanding, dominant woman — A colloquial trigram, not a classical idiom, for the female leader of a group or a forceful, authoritative woman. Carries a half-admiring, half-wary tone.
夫唱妇随	fū chàng fù suí	the husband sings and the wife follows: a harmonious married couple — Literally "the husband leads, the wife accompanies." A traditional image of marital harmony in which the couple act in accord, one taking the lead and the other in step.
父慈子孝	fù cí zǐ xiào	the father kind, the son filial: the reciprocal duty between generations — A Confucian formula for the father-son relationship, paired in the classics with 兄友弟恭 (elder brother caring, younger respectful). Authority and obedience are framed as mutual obligations, not one-way command.
父债子还	fù zhài zǐ huán	the father's debt, the son repays: a debt or wrong passes down the line — Once a literal expectation in family law, now used figuratively for any burden inherited from a previous generation. Carries a tone of grim inevitability.
哥俩好	gē liǎ hǎo	the two of us are tight: a toast and a drinking-game call — A finger-guessing drinking call and a stock phrase for two close male friends. 俩 (liǎ) is "the two of us," so the whole thing means "we two brothers are solid."
光宗耀祖	guāng zōng yào zǔ	bring glory to the clan and illuminate the ancestors — 光 (illuminate) + 宗 (clan lineage) + 耀 (cause to shine) + 祖 (ancestors). The foundational aspiration of Chinese family-oriented ambition: achievement is owed to and shared with those who came before. Used sincerely in formal contexts; with ironic distance among younger generations.
家徒四壁	jiā tú sì bì	home with only four walls — extreme poverty; owning nothing — Lit: home-merely-four-walls. 徒 = only; merely; bare. The household contains nothing but the four walls themselves. Used in literature to describe destitution. Often contrasted with 家财万贯 jiācái wànguàn (immense family wealth). A stock phrase for total financial ruin.
家喻户晓	jiā yù hù xiǎo	known to every household — a household name; universally known — Lit: family-understood-household-known. 喻 = understood; 晓 = known; aware. Every family understands it; every household knows it. 这首歌已经家喻户晓了。(This song is now known to everyone.) The go-to phrase for "universally famous" in Chinese.
皆大欢喜	jiē dà huān xǐ	all are greatly delighted — everyone ends up happy — Originally the closing formula of Buddhist sutras, where the assembly rejoices at the teaching and departs; now the phrase for win-win endings, amicable settlements, and comedies. It is also the standard Chinese title of Shakespeare's As You Like It, a translation with better provenance than most.
姐妹情深	jiě mèi qíng shēn	deep sisterly affection — A common modern four-character set phrase for the strong bond between sisters or close female friends. Frequent in titles, toasts, and warm descriptions of female friendship.
巾帼英雄	jīn guó yīng xióng	heroine — a woman of exceptional courage and achievement — Lit: kerchief-hood hero. 巾帼 jīnguó is the traditional women's headdress, used by extension to mean women collectively. A 巾帼英雄 is a female hero, a woman who has achieved what is extraordinary even by the standards of men. Hua Mulan 花木兰 is the archetype. Used to praise exceptional women in any field.
敬祖尊宗	jìng zǔ zūn zōng	to revere ancestors and honor the lineage — A set phrase expressing the core ethic of ancestor veneration: to show reverence (敬 jìng) to direct ancestors (祖) and to honor the lineage (宗) as a whole. Used in ritual speeches, genealogy prefaces, and New Year ancestral hall ceremonies.
君子之交	jūn zǐ zhī jiāo	the friendship of noble people: plain as water, lasting and unselfish — Lit: noble-person's friendship. From the saying 君子之交淡如水, "the friendship of the noble is bland as water," meaning a true bond needs no flattery or gifts and so never sours.
老当益壮	lǎo dāng yì zhuàng	old but growing ever stronger — vigorous in old age — Lit: old-yet-increasingly-vigorous. From the Book of the Later Han: the general Ma Yuan said, "A man of ambition grows more vigorous as he grows old; a hero grows stronger with age." Used to praise an older person who retains energy, capability, and drive. A compliment, never ironic.
老马识途	lǎo mǎ shí tú	an old horse knows the road — experience guides better than intelligence — From the Han Feizi: Duke Huan of Qi was lost in the mountains; an advisor suggested releasing an old war horse, which led the army home by the familiar road. Used to justify consulting experienced people rather than relying on theory or intelligence alone. One of the most famous 老 idioms.
里应外合	lǐ yīng wài hé	"inside responds, outside coordinates" — inside-outside cooperation; a coordinated attack from within and without — 里 (inside) + 应 (yīng, to respond, to correspond) + 外 (outside) + 合 (to coordinate, to join). A military and strategic idiom: the force inside a city or position responds to the force attacking from outside, each coordinating with the other.
列祖列宗	liè zǔ liè zōng	all the ancestors of the line, generation upon generation — 列 (arrayed, successive) + 祖 + 列 + 宗. A formulaic invocation of the entire ancestral chain, used in formal toasts, funeral eulogies, and solemn declarations where one speaks on behalf of the whole family line. The repetition of 列 creates a sense of unbroken succession stretching backward.
内外有别	nèi wài yǒu bié	inside and outside are distinct: insiders and outsiders are treated differently — Lit: inside-outside-have-distinction. The principle that there is a proper line between those within a family, group, or nation and those without, and that the two are not handled the same way.
匹夫之勇	pǐ fū zhī yǒng	the courage of a common man: reckless bravery without strategy — 匹夫 pǐfū is an ordinary fellow of no rank. The phrase names the brute, headlong courage of one who rushes in to fight without thought or plan, contrasted with the cool, calculated valor of a true leader. It is a gentle put-down: brave, but foolishly so.
千夫所指	qiān fū suǒ zhǐ	pointed at by a thousand men: condemned by everyone — Literally "what a thousand men point their fingers at." It describes a person reviled by the whole public, the target of universal blame and reproach. To be 千夫所指 is to stand utterly condemned in the eyes of the crowd, a heavy figure for public disgrace.
取长补短	qǔ cháng bǔ duǎn	take strengths to補 compensate weaknesses — learn from others — Lit: take-long-补-short. The standard phrase for learning from others to compensate for one's own deficiencies. 互相取长补短 "learn from each other's strengths to make up for each other's weaknesses" is the common collaborative formulation.
认贼作父	rèn zéi zuò fù	to take a thief for a father: to serve one's enemy as a benefactor — A harsh condemnation, aimed at a traitor or collaborator who submits to the very people who wronged him. The image inverts the sacred father-son bond into betrayal.
认祖归宗	rèn zǔ guī zōng	to acknowledge one's ancestors and return to the lineage — Lit: recognize-ancestor-return-lineage. Used for those who were separated from their family (by adoption, diaspora, or historical disruption) and formally reconnect with their ancestral line. A profoundly significant act in Chinese social life.
慎终追远	shèn zhōng zhuī yuǎn	careful attention to death rites and honoring distant ancestors — From the Analects (论语): 慎终追远，民德归厚矣. "When the people are careful about death rites and diligently honor distant ancestors, the virtue of the people will return to richness." Confucius's most concise statement of why ancestor veneration matters: it cultivates moral seriousness in the living.
数典忘祖	shǔ diǎn wàng zǔ	recite the classics but forget your ancestors — From the Zuozhuan: a minister who could cite all historical precedents of his state but could not name his own ancestral lineage was mocked with this phrase. Used for ingratitude, forgetting one's origins, or adopting foreign ways at the expense of one's own heritage.
四海为家	sì hǎi wéi jiā	to make one's home in all four seas — at home anywhere; cosmopolitan; rootless wandering — 四海 = the four seas = everywhere under heaven. 为 = to treat as; to make into. The whole globe is one's household. Used for travelers, exiles, and those rootlessly at ease anywhere. Both admiring (cosmopolitan freedom) and elegiac (loss of fixed roots) , depending on context.
桃李满天下	táo lǐ mǎn tiān xià	peaches and plums fill the world: a teacher's students are everywhere — Lit: peach-plum-fill-the-world. 桃李 (peach and plum trees) stand for a teacher's 弟子, the disciples cultivated over a lifetime. To say someone's 桃李满天下 is high praise for a great educator.
天之骄子	tiān zhī jiāo zǐ	a favored child of Heaven: one singularly blessed by fortune — Lit: Heaven's-spoiled-child. Originally said of the Xiongnu nomads who boasted Heaven's favor; now used of anyone who seems destined for success, sometimes with a touch of envy.
铁哥们儿	tiě gē men r	iron buddies: rock-solid male friends — 铁 (iron) intensifies 哥们儿. The metaphor is metal: a friendship that won't bend or rust. Reserved for the few friends you would drop everything for.
望女成凤	wàng nǚ chéng fèng	hoping your daughter will become a phoenix — parental ambition for daughters — The female counterpart to 望子成龙 wàng zǐ chéng lóng (hoping your son will become a dragon). 凤 fèng (phoenix) is the female auspicious creature paired with 龙 (dragon). The phoenix (凤凰 fènghuáng) represents feminine grace, beauty, and virtue.
望子成龙	wàng zǐ chéng lóng	to hope ones son becomes a dragon: to long for ones child to succeed greatly — Literally "to hope the son becomes a dragon," the dragon being the emblem of supreme success and distinction. The classic expression of parental ambition: the deep hope that ones child will rise high in life. A close partner phrase, 望女成凤, hopes the daughter becomes a phoenix.
喜出望外	xǐ chū wàng wài	joy beyond what one hoped — overjoyed by surprise — 喜 (delight) arriving from outside (外) the range of expectation (望). The precise word for good news you did not dare hope for: the scholarship, the recovered wallet, the friend at the airport gate. Stronger and more specific than 很高兴.
喜气洋洋	xǐ qì yáng yáng	brimming with joy: radiant with a festive, happy spirit — Lit: festive air spilling over. 洋洋 paints joy overflowing in every direction. Said of a face, a household, or a whole town lit up with celebration, a stock phrase at weddings and the New Year.
喜闻乐见	xǐ wén lè jiàn	glad to hear and pleased to see — popular and well-liked — Describes something the public genuinely enjoys and welcomes, literally "glad to hear it and happy to see it." 喜 (glad) and 乐 lè (happy) pair with 闻 (hear) and 见 (see). Often used of art, entertainment, or activities that are widely and warmly received.
香火	xiāng huo	incense smoke; the vitality of a temple's active veneration — Literally "incense fire" or "incense smoke," but 香火 carries meanings beyond the physical act of burning. A temple with 香火鼎盛 xiānghuo dǐngshèng (incense burning vigorously) is a temple with a healthy, active congregation, good reputation, and real spiritual efficacy.
小孩没娘，说来话长	xiǎo hái méi niáng , shuō lái huà cháng	the child has no mother: it's a long story — A 歇后语 (xiēhòuyǔ) two-part saying. The literal image, a motherless child, sets up the punchline 说来话长, "it's a long story." Spoken as a preface when the backstory is tangled and the speaker hardly knows where to begin.
小孩子家	xiǎo hái zi jiā	just a kid, only a child — Colloquial set phrase. The trailing 家 marks a category of person; said dismissively or fondly of someone too young to know better. "你一个小孩子家懂什么。"
小孩子气	xiǎo hái zi qì	childish, immature in manner — Describes adult behavior that is petty or babyish. The 气 (qì) suffix turns a noun into a temperament. Everyday spoken register: "他这样太小孩子气了。"
兄弟姐妹	xiōng dì jiě mèi	brothers and sisters; siblings — The full set of siblings named in birth-order pairs: 兄 (elder brother), 弟 (younger brother), 姐 (elder sister), 妹 (younger sister). A staple four-character phrase, and one of the few common ones to carry 姐 directly.
一夫当关	yī fū dāng guān	one man guards the pass: a position so strong that a single defender holds off an army — From the line about the road to Shu, 一夫当关，万夫莫开, "one man holding the pass, ten thousand cannot force it open." It praises a place of such natural strength that a lone defender can block a whole host, and by extension any well-held strategic point or a person stubbornly guarding a position.
倚老卖老	yǐ lǎo mài lǎo	leaning on age to trade on age — exploiting seniority — Lit: lean-on-old-sell-old. The critical idiom for elders who demand deference they have not earned, throwing their age around as a trump card to win arguments or avoid accountability.
长此以往	cháng cǐ yǐ wǎng	if this goes on for long — a warning about sustained bad behavior — Lit: long-this-by-going. Used to warn that if a current (usually bad) situation continues indefinitely, serious consequences will follow. 长此以往，身体会垮掉的 "If this keeps up, your health will collapse." Almost always negative; rarely used for positive sustained trends.
长风破浪	cháng fēng pò làng	a long wind breaking through the waves — bold ambition and perseverance — From the Tang poet Li Bai: 长风破浪会有时，直挂云帆济沧海. "There will come a time when the long wind breaks through the waves; I shall set my cloud-sail straight across the vast sea." The image is unstoppable ambition riding the great winds, no obstacle too large to overcome.
置身事外	zhì shēn shì wài	to place oneself outside the matter: to stay aloof, keep clear of it — Lit: place-body-affair-outside. To keep oneself out of an affair, neither helping nor interfering. Often mildly critical, of someone who stands apart when they ought to be involved.
追本溯源	zhuī běn sù yuán	trace back to the root and seek the source — 追 (pursue) + 本 (root) + 溯 (go upstream) + 源 (source). Used for rigorous investigation of origins: of a word, a practice, a family line, a historical claim. The metaphor of going upstream to find the source of a river runs through classical Chinese thought about genealogy and textual scholarship.
子承父业	zǐ chéng fù yè	the son carries on the father's trade: inheriting the family calling — Said when a child takes up the parent's profession or business, from a craftsman's shop to a family firm. Neutral to approving, depending on whether the inheritance is seen as continuity or constraint.
阿姨	ā yí	aunt; older woman — Auntie , used to address unfamiliar middle-aged women in informal contexts: cleaning staff, market vendors, neighbors. More personal than 女士 and warmer than nothing.
爸爸	bà ba	dad (colloquial)
百日	bǎi rì	The hundred-day mourning period following a death — The hundred-day mourning period following a death. During this time the bereaved family observes restrictions on celebration and festive activity. A commemoration ceremony at the end of the hundred days formally closes the mourning period.
悲	bēi	sorrow; grief
鞭炮	biān pào	firecrackers
表哥	biǎo gē	older male cousin — 表 (maternal/external) + 哥. An older male cousin on the mother's side, or through a paternal aunt. The 表 prefix marks cousins outside the father's patriline, distinct from 堂哥 (tánggē).
表姐	biǎo jiě	older female cousin
不孝	bù xiào	unfilial
部长	bù zhǎng	minister; head of a department — 部 bù (ministry) + 长 zhǎng (chief; head). The head of a ministry or department: 外交部长 "the Foreign Minister." The 长 here is the leader-sense, read zhǎng, not the cháng of "long."
彩礼	cǎi lǐ	Betrothal gifts , the payment from the groom's family to the bride's family as part of the formal engagement. In contemporary China, the cash component (also called 彩礼钱) has become a source of social tension in areas where amounts have escalated sharply.
插茱萸	chā zhū yú	wearing zhuyu sprigs — apotropaic plant, festival shorthand — 插 chā (to insert; to wear tucked in) + 茱萸 zhūyú (Cornus officinalis; Japanese cornelian cherry). The zhuyu shrub, with its fragrant berries and pungent scent, was believed to repel evil and disease. On Double Ninth, people wore sprigs tucked into their hair or carried them in small pouches.
嫦娥	Cháng é	Chang'e — the Moon Goddess
超度	chāo dù	to deliver souls (Buddhist ritual)
称谓	chēng wèi	Form of address, title, mode of address , the technical term for the entire system of address terms. 亲属称谓 (qīnshǔ chēngwèi) refers specifically to kinship terms. Understanding 称谓 is considered a basic social competency.
成家	chéng jiā	to start a family; to marry and establish a household — 成 chéng (to become; to accomplish; to complete) + 家 jiā. The moment a household comes into being, through marriage and setting up a home together. One of the defining life-stage transitions in Chinese culture, paired with 立业 (establish a career) in the famous set phrase 成家立业.
成长	chéng zhǎng	to grow up; to mature; personal development — 成 chéng (to become; to complete) + 长 zhǎng (to grow). The compound for the full arc of development: physical, psychological, and social maturation. 成长故事 = coming-of-age story. 成长经历 = formative experiences. Also used for adult development: 这段经历让我很成长 "this experience helped me grow a lot."
除夕	Chú xī	New Year's Eve
祠堂	cí táng	ancestral hall — the lineage's center of gravity — The 祠堂 is architecturally distinct from both domestic and religious buildings. Typically facing south, with a courtyard (天井 tiānjǐng), successive halls of increasing sanctity, and a rear hall housing the ranked spirit tablets, the building's layout enacts a cosmological and social hierarchy simultan…
此外	cǐ wài	apart from this; besides; furthermore — 此 cǐ (this) + 外 wài (outside). A formal written connector, "outside of this," used to add a further point: 此外，还有一点需要注意 "besides, there is one more point to note."
大夫	dài fu	doctor; physician (colloquial) — 大 dà + 夫 fū, but in this sense read dàifu with a neutral tone. A common, slightly informal word for a doctor, especially in northern speech: 看大夫 to see a doctor, 张大夫 Dr. Zhang. Read 大夫 dàfū instead and the same characters name a senior official of the old imperial court, a different word entirely.
大哥	dà gē	eldest brother; big bro — 大 (big) + 哥. Literally the eldest brother in the family. By extension it addresses the leader of a group, the boss of a gang, or any older man you want to flatter into helping you.
大哥大	dà gē dà	brick phone; top boss — 大哥 (big brother) + 大 again. First a nickname for the 1980s brick mobile phone, the status symbol of bosses; by extension the kingpin or undisputed number one in any field.
大家	dà jiā	everyone; everybody (warm, inclusive) — 大 dà (big) + 家 jiā (family; home). Literally "the big family," used for "everyone present" or "all of us." Much warmer and more conversational than 人们. The go-to word for addressing or including a present group: 大家好！(Hello everyone!).
大姐	dà jiě	eldest sister; ma'am — 大 (big) plus 姐 marks the eldest of several sisters at home. On the street it doubles as a friendly, respectful way to address a woman somewhat older than the speaker.
大人	dà rén	adult, grown-up
道德经	dào dé jīng	the Daodejing
登高	dēng gāo	to climb to high ground; to ascend — 登 dēng (to climb; to ascend; to mount) + 高 gāo (high; tall; elevated). The defining physical act of the festival. As a compound, 登高 appears broadly in Chinese outside the festival context , it can describe any ascent, or metaphorically, advancing in rank or achievement.
弟弟	dì di	younger brother
弟子	dì zǐ	disciple; student of a master — 弟 dì (younger brother) + 子 zǐ. The pupil who studies under a teacher and carries the lineage forward, used of philosophical, martial, and religious schools alike.
独生子女	dú shēng zǐn ǚ	Only child (lit — Only child (lit. "alone-born child-female" , zǐnǚ covers both sexes). The defining identity category for the urban Chinese generation born 1980–2015 under the one-child policy. 独生子 (dúshēng zǐ) is a son; 独生女 (dúshēng nǚ) is a daughter.
端午节	duān wǔ jié	Dragon Boat Festival — Dragon Boat Festival , celebrated on the fifth day of the fifth lunar month. Commemorates Qu Yuan's drowning in the Miluo River. Marked by dragon boat races and the eating of 粽子 zòngzi (sticky rice dumplings wrapped in bamboo leaves).
对联	duì lián	Spring Festival couplets
儿童	ér tóng	children (formal)
儿子	ér zi	son — 儿 ér (child, son) + 子 zi (suffix). The standard word for a son. Its pair is 女儿 nǚ'ér (daughter), which keeps 儿 but not 子.
二哥	èr gē	second eldest brother — 二 (two) + 哥. The second of the brothers, ranked after 大哥. The numbering system is literal: each older brother is addressed by his birth order, never by name within the family.
房子	fáng zi	house, building — 房 (building) plus the neutral-tone noun suffix 子 (zi). The default everyday word for a house or any standalone building you live in. Concrete and countable: you buy one, rent one, or build one.
风水择地	fēng shuǐ zé dì	geomantic grave siting — fortune for the living through the dead — The location and orientation of a grave (阴宅 yīnzhái, "yin dwelling") directly affects the fortune of the descendants, a doctrine that made grave geomancy (阴宅风水 yīnzhái fēngshuǐ) one of the most important applications of feng shui.
夫妇	fū fù	a married couple; husband and wife (formal) — 夫 fū (husband) + 妇 fù (married woman). A married couple, close in meaning to 夫妻 but more formal and bookish. Often attached to a surname: 张氏夫妇 the Zhang couple, 新婚夫妇 newlyweds. Where 夫妻 feels everyday and warm, 夫妇 reads as written or ceremonious.
夫妻	fū qī	husband and wife — the household foundation — The marital relationship orders the household 家 jiā, which is the structural unit between the individual and the state. The husband is obligated to provide and to lead with moral example; the wife is obligated to manage the inner household and to support the family's moral life.
夫人	fū rén	madam; lady; another man's wife (polite) — 夫 fū (man) + 人 rén (person). A respectful term for a married woman, especially the wife of a person of standing: 总统夫人 the first lady, 王夫人 Madam Wang. It honors the woman through her husband's status, so it is formal rather than intimate; you would not call your own wife 夫人 in casual speech.
父辈	fù bèi	the fathers' generation — 父 fù (father) + 辈 bèi (generation, cohort). The fathers' generation taken as a class, everyone of the parents' age and above. Set against 子辈 zǐbèi, the children's generation, when speaking of how a family changes across the line.
父母	fù mǔ	parents (father and mother) — 父 fù (father) + 母 mǔ (mother). The standard word for parents as a pair. Father comes first by convention, the same ordering that runs through the whole kinship vocabulary.

父子	fù zǐ	parent and child — the root relationship; filial piety — The foundational relationship , described by Confucians as the root from which all other social virtues grow. The child's obligation is 孝 xiào (filial piety): obedience, care in old age, ritual mourning at death, and continuation of the family line.

哥们儿	gē men r	buddy, bro, mate — 哥 + 们 (plural) + the Beijing 儿 ending. Close male friends, sworn-brother style. Singular or plural, it marks loyalty between guys; northern and casual, common in Beijing speech.

工夫	gōng fu	time and effort; trouble spent on a task — 工 gōng (work, labor) + 夫 fū (effort). The time and care put into doing something: 花工夫 to spend effort, 没工夫 to have no time. It measures the investment a task demands rather than the skill it yields, the cost side of mastery.
恭喜	gōng xǐ	to congratulate; congratulations — 恭 gōng (respectful) + 喜 xǐ (joy). To respectfully share in someone's happiness. Doubled as 恭喜恭喜 it is the warm spoken "congratulations," and 恭喜发财 gōngxǐ fācái is the standard New Year greeting wishing wealth.

故乡	gù xiāng	hometown; native place; homeland — 故 (old, former) + 乡 (xiāng, countryside, native village). The place one is from, charged with the weight of belonging and longing. 故乡 is the literary and emotional register; the plainer 家乡 (jiāxiāng) is more neutral and everyday.
棺材	guān cai	coffin — the most important object in traditional death culture — The coffin (棺材 guāncai) was traditionally a major family investment: solid wood, red lacquered, with auspicious carvings. The quality of the coffin reflected both the family's means and its filial devotion.

画家	huà jiā	painter; visual artist — 画 huà (to paint; to draw; a painting) + 家 jiā. One who has made painting their home. The 家 suffix elevates the painter from a craftsperson to a master, implying sustained creative commitment and a body of work rather than mere technical skill.
欢喜	huān xǐ	happy; joyful; delighted — 欢 huān (merry) + 喜 xǐ (glad). A warm, somewhat colloquial word for being happy and pleased, common in the south and in the phrase 欢欢喜喜 "all merry and glad," and in 皆大欢喜 "everyone happy."
回家	huí jiā	to go home; to return home — 回 huí (to return) + 家 jiā (home). One of the most common V+location phrases in the language. The directional verb 回 indicates movement back toward an origin point: home as the natural destination of return, not merely a place you happen to go.
婚礼	hūn lǐ	wedding ceremony — 婚 hūn (marriage) + 礼 lǐ (rite; ceremony). Wedding as ritual. The 礼 suffix creates ceremony words for life's great transitions: 婚礼 wedding · 葬礼 zànglǐ funeral · 成人礼 chéngrénlǐ coming-of-age ceremony · 开幕礼 kāimùlǐ opening ceremony.
婚姻	hūn yīn	marriage; matrimony — 婚 hūn (marriage, 女 woman + 昏 dusk, from the tradition of evening weddings) + 姻 yīn (marriage-alliance; matrimony, 女 woman + 因 cause/connection). The formal compound for marriage. 婚礼 hūnlǐ = wedding ceremony. 结婚 jiéhūn = to get married. 离婚 líhūn = to divorce.

祭祖	jì zǔ	ancestral offerings — the core ritual — 祭祖 (lit. "offering to ancestors") is the ritual presentation of food, incense, and goods to deceased family members. The offering table holds cooked dishes (often the ancestor's favorite foods in life), rice, fruit, and wine. Incense is lit and prostrations (拜 bài) are made.
佳节	jiā jié	auspicious festival; festive holiday — literary register — 佳 jiā (excellent; fine; auspicious) + 节 jié (festival; season; node). A slightly elevated term for a holiday or festival occasion, preferred in literary and formal contexts over the more neutral 节日 (jiérì).
家常	jiā cháng	everyday home life; homestyle; ordinary — 家 jiā (home) + 常 cháng (ordinary; often; constant). The routine of domestic life: meals, small talk, everyday rhythms. A warmly neutral word that positions the homely and ordinary as valuable rather than inferior.
家风	jiā fēng	family tradition; values passed through generations — 家 jiā (family) + 风 fēng (wind; custom; style; ethos). The invisible ethos that blows through a family across generations: the unspoken values, behavioral norms, and character traits transmitted from parents to children.
家规	jiā guī	family rules; household rules — 家 jiā (family) + 规 guī (rules; regulations; norms). The explicit code of conduct of a household, the spoken version of what 家风 transmits implicitly. Distinguished families in imperial China often had written 家规 codifying expectations for children's conduct, study, and marriage.

家庭	jiā tíng	Family; household — Family; household. The nuclear unit of parents and children who share a home. 家庭作业 (jiātíng zuòyè, homework , lit. 家乡	jiā xiāng	hometown; native place — 家 jiā + 乡 xiāng (countryside; native place, the rural origin point). The place where 家 was first made. A place of origin rather than merely an address: family roots, ancestral connections, formative landscape. Central to Chinese identity, deeply tied to belonging and longing.

家族	jiā zú	Clan; extended family network; lineage group sharing a surname. 家族企业 (jiāzú qǐyè) is a family business , often meaning a business controlled across generations by a single clan. The word implies shared ancestry, shared surname, and historically shared clan property and ancestral hall (祠堂 cítáng).
嫁妆	jià zhuang	Dowry , the goods the bride's family sends with her to the new home. Traditionally included furniture, bedding, household items, and valuable objects. The dowry and betrothal gifts are understood as a two-way transfer that balances the exchange between families.
饺子	jiǎo zi	dumplings — the northern New Year food — The quintessential northern Chinese New Year food, eaten at midnight on New Year's Eve in many northern households. The shape resembles the 元宝 yuánbǎo (ancient sycee gold ingots), making them symbols of wealth.
姐夫	jiě fu	elder sister's husband; brother-in-law — 姐 (elder sister) plus 夫 (husband) names the man married to one's older sister. Chinese kinship marks the relation precisely, distinct from 妹夫 (mèifu), a younger sister's husband.
姐姐	jiě jie	elder sister — The doubled character is the everyday word for one's older sister. The second 姐 takes a neutral tone, giving the warm, soft cadence Chinese kinship terms favor.
姐妹	jiě mèi	sisters; female friends — 姐 (elder sister) plus 妹 (younger sister) yokes the two into a single word for "sisters" as a pair or group, and by extension a tight circle of close female friends.
经验	jīng yàn	experience (accumulated know-how) — 经 jīng (pass through) + 验 yàn (to test; verify). The practical know-how gathered by having passed through and tested much: 工作经验 "work experience." Distinct from 经历, which is the events themselves rather than the lessons drawn from them.
惊喜	jīng xǐ	a pleasant surprise; to be pleasantly surprised — 惊 jīng (startled) + 喜 xǐ (joy). The happy jolt of an unexpected good thing: 一个惊喜 "a surprise (gift or event)," 又惊又喜 "both startled and delighted."
九九	jiǔ jiǔ	double nine — colloquial shorthand; homophonic longevity wish — 九 jiǔ (nine) + 九 jiǔ (nine). The colloquial compressed name for the festival, and a sound-based wish: 九九 is homophonous with 久久 (jiǔ jiǔ, for a long time; lasting), turning the calendar date into an expression of the wish for long life.
菊花	jú huā	chrysanthemum — the flower of the ninth month — 菊 jú (chrysanthemum) + 花 huā (flower; blossom). The chrysanthemum blooms in the ninth and tenth lunar months, when most flowering plants have finished. This timing made it a literary symbol of resilience, late-life achievement, and the refusal to conform to expected seasonal rhythms.
科学家	kē xu é jiā	scientist — 科学 kēxué (science, lit. "the study of branches/categories") + 家 jiā. One who has made science their home.
磕头	kē tóu	prostration — kowtow — the physical language of reverence — 磕头 (kowtow, lit. "knock head") is the full prostration: kneeling and touching the forehead to the floor.
空姐	kōng jiě	flight attendant (female) — 空 (sky, air) plus 姐 is the colloquial word for a female flight attendant, literally a "sky sister." It is a clipped, affectionate everyday term beside the formal 空中小姐 (kōngzhōng xiǎojiě).
快乐	kuài lè	happy; joyful; happiness (as a state) — 快 kuài (released; unobstructed) + 乐 lè (music; joy; ease). The standard word for happiness in modern Mandarin: functions as an adjective predicate, a pre-noun modifier, and a noun. Appears in greetings and fixed phrases far more than the simple 快 alone.
老百姓	lǎo bǎi xìng	the common people; ordinary citizens — 老 (old/established) + 百 (hundred) + 姓 (surname; clan). Lit. "the hundred old clans": the established common families as opposed to the aristocracy. The standard term for ordinary people, used across the political spectrum.
老板	lǎo bǎn	boss; business owner — 老 lǎo + 板 bǎn (plank; board, extended to the person in charge). The everyday term for boss, owner, or the person in charge of a business. In restaurants, calling out 老板！is the standard way to get the owner's attention.

老师	lǎo shī	teacher — the most common honorific in Chinese education — 老 lǎo (venerable) + 师 shī (master; teacher; expert). The standard term for teacher at all levels, from elementary school through university. Used as a title of address (王老师 "Teacher Wang") and as a term of respect for anyone with expertise to transmit.

孝心	xiào xīn	filial intention; filial love
校长	xiào zhǎng	school principal; university president — 校 xiào (school) + 长 zhǎng (elder; head; chief). The head of a school at any level, from primary school principal to university president. The pattern [institution] + 长 = head of that institution is extraordinarily productive: 院长 (dean), 部长 (minister), 市长 (mayor), 省长 (governor), 班长 (class monitor).
新年快乐	xīn nián kuài lè	Happy New Year , literally "new year happy — Happy New Year , literally "new year happy." The standard greeting for the solar New Year (January 1). For Lunar New Year, 新春快乐 (xīnchūn kuàilè) or 恭喜发财 (gōngxǐ fācái, "congratulations and may you prosper") are more appropriate.
姓	xìng	surname; clan name
姓名	xìng míng	full name — surname + given name — 姓 xìng (surname) + 名 míng (given name). The formal term for a full name. Note: Chinese names are surname-first, given-name-second, the reverse of Western convention. 你叫什么名字？= "What is your name?" (informal, just the name). 请问您的姓名？= "May I ask your full name?" (formal).
幸福	xìng fú	happiness; well-being; blessed fortune — 幸 xìng (fortunate; lucky; to survive) + 福 fú (blessing). The compound for subjective happiness and well-being , what you feel when life is going right. 幸福感 = sense of well-being. 幸福生活 = a happy life. One of the most searched Chinese words globally.
兄	xiōng	elder brother (literary)
兄弟	xiōng dì	elder and younger sibling — fraternal respect — 悌 tì , fraternal respect , is the virtue governing sibling relations. The elder brother owes kindness and example; the younger owes deference and respect.
血脉	xu è mài	bloodline
压岁钱	yā suì qián	lucky money for children at New Year — New Year's money, given in red envelopes; literally "money that suppresses the year-beast." The year-beast (岁 suì) was a mythological monster; the money and noise were protective against it.
养老	yǎng lǎo	elder care
样子	yàng zi	appearance; look; manner; the way it seems — 样 yàng (appearance) + 子 zi (noun-suffix). The look or manner of a person or thing. The idiom 看样子 kàn yàngzi means "from the looks of it." For the toneless suffix 子, see the 子 entry.
爷爷	yé ye	grandpa (colloquial)
音乐家	yīn yu è jiā	musician (mastery level); composer — 音乐 yīnyuè (music) + 家 jiā. Implies serious, dedicated musical mastery: a composer, a concert performer, a conductor rather than a casual musician. Distinguished from 歌手 gēshǒu (singer/pop artist) by the 家 suffix, which implies depth, discipline, and classical standing.
婴儿	yīng ér	infant, baby
忧	yōu	worry; anxiety
渔夫	yú fū	fisherman — 渔 yú (to fish) + 夫 fū (man). A man who makes his living by fishing. Like 农夫 it has a timeless, story-book ring, and it features in the famous proverb about the fisherman who profits while the snipe and the clam are locked in struggle.
玉兔	yù tù	the Jade Rabbit
元宵节	Yuán xiāo Jié	Lantern Festival
月饼	yu è bǐng	mooncake — 月 yuè + 饼 bǐng (flat cake; pastry). The round cake eaten during Mid-Autumn Festival, shaped to mirror the full moon. Traditional fillings include lotus seed paste and salted egg yolk (representing the moon).
张总	zhāng zǒng	Director/CEO Zhang , the surname + 总 form is among the most useful professional address terms. 总 is short for 总经理 (general manager) or 总裁 (president/CEO). It can safely be used for any senior executive and errs on the side of flattery. Variants: 总监 zǒngjiān (director), 副总 fùzǒng (deputy director).
长辈	zhǎng bèi	senior generation
长江	Cháng Jiāng	the Yangtze River — lit. "the long river" — 长 cháng (long) + 江 jiāng (large river; river south of Huai River). The Yangtze, Asia's longest river at 6,300 km, is simply "the Long River." Compare: 黄河 Huáng Hé (Yellow River) is named for its color; 长江 is named for its length.
丈夫	zhàng fu	husband — 丈 zhàng (an honorific for an adult man) + 夫 fū (man). The ordinary, neutral word for one's husband, the man one is married to. In older usage 大丈夫 dà zhàngfu also means a true man, a man of character, but on its own 丈夫 simply names the spouse.
正名	zhèng míng	rectification of names; calling things by their correct names — A key political-philosophical doctrine of Confucius. When a disciple asks what he would do first if given control of a state, Confucius says: rectify names 必也正名乎.
政治家	zhèng zhì jiā	statesman; skilled politician — 政治 zhèngzhì (politics) + 家 jiā. More elevated than 政客 zhèngkè (politician, often pejorative, implying opportunism and maneuvering). 政治家 implies vision, skill, and genuine mastery of governance: a statesman rather than a mere political operator. The 家 suffix confers dignity.
纸钱	zhǐ qián	spirit money — currency for the afterlife — 纸钱 (also 冥纸 míngzhǐ, 金纸 jīnzhǐ in Taiwan, or 冥币 míngbì) are paper facsimiles of money and goods burnt as offerings; the smoke carries them to the dead.
中秋节	Zhōng qiū jié	Mid-Autumn Festival
中元节	Zhōng yuán Jié	the Ghost Festival — Daoist name for the 15th of the 7th month — 中 zhōng (middle) + 元 yuán (origin; first; primary) + 节 jié (festival; season). The Daoist name, derived from the Three Officials calendar: 上元 (upper prime, 1st month, 15th), 中元 (middle prime, 7th month, 15th), 下元 (lower prime, 10th month, 15th). Each 元 is the birthday of one official.
种子	zhǒng zi	seed (of a plant); (sports) seeded player — 种 zhǒng (kind; to sow) + 子 zi. The seed sense of 子 made explicit. By extension it names a "seeded" competitor in a tournament, the one expected to grow into a winner.
重阳	Chóng yáng	Double Ninth; Double Yang — the festival name — 重 chóng (double; repeated; layered) + 阳 yáng (yang; the masculine creative principle; odd numbers; heaven). The name describes the cosmological condition of the date: two nine-yang numbers coinciding.
重阳糕	Chóng yáng gāo	Double Ninth cake — the city dweller substitution — 重阳 Chóngyáng (the festival) + 糕 gāo (cake; steamed or baked confection). The cleverest detail of the festival: 糕 (gāo, cake) is homophonous with 高 (gāo, high).
重阳节	Chóng yáng Jié	Double Ninth Festival (autumn)
茱萸	zhū yú	zhuyu; Japanese cornelian cherry — the apotropaic sprig — 茱萸 is the Chinese name for Cornus officinalis (Japanese cornelian cherry) and related Zanthoxylum species. The plant is pungent and strongly scented, which in Chinese folk medicine and ritual practice made it repellent to malevolent forces and disease.
桌子	zhuō zi	table; desk — 桌 zhuō (table) + 子 zi. A textbook example of the bare suffix: the root 桌 rarely stands alone, and 子 makes it a usable noun.
子女	zǐn ǚ	sons and daughters; children (collectively) — 子 zǐ (sons) + 女 nǚ (daughters). A formal, collective term for one's children as a group, common in writing about family and law. Here 子 keeps its full third tone.
姊	zǐ	elder sister (literary)
自家	zì jiā	one's own family/people; one's own — 自 zì (self; one's own) + 家 jiā (family; home). Marks something as belonging to or originating from one's own household. Central to the Chinese social concept of insiders vs. outsiders: 自家人 (one of our own) vs. 外人 wàirén (an outsider).
宗族	zōng zú	lineage — the corporate kinship group — 宗族 is the term for the patrilineal descent group: all those who share a common named male ancestor and who collectively maintain the ancestral hall and genealogy. In rural China, entire villages are often single-lineage settlements (单姓村 dān xìng cūn), with all residents sharing a surname.
族谱	zú pǔ	clan genealogy — the lineage's living memory — 族谱 (also 家谱 jiāpǔ or 宗谱 zōngpǔ) is the written genealogical record of a lineage. It records birth, death, marriage, official appointments, and notable achievements of every member, generation by generation.
祖父	zǔ fù	paternal grandfather (formal) — 祖 zǔ (ancestor, forebear) + 父 fù (father). The father's father, in formal register. Spoken Mandarin uses 爷爷 yéye for the same man, the way 爸爸 stands in for 父亲.
祖宗	zǔ zōng	ancestors (collective, formal)
作家	zuò jiā	writer; author (literary) — 作 zuò (to create; to make; to compose) + 家 jiā. The term implies literary seriousness and sustained creative output, not just anyone who writes. The 家 suffix distinguishes this from 记者 jìzhě (journalist) or 编辑 biānjí (editor), which are professional roles without the mastercraft implication.
