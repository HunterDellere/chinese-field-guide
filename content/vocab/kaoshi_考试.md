faq:
  - q: "What was the 科举 (kējǔ) imperial examination system?"
    a: "The civil-service examination that selected officials by written test rather than birth, run from 605 CE under the Sui dynasty until its abolition in 1905. Candidates climbed from county exams to the palace exam, and the top palace graduate was the 状元 (zhuàngyuán), a word still used for the top scorer of anything."
  - q: "What is the 高考 (gāokǎo)?"
    a: "The national university entrance examination, held every June, whose score largely determines university admission. It is the modern descendant of the exam tradition: cities quiet down around test sites, and the results make provincial news."
  - q: "How do you say pass and fail an exam in Chinese?"
    a: "及格 (jígé) is to pass, 不及格 to fail, with 60 out of 100 as the customary passing line. To take an exam is 参加考试 or simply 考; 考上 (kǎoshàng) means to test into a school, the verb of every family's ambitions."

      <h1 class="topic-hero-title">考试</h1>
      <span class="topic-hero-title-py">kǎoshì</span>
      <p class="topic-hero-desc">China ran the world's longest experiment in testing, thirteen centuries of imperial exams, and the vocabulary still sits every June for the gaokao.</p>
    </header>

    <span class="section-anchor" id="etymology"></span>
    <div class="section-head">
      <span class="sh-cn">字源</span>
      <span class="sh-py">zìyuán</span>
      <span class="sh-en">Etymology &amp; Structure</span>
      <span class="sh-rule"></span>
    </div>

    <div class="scholar" data-glyph="考">
      <div class="scholar-label">老人与言 lǎorén yǔ yán · The Elder and the Word</div>
      <p><strong>考</strong> (kǎo) began as a bent figure with long hair leaning on a staff, a graph nearly identical to 老 (lǎo, old), and its earliest senses cluster around aged men and deceased fathers (the ritual formula 先考 still means "my late father" on traditional gravestones). The leap to "examine" came through the elder's role: the one who scrutinizes, questions, and judges. The character now heads the whole verification family: 考试 (exam), 考验 (kǎoyàn, an ordeal that tests character), 考察 (kǎochá, to inspect on site), 考古 (kǎogǔ, archaeology, "examining the ancient"), and 思考 (sīkǎo, to think something through).</p>
      <p><strong>试</strong> (shì) is the speech radical 讠 plus 式 (shì, pattern, formula) doing double duty as phonetic and hint: to try something against a pattern. Alone it is the everyday verb "to try" (试一试, give it a try; 试衣间, the fitting room). Welded together, 考试 is the trial by questioning, and the word serves as both noun and verb: 明天考试 (there's an exam tomorrow), 考得怎么样 (how did the exam go).</p>
    </div>

    <span class="section-anchor" id="keju"></span>
    <div class="section-head">
      <span class="sh-cn">科举</span>
      <span class="sh-py">kējǔ</span>
      <span class="sh-en">The Imperial Examination</span>
      <span class="sh-rule"></span>
    </div>

    <div class="scholar" data-glyph="举">
      <div class="scholar-label">朝为田舍郎 zhāo wéi tiánshè láng · A Farm Boy at Dawn</div>
      <p>The 科举 (kējǔ) system, formalized under the Sui dynasty in 605 CE and abolished in 1905, was the world's longest-running standardized test: a ladder of written examinations, from county through province to the capital and finally the palace, that selected the empire's officials by performance on the Confucian classics rather than by birth. It was imperfect meritocracy (preparation took years a poor family could rarely fund), but the ideal it institutionalized was radical and durable: the Song-era school rhyme ran 朝为田舍郎，暮登天子堂, "a farm boy in the morning, in the Son of Heaven's hall by dusk." Exam success restructured families' entire economic strategies, and the exam-cell compounds where candidates wrote for days, one candidate per brick stall, still stand in Nanjing as a museum.</p>
      <p>The system minted vocabulary that never expired. The palace exam's top graduate was the <strong>状元</strong> (zhuàngyuán), and today's provincial gaokao top scorer is called exactly that, as is the top performer in any field (销售状元, the sales champion). 落榜 (luòbǎng, "falling off the list") is still failing an entrance exam, from the posted rolls of successful candidates; 金榜题名 ("one's name inscribed on the golden list") is still the congratulation for exam success. Even cheating kept its heritage: examination-era crib garments, shirts inked with microscopic classics, sit in museums as ancestors of today's contraband smartwatches.</p>
    </div>

    <span class="section-anchor" id="gaokao"></span>
    <div class="section-head">
      <span class="sh-cn">高考</span>
      <span class="sh-py">gāokǎo</span>
      <span class="sh-en">The Gaokao</span>
      <span class="sh-rule"></span>
    </div>

    <div class="scholar" data-glyph="高">
      <div class="scholar-label">六月的全国安静 · The June the Country Goes Quiet</div>
      <p>The 高考 (gāokǎo, short for 普通高等学校招生全国统一考试, the national unified university entrance exam) is the exam tradition's living heir, sat by roughly thirteen million students each June over two to three days. The country visibly reorganizes around it: construction sites near test centers pause, traffic police escort students with forgotten ID cards, and parents wait at the gates, mothers sometimes in qipao dresses for the pun 旗开得胜 ("victory at the first unfurling of the flag"). A student's 分数 (score) plus the province's 分数线 (cutoff lines) determine which universities are reachable, and 报志愿 (filling in ranked school choices) is a family research project of its own.</p>
      <p>The gaokao's cultural weight generates its own lexicon: 复读 (fùdú) is repeating the final year for another attempt; 陪读 (péidú) is a parent relocating to accompany a studying child; 学霸 (xuébà, "study tyrant") is the effortless top student and 学渣 (xuézhā, "study dregs") the self-deprecating opposite. The verb of triumph is 考上 (kǎoshàng, to test into): 她考上北大了, she got into Peking University, a sentence that travels through a family's group chats within minutes of the results.</p>
    </div>

    <div class="cards">

      <div class="card c-teal">
        <div class="card-head">
          <span class="card-cn">及格</span>
          <span class="card-py">jígé</span>
          <span class="card-en">to pass (an exam or standard)</span>
        </div>
        <div class="tags"><span class="tag tag-v">V 动词 dòngcí</span></div>
        <div class="card-def">
          及 (reach) + 格 (the standard grid): to reach the mark, customarily 60 out of 100. 不及格 is failing, and the near-miss score of 59 is proverbial for agony: 59分 is one point short of relief.
        </div>
        <div class="examples">
          <div class="example">
            <div class="ex-cn">这次数学考试他刚好及格。</div>
            <div class="ex-py">Zhè cì shùxué kǎoshì tā gānghǎo jígé.</div>
            <div class="ex-en">He just barely passed this math exam.</div>
          </div>
        </div>
      </div>

      <div class="card c-ochre">
        <div class="card-head">
          <span class="card-cn">复习</span>
          <span class="card-py">fùxí</span>
          <span class="card-en">to review, revise</span>
        </div>
        <div class="tags"><span class="tag tag-v">V 动词 dòngcí</span></div>
        <div class="card-def">
          复 (again) + 习 (practice, whose traditional form 習 shows wings over the sun: a fledgling's repeated flights). The exam-season verb, echoing the Analects' opening line about the pleasure of timely review, which every Chinese student can quote and few believe during finals.
        </div>
        <div class="examples">
          <div class="example">
            <div class="ex-cn">明天考试，今晚我要复习到十二点。</div>
            <div class="ex-py">Míngtiān kǎoshì, jīnwǎn wǒ yào fùxí dào shí'èr diǎn.</div>
            <div class="ex-en">The exam is tomorrow; tonight I'll be reviewing until midnight.</div>
          </div>
        </div>
      </div>

      <div class="card c-violet">
        <div class="card-head">
          <span class="card-cn">状元</span>
          <span class="card-py">zhuàngyuán</span>
          <span class="card-en">top scorer; the imperial exam's first graduate</span>
        </div>
        <div class="tags"><span class="tag tag-n">N 名词 míngcí</span></div>
        <div class="card-def">
          The palace examination's number one, personally ranked by the emperor, paraded through the capital on horseback. The word now crowns any field's best: 高考状元 (top gaokao scorer), 行行出状元 ("every trade produces its zhuangyuan," the proverb that dignifies all work).
        </div>
        <div class="examples">
          <div class="example">
            <div class="ex-cn">三百六十行，行行出状元。</div>
            <div class="ex-py">Sānbǎi liùshí háng, háng háng chū zhuàngyuán.</div>
            <div class="ex-en">Of the three hundred sixty trades, every one produces its own champion.</div>
          </div>
        </div>
      </div>

      <div class="card c-sienna">
        <div class="card-head">
          <span class="card-cn">作弊</span>
          <span class="card-py">zuòbì</span>
          <span class="card-en">to cheat (on an exam)</span>
        </div>
        <div class="tags"><span class="tag tag-v">V 动词 dòngcí</span></div>
        <div class="card-def">
          作 (do) + 弊 (fraud, malpractice). Exam cheating is a criminal offense in China for national exams, policed with metal detectors and signal jammers, which measures how much the tests are worth. The honest sibling 打小抄 (crib notes) covers the classroom misdemeanor.
        </div>
        <div class="examples">
          <div class="example">
            <div class="ex-cn">考试作弊会被取消成绩。</div>
            <div class="ex-py">Kǎoshì zuòbì huì bèi qǔxiāo chéngjì.</div>
            <div class="ex-en">Cheating on an exam will get your results canceled.</div>
          </div>
        </div>

    <div class="chengyu-grid">

      <div class="cy">
        <span class="cy-cn">金榜题名</span>
        <span class="cy-py">jīn bǎng tí míng</span>
        <span class="cy-en">one's name inscribed on the golden list — exam success</span>
        <span class="cy-note">The 金榜 was the yellow placard of palace-exam graduates posted outside the examination compound. Classical culture counted it among life's four great joys, alongside the wedding night, rain after drought, and meeting an old friend far from home. Today it captions every gaokao congratulations post.</span>
      </div>

      <div class="cy">
        <span class="cy-cn">名落孙山</span>
        <span class="cy-py">míng luò Sūn Shān</span>
        <span class="cy-en">one's name fell behind Sun Shan's — to fail an examination</span>
        <span class="cy-note">Sun Shan, a Song-dynasty wit, passed the provincial exam in last place. Asked by a neighbor whether the neighbor's son had passed, he answered in verse: "The last name on the list was Sun Shan; your worthy son came after Sun Shan." The gentlest failure notice in the language, still the standard literary way to say someone did not make the cut.</span>
      </div>

      <div class="cy">
        <span class="cy-cn">十年寒窗</span>
        <span class="cy-py">shí nián hán chuāng</span>
        <span class="cy-en">ten years at a cold window — long, austere years of study</span>
        <span class="cy-note">The image of the exam candidate reading through winters without heat. The full couplet adds the payoff: ten years at the cold window unknown, one exam success and the world knows your name (十年寒窗无人问，一举成名天下知). Modern parents invoke it during gaokao season with full sincerity.</span>
      </div>

      <div class="cy">
        <span class="cy-cn">临时抱佛脚</span>
        <span class="cy-py">línshí bào fójiǎo</span>
        <span class="cy-en">clasping the Buddha's feet at the last minute — cramming</span>
        <span class="cy-note">From a proverb about neglecting incense in ordinary times and embracing the statue's feet only when disaster looms (平时不烧香，临时抱佛脚). The universal student condition, named without mercy. Usable for any deferred preparation: visas, taxes, wedding speeches.</span>
      </div>
