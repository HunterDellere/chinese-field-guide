faq:
  - q: "What does 挂号 (guàhào) mean at a Chinese hospital?"
    a: "Registration: literally 'hanging a number.' Before seeing any doctor you take a numbered ticket for a department, historically at a service window and now mostly by phone app. The word is the gateway to the whole visit, and 挂不上号 (couldn't get a number) is the standard complaint about crowded top hospitals."
  - q: "How do you describe symptoms in Chinese?"
    a: "The core set: 发烧 (fāshāo, fever), 咳嗽 (késou, cough), 头疼 (tóuténg, headache), 嗓子疼 (sore throat), 拉肚子 (lādùzi, diarrhea, 'pulling stomach'), 恶心 (ěxīn, nausea). The catch-all is 不舒服 (bù shūfu, 'not comfortable'), which is also the polite way to call in sick."
  - q: "What is the difference between 中医 and 西医 at the hospital?"
    a: "中医 (zhōngyī) is traditional Chinese medicine, 西医 (xīyī) is biomedicine, and most Chinese cities offer both, sometimes in the same hospital. Patients commonly use them in parallel: 西医 for acute problems, 中医 for chronic complaints and 调理 (tiáolǐ, regulating the body's balance)."

      <h1 class="topic-hero-title">看病</h1>
      <span class="topic-hero-title-py">kànbìng</span>
      <p class="topic-hero-desc">Getting sick in Chinese starts with a queue number, runs on the verb "look," and ends with the eternal prescription: drink more hot water.</p>
    </header>

    <span class="section-anchor" id="word"></span>
    <div class="section-head">
      <span class="sh-cn">看病</span>
      <span class="sh-py">kànbìng</span>
      <span class="sh-en">The Word</span>
      <span class="sh-rule"></span>
    </div>

    <div class="scholar" data-glyph="病">
      <div class="scholar-label">看的两个方向 kàn de liǎng gè fāngxiàng · A Verb That Points Both Ways</div>
      <p>看病 (kànbìng) is "to look at illness," and the phrase points in both directions: the patient 看病 (goes to see a doctor) and the doctor 看病 (sees patients). 看医生 ("see a doctor") also works, but 看病 is the native idiom, and its grammar reveals how the language frames the event: illness is a thing one goes to have looked at. The sick person 生病了 (shēngbìng le, "has generated illness," the same 生 that generates everything else in the language) or, more casually, 病了.</p>
      <p>病 itself wears the illness radical 疒, a pictograph of a person laid out on a bed, which brands the whole sick-bay of the script: 疼 (téng, to hurt), 痛 (tòng, pain), 瘦 (shòu, thin), 癌 (ái, cancer), 疫 (yì, epidemic, the character of 疫情, the pandemic-situation word of 2020). Spotting 疒 on an unfamiliar character is a reliable warning that the news is medical.</p>
    </div>

      <span class="label">医院流程 yīyuàn liúchéng · The Hospital Circuit</span>
      <strong>挂号 guàhào</strong> → register, take a number : the first stop, now mostly in-app<br>
      <strong>门诊 ménzhěn</strong> → outpatient clinic ; 急诊 jízhěn → the ER<br>
      <strong>科 kē</strong> → department : 内科 internal medicine, 外科 surgery, 儿科 pediatrics, 牙科 dental<br>
      <strong>化验 huàyàn / 拍片 pāipiàn</strong> → lab tests / imaging ("taking films")<br>
      <strong>开药 kāiyào</strong> → prescribe ; 取药 qǔyào → collect medicine at the pharmacy window<br>
      <strong>住院 zhùyuàn</strong> → be hospitalized ; 出院 chūyuàn → be discharged
    </div>

    <div class="scholar" data-glyph="号">
      <div class="scholar-label">挂不上号 guà bu shàng hào · When the Numbers Run Out</div>
      <p>A Chinese hospital visit is self-service navigation, and 挂号 (guàhào, "hang a number") is its keystone word. The patient picks a department, takes a numbered slot, and queues for a consultation that may last three minutes; there is no gatekeeping general practitioner, so patients self-triage straight to 皮肤科 (dermatology) or 骨科 (orthopedics). A regular slot is a 普通号, a senior-specialist slot is a 专家号 (zhuānjiāhào), and the scarcity of 专家号 at famous hospitals created a black market of scalpers, 号贩子 ("number traffickers"), whom the app-based booking systems were partly built to defeat. The vocabulary of queueing (排队 páiduì), windows (窗口 chuāngkǒu), and 先交费 ("pay first," at every stage) completes the circuit.</p>
      <p>The payment word 医保 (yībǎo, state medical insurance) matters practically: pharmacies and registration windows ask 有没有医保 as routinely as supermarkets ask about bags. For minor complaints most people skip the hospital entirely and walk into a 药店 (yàodiàn, pharmacy), where the staff will recommend over-the-counter standbys; the fever-and-cold aisle is a genre of its own, from 感冒灵 cold granules to 板蓝根, the isatis-root drink stockpiled by mothers at the first cough of any epidemic.</p>
    </div>

    <div class="section-head">
      <span class="sh-cn">症状</span>
      <span class="sh-py">zhèngzhuàng</span>
      <span class="sh-en">Saying Where It Hurts</span>
      <span class="sh-rule"></span>
    </div>

    <div class="scholar" data-glyph="疼">
      <div class="scholar-label">哪里不舒服? nǎlǐ bù shūfu · Where Are You Not Comfortable?</div>
      <p>The doctor's opening question is 哪里不舒服? ("where are you not comfortable?"), and 不舒服 (bù shūfu) is the great useful vagueness of medical Chinese: it covers everything from a headache to heartbreak and is also the polite formula for calling in sick. Specifics attach 疼 (téng) or 痛 (tòng), both "hurt," to body parts: 头疼 (headache), 肚子疼 (stomachache), 嗓子疼 (sore throat), 牙疼 (toothache, proverbially not a disease yet agonizing enough to kill, 牙疼不是病，疼起来真要命). Fever is 发烧 (fāshāo, "emit burn"), measured and reported in degrees: 烧到三十九度 is a 39-degree fever and grounds for the ER.</p>
      <p>The everyday illness canon: 感冒 (gǎnmào, the common cold, which speakers subdivide with TCM's temperature logic into 风寒感冒 wind-cold and 风热感冒 wind-heat varieties, each with its own pharmacy shelf), 咳嗽 (késou, cough), 拉肚子 (lādùzi, diarrhea, "pulling stomach," blunter and more common than the clinical 腹泻), 恶心 (ěxīn, nausea, which doubles as the word for "disgusting"), and 过敏 (guòmǐn, allergy, "excess sensitivity"). 上火 (shànghuǒ, "internal heat rising") deserves special note: it is a TCM folk diagnosis with no biomedical equivalent, blamed for canker sores, sore throats, and pimples, and treated by avoiding fried food and drinking cooling teas. Every Chinese speaker uses it; no translation survives contact with it.</p>
    </div>

    <span class="section-anchor" id="culture"></span>
    <div class="section-head">
      <span class="sh-cn">多喝热水</span>
      <span class="sh-py">duō hē rèshuǐ</span>
      <span class="sh-en">Treatment Culture</span>
      <span class="sh-rule"></span>
    </div>

    <div class="scholar" data-glyph="药">
      <div class="scholar-label">输液与热水 shūyè yǔ rèshuǐ · The Drip and the Thermos</div>
      <p>Two treatment habits define the popular culture of Chinese medicine-taking. The first is 输液 (shūyè, the IV drip, colloquially 打点滴 or 挂水 "hanging water"): for decades an ordinary cold could end in a row of chairs under IV stands, patients scrolling phones with a needle taped to the wrist. Health authorities have campaigned against routine drips and antibiotic overuse since the mid-2010s, and many hospitals have abolished outpatient drips for minor illness, but the cultural expectation that a "real" treatment involves a bag on a pole fades slowly, especially for anxious grandparents supervising a child's cold. The second is thermal: cold drinks are suspect in illness, and the reflexive advice for nearly any complaint is 多喝热水 ("drink more hot water"). The phrase is so universal that it became the internet's shorthand for a boyfriend's useless concern, yet the thermos of hot water remains the most Chinese of comforts, handed out on trains, in offices, and at every hospital water dispenser.</p>
      <p>Alongside biomedicine (西医 xīyī), traditional medicine (中医 zhōngyī) runs as a parallel, state-supported system covered in this guide's TCM entries: hospitals of 中医院, the pulse-taking and tongue-inspection diagnostics, herbal decoctions (中药 zhōngyào) brewed into famously bitter soups, and the verb 调理 (tiáolǐ, to regulate and recondition the body over weeks rather than days). The practical folk division of labor: 西医 for the acute and surgical, 中医 for the chronic, the vague, and the postpartum. A learner needs no position in the perennial 中西医 debate, but does need the vocabulary, because the first question a Chinese friend asks about any ailment may well be whether you have tried both.</p>
