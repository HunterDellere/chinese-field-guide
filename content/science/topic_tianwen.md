faq:
  - q: "Why are the planets named after the five elements in Chinese?"
    a: "Chinese astronomers correlated the five visible planets with the five phases (五行): Mercury is 水星 (water star), Venus 金星 (metal star), Mars 火星 (fire star), Jupiter 木星 (wood star), and Saturn 土星 (earth star). The correlation was fixed by Han times; planets discovered by telescope got descriptive names instead, like 海王星 (sea-king star) for Neptune."
  - q: "What is the difference between 恒星 (héngxīng) and 行星 (xíngxīng)?"
    a: "恒星 is 'constant star,' a fixed star like the sun; 行星 is 'moving star,' a planet, named for the wandering that also gave Greek planetes its meaning. A satellite is 卫星 ('guard star') and a comet is 彗星 ('broom star')."
  - q: "What does 宇宙 (yǔzhòu, universe) literally mean?"
    a: "Space plus time. The Han-era Huainanzi defines it: 'All that is above, below, and in the four directions is called 宇; all past and present is called 宙.' The word for universe has had spacetime built into it for two thousand years."

      <h1 class="topic-hero-title">天文</h1>
      <span class="topic-hero-title-py">tiānwén</span>
      <p class="topic-hero-desc">The five visible planets are named for the five phases, comets are brooms, and the space program borrows its names from mythology.</p>
    </header>

    <span class="section-anchor" id="word"></span>
    <div class="section-head">
      <span class="sh-cn">天文</span>
      <span class="sh-py">tiānwén</span>
      <span class="sh-en">The Word</span>
      <span class="sh-rule"></span>
    </div>

    <div class="scholar" data-glyph="天">
      <div class="scholar-label">天上的纹 tiānshàng de wén · The Patterns of Heaven</div>
      <p>天文 (tiānwén) is "heaven's writing": 文 is the same character as in 文字 (writing) and 文化 (culture), and its early sense was pattern, markings, the crossing lines on a surface. The sky was a text, and reading it was a state function. Imperial China maintained an astronomical bureau continuously for some two thousand years, because celestial events were politically legible: a comet or an unpredicted eclipse could signal that the dynasty's mandate was slipping. The observational records this bureaucracy produced (the supernova of 1054, returns of Halley's comet back to 240 BCE, sunspot observations centuries before European telescopes) are still mined by modern astronomers, which makes the imperial archives one of the longest-running datasets in science.</p>
      <p>The modern discipline is 天文学, its practitioner a 天文学家, and the observatory a 天文台 (tiānwéntái), the same word that names the historic Beijing Ancient Observatory with its Ming-era bronze instruments. A separate word, 占星 (zhānxīng), covers astrology, and modern Chinese keeps the two as distinct as English does.</p>
    </div>

    <div class="pattern">
      <span class="label">行星命名 xíngxīng mìngmíng · Naming the Planets</span>
      <strong>水星 shuǐxīng</strong> → Mercury : the water star<br>
      <strong>金星 jīnxīng</strong> → Venus : the metal star; as morning star anciently called 启明 (qǐmíng, "opening brightness")<br>
      <strong>火星 huǒxīng</strong> → Mars : the fire star; also the everyday word for a spark<br>
      <strong>木星 mùxīng</strong> → Jupiter : the wood star; anciently 岁星 (suìxīng, "year star") for its ~12-year cycle<br>
      <strong>土星 tǔxīng</strong> → Saturn : the earth star<br>
      <strong>天王星 / 海王星</strong> → Uranus ("sky-king star") / Neptune ("sea-king star") : telescope-era planets got translated mythology instead
    </div>

    <div class="scholar" data-glyph="星">
      <div class="scholar-label">五行在天 wǔxíng zài tiān · The Five Phases Overhead</div>
      <p>The five planets visible to the naked eye were correlated with the five phases (五行 wǔxíng) by Han times, so the weekly experience of a Chinese speaker includes cosmology: Mars is literally the fire star, and its reddish color made the assignment feel observational rather than arbitrary. Jupiter's older name 岁星, the "year star," reflects real astronomy, since its roughly twelve-year circuit of the sky calibrated the twelve earthly branches of the traditional calendar. The correlation runs deeper than names; the same five-phase grid organizes the seasons, directions, organs, and colors, and the planets were its celestial column.</p>
      <p>Planets found after the telescope broke the pattern gracefully. Uranus became 天王星 and Neptune 海王星, translating the Greek sky-god and sea-god by sense. Pluto was 冥王星, the "underworld-king star," and when it was demoted in 2006 Chinese media had the same fun with the news as everyone else. Meanwhile 火星 has become one of the most-typed words in Chinese internet culture: 火星人 (Martian) is slang for someone hopelessly out of the loop, from the taunt 你是从火星来的吗 ("did you just arrive from Mars?").</p>
    </div>

    <div class="scholar" data-glyph="彗">
      <div class="scholar-label">恒、行、卫、彗 · Four Kinds of Star</div>
      <p>Chinese sorts the sky with modifiers on 星 (xīng, star). A fixed star is a 恒星 (héngxīng, "constant star"); a planet is a 行星 (xíngxīng, "moving star"), the same wandering that gave Greek <em>planetes</em> its meaning; a satellite, natural or artificial, is a 卫星 (wèixīng, "guard star"), so a satellite dish is a 卫星天线 and a flagship's smaller companion city is jokingly its 卫星城. A comet is a 彗星 (huìxīng, "broom star"), named for the tail, and the folk name 扫把星 (sàobǎxīng, "broom star") is a live insult for a person who brings bad luck, a fossil of the old omen-reading in which comets swept away dynasties.</p>
      <p>Two more members complete the set: 流星 (liúxīng, "flowing star") is a meteor, whence 流星雨 (meteor shower), and 卫星 in its state-media sense produced the Great Leap Forward idiom 放卫星 ("launch a satellite"), meaning to announce an inflated achievement, coined in 1958 when production miracles were reported alongside Sputnik-era enthusiasm. The morpheme also does celebrity duty: 明星 (míngxīng, "bright star") is the word for a star of screen or stage, and 追星 ("chasing stars") is fandom.</p>
    </div>

    <div class="section-head">
      <span class="sh-cn">宇宙与航天</span>
      <span class="sh-py">yǔzhòu yǔ hángtiān</span>
      <span class="sh-en">Space-Time and the Space Program</span>
      <span class="sh-rule"></span>

    <div class="scholar" data-glyph="宇">
      <div class="scholar-label">上下四方，古往今来 · All Space, All Time</div>
      <p>宇宙 (yǔzhòu, universe) is a two-character definition of spacetime that predates the physics by two millennia. The Han compendium <em>Huainanzi</em> (淮南子, 2nd century BCE) states it: all that is above, below, and in the four directions is called 宇; all past and present is called 宙. Space plus time, fused into the word for cosmos. Modern physics simply moved in: 宇宙学 is cosmology, 宇宙大爆炸 is the Big Bang ("the great cosmic explosion"), and 宇航员 (yǔhángyuán, "cosmos-voyaging personnel") is one of the words for astronaut, alongside 航天员 (hángtiānyuán, "heaven-sailing personnel"), the official term for Chinese crews, which English borrowed back as "taikonaut."</p>
      <p>The space program names its hardware from the classical sky. The crewed spacecraft is 神舟 (Shénzhōu, "divine vessel," homophonous with 神州, an ancient name for China). The lunar probes are 嫦娥 (Cháng'é), after the goddess who fled to the moon, and their rover is 玉兔 (Yùtù), her jade rabbit. The space station is 天宫 (Tiāngōng, "heavenly palace"), the Mars rover is 祝融 (Zhùróng), the fire god, and the global navigation system is 北斗 (Běidǒu), the Northern Dipper that oriented travelers for three thousand years. Every launch is a small examination in classical mythology, administered by the evening news.</p>
    </div>

      <div class="card c-teal">
        <div class="card-head"><span class="tag-n">n</span> 天文 tiānwén</div>
        <p>Astronomy: heaven's patterns. 天文数字 ("an astronomical figure") is the standard idiom for an unthinkably large sum, same metaphor as English.</p>
      </div>
      <div class="card c-ochre">
        <div class="card-head"><span class="tag-n">n</span> 宇宙 yǔzhòu</div>
        <p>The universe: 宇 all space, 宙 all time, per the Huainanzi's definition. 元宇宙 (yuán yǔzhòu) is the metaverse, the newest tenant in a very old word.</p>
      </div>
      <div class="card c-sienna">
        <div class="card-head"><span class="tag-n">n</span> 星星 xīngxing</div>
        <p>Star, in the affectionate reduplicated form used in speech and songs. Single 星 does the compounding work: 星空 (starry sky), 星系 (galaxy, "star system"), 星座 (constellation, and the zodiac sign small talk runs on).</p>
      </div>
      <div class="card c-violet">
        <div class="card-head"><span class="tag-n">n</span> 月亮 yuèliang</div>
        <p>The moon. Formal and astronomical contexts use 月球 (yuèqiú, "moon sphere"); poetry and mid-autumn nostalgia keep 月亮. A full moon is 满月, also the name of a baby's one-month celebration.</p>
      </div>
      <div class="card c-red">
        <div class="card-head"><span class="tag-n">n</span> 太阳 tàiyáng</div>
        <p>The sun: "supreme yang," cosmology embedded in the everyday word. 太阳系 is the solar system; 晒太阳 is to sun oneself, the retiree's park pastime.</p>
      </div>
      <div class="card c-teal">
        <div class="card-head"><span class="tag-n">n</span> 航天 hángtiān</div>
        <p>Spaceflight: "sailing the heavens," on the model of 航海 (seafaring). 航天员 is the official word for China's astronauts; the industry is 航天工程, space engineering.</p>
