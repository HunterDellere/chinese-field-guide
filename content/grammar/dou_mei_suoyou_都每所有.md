faq:
  - q: "What does 都 (dōu) mean and why does it appear so often?"
    a: "都 dōu means 'all' or 'in every case', and it is an adverb, so it stands before the verb and totalizes whatever came before it: 我们都去 ('we all go'). It appears constantly because most other quantifiers require it: 每个人都…, 所有的书都…, 谁都…. In Mandarin, 'all' is usually said at the verb, not at the noun."
  - q: "What is the difference between 每 (měi) and 各 (gè)?"
    a: "每 měi means 'every', treating the members as identical instances of a set, and it almost always calls for 都 at the verb: 每个学生都有课本 ('every student has a textbook'). 各 gè means 'each, the various', stressing the members' individuality and variety, and it usually skips 都: 各国有各国的国情 ('each country has its own conditions')."
  - q: "How do you say 'none' or 'nothing' in Chinese?"
    a: "Mandarin has no single word for 'none'. It negates a universal instead: question words plus 都/也 plus a negative. 谁都不知道 ('nobody knows'), 我什么都没吃 ('I ate nothing'), 一个人也没来 ('not a single person came'). The pattern 一 + measure word + 也/都 + negative is the standard emphatic zero."
  - q: "Do 所有 and 每 always need 都?"
    a: "Very nearly. A quantified subject is normally echoed by 都 before the verb: 所有的学生都来了, 每张桌子都是新的. Dropping 都 sounds incomplete in standard Mandarin. The exceptions are 每 phrases acting as adverbials of frequency (我每天喝茶 is fine without 都, though 我每天都喝茶 adds emphasis) and 各, which prefers no 都."

      <h1 class="topic-hero-title">都 · 每 · 所有</h1>
      <span class="topic-hero-title-py">dōu · měi · suǒyǒu</span>
      <p class="topic-hero-desc">All, every, some, many, none: 都 carries the whole quantifier system, and nearly every other word in it leans on 都 to work.</p>

    <!-- ── ETYMOLOGY ── -->
    <span class="section-anchor" id="etymology"></span>
    <div class="section-head">
      <span class="sh-cn">字源与逻辑</span>
      <span class="sh-py">zìyuán yǔ luójí</span>
      <span class="sh-en">Etymology &amp; The Logic of Quantifying at the Verb</span>
      <span class="sh-rule"></span>
    </div>

    <div class="scholar" data-glyph="都">
      <div class="scholar-label">字源洞见 zìyuán dòngjiàn · Etymological Insight</div>
      <p><strong>都</strong> read dū meant, and still means, "capital city" (首都 shǒudū). The adverb 都 dōu ("all") grew out of the capital sense: the capital is where everything gathers, and by the medieval vernacular the character was already doing duty for "altogether, in every case". Mandarin kept both readings in the one glyph: 都 dū the place where all things converge, 都 dōu the word that makes a sentence converge on totality.</p>
      <p>The structural fact that organizes this whole page: English quantifies inside the noun phrase ("all students", "every book", "nothing"), while Mandarin prefers to quantify at the verb. 学生都来了 puts "all" (都) between subject and verb, and the noun 学生 stays bare. Even when a noun-side quantifier like 每 (every) or 所有 (all) appears, the sentence still wants 都 at the verb: 每个学生<strong>都</strong>来了, 所有的书<strong>都</strong>卖完了. The noun-side word announces the set; 都 executes the quantification.</p>
      <p>The same verb-side logic produces Mandarin's version of "nobody" and "nothing". There is no dedicated word for "none": the language takes a question word (谁 who, 什么 what, 哪儿 where), totalizes it with 都 or 也, and negates the verb. 谁都不知道 is literally "who all not know": nobody knows. 我什么都没吃 is "I what all didn't eat": I ate nothing. Learners who look for a noun meaning "nothing" are looking in the wrong place; the zero lives in the verb phrase.</p>
      <p>This page covers the quantity words themselves. The units used to count nouns (个, 本, 条, 张) have their own entry: 量词 measure words. The question words 多少 and 几, which double as quantity expressions, are treated in the 多少 · 几 entry.</p>
    </div>

    <div class="section-head">

    <div class="pattern">
      <span class="label">量化结构 liànghuà jiégòu · The quantifier skeleton</span>
      <strong>每 + MW + Noun + 都 + Verb</strong> → 每个学生<strong>都</strong>有课本 (every student has a textbook)<br>
      <strong>所有 (的) + Noun + 都 + Verb</strong> → 所有的书<strong>都</strong>卖完了 (all the books are sold out)<br>
      <strong>Subject + 都 + Verb</strong> → 我们<strong>都</strong>去 (we're all going)<br>
      <strong>疑问词 + 都/也 + (不/没) + Verb</strong> → 谁<strong>都</strong>不知道 (nobody knows) · 什么<strong>都</strong>行 (anything works)<br>
      <strong>一 + MW + (Noun) + 也/都 + 不/没 + Verb</strong> → 一个人<strong>也</strong>没来 (not a single person came)
    </div>

    <div class="scholar" data-glyph="每">
      <div class="scholar-label">学者洞见 xuézhě dòngjiàn · 都 is an adverb, and that explains everything</div>
      <p>都 is grammatically an adverb, so it obeys the adverb rule: directly before the verb, after the subject. It totalizes leftward, sweeping up whatever plural thing stands before it. That is why ✗都我们去 is impossible: with nothing to its left inside the clause, 都 has nothing to quantify. It is also why the object version of "all" fronts the object: 这些菜我都喜欢 (these dishes, I like them all) moves 这些菜 before 都 so the adverb can reach it.</p>
      <p>Once 都 is understood as "at the verb, scoping left", the co-occurrence rules stop being arbitrary. 每个学生都来了 is belt and suspenders: 每 defines the set member by member, 都 confirms at the verb that no member is missing. Standard Mandarin wants both; dropping 都 after a quantified subject is the error native ears catch first.</p>
    </div>

        <div class="card-head">
          <span class="card-cn">都</span>
          <span class="card-py">dōu</span>
          <span class="card-en">all, in every case: the workhorse</span>
        </div>
        <div class="tags"><span class="tag tag-adv">副词 adverb</span></div>
        <div class="card-def">
          The single most important quantity word in Mandarin. 都 stands before the verb and asserts that the predicate holds for every member of whatever is to its left: 他们都是学生 (they are all students). It also totalizes time (每次都迟到, late every time) and doubles as "even" in the 连…都 construction. Full entry: 都.
        </div>

          <div class="example">
            <div class="ex-cn">我们都喜欢吃中国菜。</div>
            <div class="ex-py">Wǒmen dōu xǐhuan chī Zhōngguó cài.</div>
            <div class="ex-en">We all like Chinese food.</div>
          </div>
          <div class="example">
            <div class="ex-cn">这些书我都看过了。</div>
            <div class="ex-py">Zhèxiē shū wǒ dōu kàn guo le.</div>
            <div class="ex-en">I've read all of these books.</div>
          </div>
        </div>
      </div>

      <div class="card c-ochre">
        <div class="card-head">
          <span class="card-cn">每</span>
          <span class="card-py">měi</span>
          <span class="card-en">every, each one of an identical series</span>
        </div>
        <div class="tags"><span class="tag tag-adj">限定 determiner</span></div>
        <div class="card-def">
          每 walks through a set member by member: 每个人 (every person), 每本书 (every book). It requires a measure word before the noun, with a short list of exceptions that are themselves measure-like: 每天, 每年, 每次 (day, year, time). As a subject quantifier it wants 都 at the verb: 每个房间都有空调 (every room has air conditioning).
        </div>
        <div class="examples">
          <div class="example">
            <div class="ex-cn">每个孩子都需要鼓励。</div>
            <div class="ex-py">Měi gè háizi dōu xūyào gǔlì.</div>
            <div class="ex-en">Every child needs encouragement.</div>
          </div>
          <div class="example">
            <div class="ex-cn">我每天都跑步。</div>
            <div class="ex-py">Wǒ měitiān dōu pǎobù.</div>
            <div class="ex-en">I run every day.</div>
          </div>
        </div>
        <div class="note">
          <span class="note-label">辨析 biànxī · 每 vs. 各</span>
          每 treats members as interchangeable instances: 每个学生都交了作业 (every student handed in homework, no exceptions). 各 gè stresses their separateness and variety, attaches to a smaller set of nouns, and usually skips 都: 各国有各国的国情 (each country has its own circumstances). 每 counts; 各 differentiates.
        </div>
      </div>

      <div class="card c-teal">
        <div class="card-head">
          <span class="card-cn">所有 · 全部</span>
          <span class="card-py">suǒyǒu · quánbù</span>
          <span class="card-en">all of, the entirety of a set</span>
        </div>
        <div class="tags"><span class="tag tag-adj">限定 determiner</span></div>
        <div class="card-def">
          所有(的) + noun names the complete set up front: 所有的学生都来了 (all the students came). 所有 began as "that which is possessed" (所 + 有, what one has), and the possessive flavor survives in 我所有的钱 (all the money I have). 全部 quánbù is its close synonym, slightly more formal, happy as a standalone noun: 这是全部 (this is all of it). Both call for 都 when the phrase is the subject.
        </div>
        <div class="examples">
          <div class="example">
            <div class="ex-cn">所有的座位都卖完了。</div>
            <div class="ex-py">Suǒyǒu de zuòwèi dōu mài wán le.</div>
            <div class="ex-en">All the seats are sold out.</div>
          </div>
          <div class="example">
            <div class="ex-cn">他把全部时间都花在工作上。</div>
            <div class="ex-py">Tā bǎ quánbù shíjiān dōu huā zài gōngzuò shàng.</div>
            <div class="ex-en">He spends all his time on work.</div>
          </div>
        </div>
      </div>

      <div class="card c-violet">
        <div class="card-head">
          <span class="card-cn">全 · 整</span>
          <span class="card-py">quán · zhěng</span>
          <span class="card-en">whole vs. entire-and-unbroken</span>
        </div>
        <div class="tags"><span class="tag tag-adj">限定 determiner</span></div>
        <div class="card-def">
          全 fuses directly onto nouns to mean "the whole": 全家 (the whole family), 全国 (the whole country), 全世界 (the whole world). 整 zhěng insists the unit is complete and uncut: 整个 (the entire), 整天 (all day long), 整整三年 (three full years). The contrast: 全班都及格了 quantifies the members (everyone in the class passed); 整个班 pictures the class as one undivided block.

        <div class="card-def">

          <div class="example">

            <div class="ex-en">On weekends the park is crowded.</div>
          </div>
          <div class="example">
            <div class="ex-cn">这次旅行花了不少钱。</div>
            <div class="ex-py">Zhè cì lǚxíng huā le bù shǎo qián.</div>
            <div class="ex-en">This trip cost quite a bit of money.</div>
          </div>
        </div>
      </div>

      <div class="card c-sienna">
        <div class="card-head">
          <span class="card-cn">大多数 · 少数</span>
          <span class="card-py">dàduōshù · shǎoshù</span>
          <span class="card-en">the majority · the minority</span>
        </div>
        <div class="tags"><span class="tag tag-n">数量 quantity</span></div>
        <div class="card-def">
          大多数 (the great majority) and 少数 (the few) partition a set by proportion: 大多数人都同意 (most people agree), with the majority phrase taking 都 like any near-universal. 少数 appears in the fixed civic vocabulary 少数民族 (minority nationalities, China's official term for its 55 recognized non-Han groups). The proverb-shaped 少数服从多数 (the minority defers to the majority) is the standard formula for votes.
        </div>
        <div class="examples">

      <div class="card c-red">
        <div class="card-head">
          <span class="card-cn">谁都 · 什么都 · 哪儿都</span>
          <span class="card-py">shéi dōu · shénme dōu · nǎr dōu</span>
          <span class="card-en">question words as quantifiers: everyone / nothing / nowhere</span>
        </div>
        <div class="tags"><span class="tag tag-v">疑问词 wh-quantifier</span></div>
        <div class="card-def">
          A question word plus 都 stops asking and starts quantifying. Affirmative, it means "every": 谁都认识他 (everyone knows him), 哪儿都行 (anywhere is fine). Negated, it means "none": 谁都不知道 (nobody knows), 我什么都没吃 (I ate nothing), 哪儿都不想去 (don't want to go anywhere). 也 can replace 都 in the negative versions with a slightly stronger "not even" flavor.
        </div>
        <div class="examples">
          <div class="example">
            <div class="ex-cn">这件事谁都不知道。</div>
            <div class="ex-py">Zhè jiàn shì shéi dōu bù zhīdào.</div>
            <div class="ex-en">Nobody knows about this.</div>
          </div>
          <div class="example">

            <div class="ex-en">He said nothing today.</div>
          </div>
        </div>

      <div class="card c-teal">
        <div class="card-head">
          <span class="card-cn">一 + 量词 + 也/都 + 不/没</span>
          <span class="card-py">yī + MW + yě/dōu + bù/méi</span>
          <span class="card-en">not even one: the emphatic zero</span>
        </div>
        <div class="tags"><span class="tag tag-v">强调 emphatic</span></div>
        <div class="card-def">
          The strongest "none" in the language: put 一 + measure word (+ noun) before 也 or 都, then negate. 一个人也没来 (not a single person came), 我一分钱都没有 (I don't have a single cent), 他一句话也不说 (he won't say a single word). The 一-phrase must precede the negation; ✗没来一个人 loses the "not even" force. This is the pattern behind the textbook sentence 我一点儿也不累 (I'm not tired at all).
        </div>
        <div class="examples">
          <div class="example">
            <div class="ex-cn">冰箱里一点儿吃的都没有。</div>

    <span class="section-anchor" id="scope"></span>
    <div class="section-head">
      <span class="sh-cn">都不 与 不都</span>
      <span class="sh-py">dōu bù yǔ bù dōu</span>
      <span class="sh-en">都不 vs. 不都: Where Negation Lands</span>
      <span class="sh-rule"></span>
    </div>

    <div class="pattern">
      <span class="label">辖域 xiáyù · Order decides meaning</span>
      <strong>都 + 不</strong> → negation inside the totality → 他们<strong>都不</strong>来 (none of them are coming)<br>
      <strong>不 + 都</strong> → negation of the totality → 他们<strong>不都</strong>来 (not all of them are coming; some are)<br>
      Same three words, opposite worlds: word order is the only difference.
    </div>

    <div class="scholar" data-glyph="不">
      <div class="scholar-label">学者洞见 xuézhě dòngjiàn · A logic lesson in two syllables</div>
      <p>都不 asserts "for every one of them: not". 不都 asserts "not: for every one of them". Formal logic writes these ∀¬ and ¬∀ and spends a lecture on the difference; Mandarin encodes it purely by linear order, whichever word comes first takes the wider scope. English needs a paraphrase ("none of them" vs. "not all of them") to keep the two apart.</p>
      <p>The same ordering logic runs through the whole adverb system: 也不 (also not) vs. 不也 (isn't it also…?), 还不 (still not) vs. 不还 (rhetorical "don't you still…"). Getting 都不 vs. 不都 right is the cleanest early test of whether a learner has internalized that Mandarin adverbs scope rightward over everything that follows them.</p>
    </div>

        <span class="cy-note">应有 (what ought to be there) + 尽有 (is exhaustively there). The stock phrase for a well-provisioned shop or feature-complete product: 这家超市商品应有尽有 "This supermarket has everything you could want." The structural mirror of 一无所有: total presence against total absence.</span>
