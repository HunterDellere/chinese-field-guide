faq:
  - q: "What is the Art of War (孙子兵法)?"
    a: "孙子兵法 Sūnzǐ Bīngfǎ, known in English as The Art of War, is a Chinese military treatise of 13 chapters traditionally attributed to the strategist 孙武 Sun Wu (Sunzi). Composed around the late Spring and Autumn period (roughly 5th century BCE), it is the oldest known work of military strategy and the most influential. Rather than tactics for a particular battle, it sets out general principles: that war is a vital matter of state to be entered reluctantly, that the highest skill is to win without fighting, and that victory depends on knowing both yourself and your enemy."
  - q: "Who was Sun Tzu?"
    a: "孙武 Sun Wu, honoured as 孙子 Sunzi ('Master Sun'), is the strategist to whom the Art of War is attributed. Tradition places him as a general serving the southern state of 吴 Wu in the late 6th century BCE. The 史记 records a famous (and likely legendary) story in which he demonstrated discipline to the king of Wu by drilling the palace concubines into soldiers. Modern scholars debate whether a single historical Sun Wu wrote the text or whether it accumulated within a strategic tradition; either way the work is genuinely ancient and coherent in outlook."
  - q: "What is the most famous idea in the Art of War?"
    a: "Two ideas dominate. The first is 不战而屈人之兵 ('to subdue the enemy without fighting'): the supreme excellence is to win by strategy, diplomacy, and positioning rather than by costly battle. The second is 知己知彼，百战不殆 ('know yourself and know your enemy, and in a hundred battles you will never be in peril'): accurate knowledge of both sides is the foundation of all sound strategy. The book also teaches that 兵者，诡道也, 'warfare is the way of deception.'"
  - q: "Why is the Art of War still read today?"
    a: "Because its principles are pitched at the level of strategy in general, not just ancient warfare. It treats conflict as something to be won through preparation, information, positioning, timing, and economy of force rather than brute strength, and it stresses minimising cost and avoiding prolonged struggle. These ideas transfer readily to business competition, negotiation, law, sport, and politics, which is why the text is taught in military academies and business schools worldwide and remains one of the most cited Chinese books outside China."

      <span class="topic-hero-eyebrow">Philosophy · 哲学 zhéxué</span>
      <h1 class="topic-hero-title">孙子兵法</h1>
      <span class="topic-hero-title-py">sūnzǐ bīngfǎ</span>
      <p class="topic-hero-desc">Thirteen terse chapters on conflict, attributed to the strategist Sun Wu, that became the oldest and most influential military treatise in the world. Its central teaching is that the supreme art of war is to subdue the enemy without fighting at all.</p>
    </header>

    <!-- ── TEXT ── -->
    <span class="section-anchor" id="text"></span>
    <div class="section-head">
      <span class="sh-cn">这本书</span>
      <span class="sh-py">zhè běn shū</span>
      <span class="sh-en">The Text — Thirteen Chapters</span>
      <span class="sh-rule"></span>
    </div>

    <div class="scholar" data-glyph="兵">
      <div class="scholar-label">文本结构 wénběn jiégòu · Structure of the Text</div>
      <p>兵法 bīngfǎ means, literally, "soldier methods," the art and science of military force; 孙子 is "Master Sun." The 孙子兵法 is thus "Master Sun's methods of war." It is a short work, 13 chapters of compressed maxims, that reads less like a manual of drills than a distilled philosophy of conflict. Each chapter takes a single dimension of war (planning, the conduct of war, strategic attack, dispositions, force, weaknesses and strengths, manoeuvre, terrain, the use of spies) and states its principles in dense, aphoristic lines meant to be memorised and turned over.</p>
      <p>What makes the book ancient is not just its date but its outlook. It belongs to the late 春秋 (Spring and Autumn) and early 战国 (Warring States) world, when warfare in China was shifting from ritualised chariot engagements between aristocrats toward mass infantry armies, professional generals, and total mobilisation of the state. The 孙子兵法 is the first text to treat war as a coldly rational problem of statecraft, to be analysed, calculated, and minimised rather than glorified. Its opening words set this tone: 兵者，国之大事 ("war is the great affair of the state"), a matter of life and death, to be studied with the utmost seriousness and entered with the utmost reluctance.</p>
      <p>The received text was confirmed and clarified by a major archaeological find: in 1972, bamboo-strip versions of the 孙子兵法 were excavated at Yinque Mountain (银雀山) in Shandong from a Han-era tomb, settling old doubts about the text's antiquity and integrity. The work also became the first and most important of the 武经七书, the Seven Military Classics canonised for the imperial military examinations.</p>
    </div>

    <!-- ── SUN WU ── -->
    <span class="section-anchor" id="sunwu"></span>
    <div class="section-head">
      <span class="sh-cn">孙武其人</span>
      <span class="sh-py">sūnwǔ qírén</span>
      <span class="sh-en">Sun Wu — The Strategist of Wu</span>
      <span class="sh-rule"></span>
    </div>

    <div class="scholar" data-glyph="孙">
      <div class="scholar-label">作者问题 zuòzhě wèntí · The Author Question</div>
      <p>Tradition names the author 孙武 Sun Wu, a general from the state of 齐 Qi who took service with 吴 Wu in the late 6th century BCE under King Helü 阖闾. The 史记 of Sima Qian preserves the most famous story about him, almost certainly legendary but revealing of his reputation. To test his claim that he could make soldiers of anyone, the king handed him 180 palace women to drill. Sun Wu appointed two of the king's favourite concubines as company commanders and gave the order to turn right; the women only laughed. He explained the order again, clearly, and repeated it; again they laughed. He then declared that when orders are clear and still disobeyed, the fault lies with the officers, and he had the two favourites beheaded over the king's frantic objection. The remaining women performed every drill in perfect silence. The point of the story is the book's own: discipline is the precondition of any strategy, and a general's authority, once delegated, must be absolute even against the sovereign's wishes.</p>
      <p>As with Laozi and the 道德经, modern scholars are uncertain whether a single historical Sun Wu wrote the whole text or whether it crystallised within a school of military thought, possibly associated with his descendant Sun Bin 孙膑, another famous strategist. The debate does not affect the work's coherence: whoever assembled it, the 孙子兵法 speaks with one disciplined, unsentimental voice from beginning to end.</p>
    </div>

    <!-- ── WINNING ── -->
    <span class="section-anchor" id="winning"></span>
    <div class="section-head">
      <span class="sh-cn">不战而胜</span>
      <span class="sh-py">bù zhàn ér shèng</span>
      <span class="sh-en">Winning Without Fighting — The Supreme Skill</span>
      <span class="sh-rule"></span>
    </div>

    <div class="scholar" data-glyph="胜">
      <div class="scholar-label">核心思想 héxīn sīxiǎng · Core Idea</div>
      <p>The most distinctive teaching of the 孙子兵法 is that battle is the worst, not the best, way to win a war. 百战百胜，非善之善者也；不战而屈人之兵，善之善者也, "to win a hundred battles in a hundred fights is not the highest excellence; to subdue the enemy's army without fighting is the highest excellence." Fighting is costly, uncertain, and destructive even in victory. The truly skilled commander wins before the armies ever close, by destroying the enemy's plans, breaking his alliances, and rendering his position hopeless, so that he must yield without a battle.</p>
      <p>From this flows a clear hierarchy of strategy: the best policy is to attack the enemy's strategy itself; next best is to attack his alliances; next is to attack his army in the field; the worst of all is to besiege walled cities, which is slow, ruinous, and bloody. War, for Sunzi, is an economic problem before it is a heroic one. Prolonged campaigns drain the treasury, exhaust the people, and invite other powers to strike. Hence the counterintuitive maxim that there is no instance of a country benefiting from prolonged warfare, and that the wise general wins quickly or not at all.</p>
      <p>This is why the 孙子兵法 reads as much as a work of philosophy and statecraft as of military tactics. Its deepest counsel is to make force unnecessary: to position oneself so advantageously, through preparation and information, that the outcome is decided before the first blow. The aim is not glory in battle but the preservation of one's own state and, ideally, the enemy's, taken whole rather than shattered.</p>
    </div>

    <!-- ── KNOWING ── -->
    <span class="section-anchor" id="knowing"></span>
    <div class="section-head">
      <span class="sh-cn">知己知彼</span>
      <span class="sh-py">zhī jǐ zhī bǐ</span>
      <span class="sh-en">Know Self and Enemy — Strategy Built on Knowledge</span>
      <span class="sh-rule"></span>
    </div>

    <div class="scholar" data-glyph="知">
      <div class="scholar-label">情报与计算 qíngbào yǔ jìsuàn · Knowledge and Calculation</div>
      <p>The book's single most quoted line is its theory of knowledge: 知己知彼，百战不殆, "know yourself and know the enemy, and in a hundred battles you will never be in peril." The pairing is deliberate and complete. Know only the enemy and not yourself, and you will win some and lose some. Know neither, and you will lose every time. Sound strategy rests on accurate, unflinching assessment of both sides: their strengths, their weaknesses, their supplies, their morale, their commanders, their terrain.</p>
      <p>This is why the 孙子兵法 ends with a chapter on the use of spies (用间), which it treats not as a dirty necessity but as the cheapest and most decisive of all expenditures. To begrudge the cost of intelligence while spending fortunes on armies, it argues, is the height of inhumanity, because ignorance is what gets armies destroyed. Knowing the enemy's dispositions in advance is what lets a general win without fighting.</p>
      <p>The same insistence on calculation runs through the opening chapter, which lists the five factors to weigh before any campaign: the Way (道, whether the people are united with their ruler), Heaven (天, the seasons and weather), Earth (地, the terrain), the Commander (将), and Method and discipline (法). A general who masters these will win; one who ignores them will lose. War, for Sunzi, is decided in the planning tent before it is decided on the field.</p>
    </div>

    <!-- ── DECEPTION ── -->
    <span class="section-anchor" id="deception"></span>
    <div class="section-head">
      <span class="sh-cn">诡道</span>
      <span class="sh-py">guǐ dào</span>
      <span class="sh-en">The Way of Deception — Shaping Perception</span>
      <span class="sh-rule"></span>
    </div>

    <div class="scholar" data-glyph="诡">
      <div class="scholar-label">兵不厌诈 bīng bù yàn zhà · War Does Not Tire of Deceit</div>
      <p>兵者，诡道也, "warfare is the way of deception." This blunt line, near the start of the book, marks the 孙子兵法 as utterly without illusions about the chivalric ideals of an earlier age. The competent commander shapes what the enemy believes. When able, he appears unable; when active, he appears inactive; when near, he makes the enemy think he is far. He lures with bait, feigns disorder to invite an attack, and strikes where he is not expected. The whole aim is to keep the enemy uncertain and reactive while one's own intentions stay hidden.</p>
      <p>This connects to the book's prizing of 形 (form, disposition) and 势 (force, momentum, configuration of advantage). The master strategist makes his own position formless and unreadable, like water that has no fixed shape and takes its form from the ground it flows over: 兵无常势，水无常形. He concentrates strength against the enemy's weakness, avoids strength, and times his strike for the moment of maximum momentum, the way a drawn-back current releases all at once. Victory, for Sunzi, is not a matter of fighting harder but of arranging conditions so that winning becomes almost effortless, the natural result of superior positioning.</p>
    </div>

    <!-- ── LEGACY ── -->
    <span class="section-anchor" id="legacy"></span>
    <div class="section-head">
      <span class="sh-cn">影响</span>
      <span class="sh-py">yǐngxiǎng</span>
      <span class="sh-en">Legacy — From Battlefield to Boardroom</span>
      <span class="sh-rule"></span>
    </div>

    <div class="scholar" data-glyph="法">
      <div class="scholar-label">历史影响 lìshǐ yǐngxiǎng · Historical Impact</div>
      <p>Within China, the 孙子兵法 became the foundation of all later military thought and the lead text of the Seven Military Classics. Generals from the 三国 strategist 诸葛亮 Zhuge Liang onward absorbed its principles, and its phrases entered ordinary speech as chengyu: 出其不意 ("strike where unexpected"), 速战速决 ("fight fast, settle fast"), 知己知彼. Its influence carried into the Chinese strategic tradition through every dynasty and shaped how conflict, competition, and even diplomacy were conceived.</p>
      <p>Its reach beyond China is unusual for a classical Chinese text. It travelled early to Japan, where it shaped samurai strategy and is still studied, and to Korea and Vietnam. A French Jesuit translation reached Europe in the 18th century, and by the 20th the book was read closely in military academies worldwide. Mao Zedong drew on it for his theory of protracted, asymmetric war; Western generals cited it in the Gulf wars.</p>
      <p>In recent decades the 孙子兵法 has had a second career entirely outside warfare. Because its principles concern strategy in the abstract (information, positioning, timing, economy of force, winning without direct confrontation), they map readily onto business competition, negotiation, litigation, sport, and politics. It is one of the few ancient texts taught in both war colleges and business schools, and one of the most widely translated Chinese works in the world, a 2,500-year-old book that still functions as a practical guide to any contest of wills.</p>
    </div>

      <span class="sh-cn">名句</span>

      <span class="sh-en">Famous Lines — The Most Quoted Maxims</span>

    <div class="chengyu-grid">

      <div class="cy">
        <span class="cy-cn">知己知彼，百战不殆。</span>
        <span class="cy-py">zhī jǐ zhī bǐ, bǎi zhàn bù dài.</span>
        <span class="cy-en">Know yourself and know the enemy, and in a hundred battles you will never be in peril.</span>
        <span class="cy-note">The single most quoted line in the Art of War and one of the best-known Chinese sayings worldwide. The full passage continues that one who knows only himself will win some and lose some, and one who knows neither will lose every time. It has become a general maxim for any competitive situation: thorough knowledge of both your own position and your opponent's is the foundation of success. Now a standard chengyu, 知己知彼.</span>
      </div>

      <div class="cy">
        <span class="cy-cn">不战而屈人之兵，善之善者也。</span>
        <span class="cy-py">bù zhàn ér qū rén zhī bīng, shàn zhī shàn zhě yě.</span>
        <span class="cy-en">To subdue the enemy without fighting is the supreme excellence.</span>
        <span class="cy-note">The core of Sunzi's strategic philosophy: the highest skill is to win before any battle is fought, by breaking the enemy's strategy and alliances and rendering his position hopeless. Battle is the costly last resort, not the goal. The line is endlessly cited in modern strategy, negotiation, and management as the principle that the best victory is the one that costs nothing to win.</span>
      </div>

      <div class="cy">
        <span class="cy-cn">兵者，诡道也。</span>
        <span class="cy-py">bīng zhě, guǐ dào yě.</span>
        <span class="cy-en">Warfare is the way of deception.</span>
        <span class="cy-note">A blunt rejection of chivalric ideals: success in conflict depends on shaping the enemy's perceptions, concealing one's own strength and intentions, and striking where one is not expected. The related everyday saying is 兵不厌诈, "in war one never tires of deceit." Cited whenever the legitimacy of strategic misdirection, in any contest, is at issue.</span>
      </div>

      <div class="cy">
        <span class="cy-cn">兵无常势，水无常形。</span>
        <span class="cy-py">bīng wú cháng shì, shuǐ wú cháng xíng.</span>
        <span class="cy-en">An army has no fixed configuration, as water has no fixed shape.</span>
        <span class="cy-note">Sunzi's image for strategic adaptability, strikingly close to the water imagery of the Daodejing. As water takes its shape from the ground it flows over, the skilled commander has no rigid plan but adapts his dispositions to the enemy and the terrain, concentrating against weakness and never repeating a formula. The line is the classic statement of flexibility as the heart of strategy.</span>
      </div>

      <div class="cy">
        <span class="cy-cn">出其不意，攻其无备。</span>
        <span class="cy-py">chū qí bù yì, gōng qí wú bèi.</span>
        <span class="cy-en">Emerge where unexpected; attack where unprepared.</span>
        <span class="cy-note">From the first chapter, now the standard chengyu 出其不意 ("catch off guard"). The principle is to direct one's effort at the point and moment the opponent has not anticipated and cannot defend, turning surprise itself into a decisive advantage. Used far beyond warfare for any move that succeeds by being unexpected.</span>
      </div>
