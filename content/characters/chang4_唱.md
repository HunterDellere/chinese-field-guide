    <span class="section-anchor" id="etymology"></span>
    <div class="section-head"><span class="sh-cn">字源</span><span class="sh-py">zìyuán</span><span class="sh-en">Etymology &amp; Structure</span><span class="sh-rule"></span></div>

    <div class="scholar" data-glyph="唱">
      <div class="scholar-label">字源洞见 zìyuán dòngjiàn · Etymological Insight</div>
      <p><strong>唱 = 口 + 昌</strong>, a clean pictophonetic. The mouth radical 口 (kǒu) sits on the left and names the act: this is something done with the open mouth. On the right, 昌 (chāng) is the phonetic, supplying the sound the whole character borrows. The Shuōwén Jiězì glosses 唱 simply as 導也, to lead, the lead voice that the chorus follows.</p>
      <p>The phonetic 昌 is itself two suns stacked, 日 over 日, an image of brightness and flourishing prosperity. It carries no meaning into 唱 beyond its sound, yet the pairing is fortunate: a bright, rising thing voiced through the mouth. In the seal script the left element is unmistakably the boxed mouth, the right a doubled radiant disc.</p>
      <p>From the core sense of singing, 唱 extended outward along the line of the carrying voice. To 唱 is to call a name aloud at roll-call (唱名), to lead a tune that others take up, to announce a price at auction. The thread is always the same: one voice projected so that others receive it and respond.</p>
      <p>Japanese keeps 唱 in 合唱 (gasshō, chorus) and 唱える (tonaeru, to chant or advocate); Korean reads it 창 (chang), the 창 of 합창 (hapchang, chorus) and 창가 (changga, a sung anthem). Across all three the mouth radical holds the meaning steady while only the sound shifts.</p>

    <div class="scholar" data-glyph="唱" style="margin-top:2.5rem;">
      <div class="scholar-label">记忆法 jìyìfǎ · Master Retention Image</div>
      <p>Picture a street singer at dawn. On the left of her stands an open mouth, 口, rounded into an O as the first note leaves it. Behind her the sky shows two suns stacked one above the other, 昌, the morning blaze doubling its own light. The mouth gives the singing; the twin suns give only the bright, rising sound that carries the word.</p>
      <p>Hold that split. The meaning lives entirely in the little square 口 on the left, the part you can see making the sound. The right half, 昌, is borrowed light: it tells your ear the syllable is near "chāng" and pushes it to the falling fourth tone, chàng, the voice dropping at the end of the breath.</p>
      <p>Now let the crowd gather. One mouth becomes many in 合唱, voices joined; the bright disc spins flat in 唱片, the singing pressed onto a record; and on stage it grows into 演唱, the performed song. Every one of them keeps that same open mouth on the left, the act of voicing held steady while the world around it changes.</p>
