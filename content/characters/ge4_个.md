    <!-- ── ETYMOLOGY ── -->
    <span class="section-anchor" id="etymology"></span>
    <div class="section-head">
      <span class="sh-cn">字源</span>
      <span class="sh-py">zìyuán</span>
      <span class="sh-en">Etymology &amp; Structure</span>
      <span class="sh-rule"></span>
    </div>

    <div class="scholar" data-glyph="个">
      <div class="scholar-label">字源洞见 zìyuán dòngjiàn · Etymological Insight</div>
      <p><strong>个 gè traces back to a single stalk of bamboo.</strong> The traditional form 個 is a phonetic-semantic compound: 亻 (rén, person) supplies the human sense of a single individual, and 固 (gù) supplies the sound. But the simplified 个 reaches further back, to an old graph that pictured one half of 竹 (zhú, bamboo). 竹 is two bamboo leaves side by side; split it down the middle and you are left with the shape of 个, a single drooping leaf. Counting stalks one by one is one of the oldest uses of a tally word, which is exactly the job 个 still does.</p>
      <p>From "one stalk, one piece" the character settles into two everyday roles:</p>
      <p><strong>1. The universal measure word.</strong> Chinese counts nouns with a classifier between the number and the noun, and 个 is the default one, reached for whenever no more specific classifier fits: 一个人 yí ge rén (one person), 一个问题 yí ge wèntí (one question), 三个苹果 sān ge píngguǒ (three apples). In speech it is usually neutral-toned (ge) and is by far the most common classifier in the language.<br>
      <strong>2. Individual, single.</strong> The same "one piece" sense, applied to a single member of a group: 个人 gèrén (the individual; personal), 个性 gèxìng (personality, what is particular to one person), 个别 gèbié (individual; a few).</p>
      <p>The thread is singleness. Whether 个 is tallying apples or naming the lone self set apart from the crowd, it picks out one unit from many. To count a thing is to treat it as a discrete piece, and that same act of picking out one is what makes 个 the root of "the individual."</p>
    </div>

    <!-- ── CLASSIFIER ── -->
    <span class="section-anchor" id="classifier"></span>
    <div class="section-head">
      <span class="sh-cn">一个</span>
      <span class="sh-py">yí gè</span>
      <span class="sh-en">The Universal Measure Word</span>
      <span class="sh-rule"></span>
    </div>

    <div class="scholar" data-glyph="个">
      <div class="scholar-label">语法洞见 yǔfǎ dòngjiàn · Grammatical Insight</div>
      <p>Chinese does not say "three apples" directly. It says "three [classifier] apples," 三个苹果, with a measure word naming the kind of thing being counted. Many nouns take a specific classifier: 本 běn for bound books, 张 zhāng for flat sheets, 只 zhī for many animals, 条 tiáo for long thin things. 个 gè is the general-purpose default, the one you reach for when no special classifier comes to mind, and the one that covers people, abstract ideas, and most ordinary objects.</p>
      <p>Because it fits almost anywhere, 个 is the first classifier a learner meets and the safest fallback in conversation: 一个人 (a person), 一个想法 (an idea), 一个星期 (a week), 那个东西 (that thing). It is also the workhorse behind 这个 zhège (this one) and 那个 nàge (that one). When 个 is doing this counting job, it usually drops to a neutral tone (ge) in fluent speech, even though its citation tone is the falling fourth (gè).</p>
    </div>

      <!-- 一个 -->
      <div class="card c-red">
        <div class="card-head">
          <span class="card-cn">一个</span>
          <span class="card-py">yí ge</span>
          <span class="card-en">one (of something); a; an</span>
        </div>
        <div class="tags"><span class="tag tag-n">MW 量词 liàngcí</span></div>
        <div class="card-def">
          一 yī (one) + 个 ge (measure word). The most basic count phrase in the language, sitting before a noun: 一个人 "one person," 一个苹果 "one apple." Note the sandhi: before the fourth-tone 个, the 一 shifts to a rising second tone (yí).
        </div>
        <div class="examples">
          <div class="example">

            <div class="ex-en">I would like to buy an apple.</div>
          </div>
          <div class="example">
            <div class="ex-cn">这是一个很好的问题。</div>
            <div class="ex-py">Zhè shì yí ge hěn hǎo de wèntí.</div>
            <div class="ex-en">This is a very good question.</div>
          </div>
        </div>
      </div>

      <!-- 这个 -->
      <div class="card c-red">
        <div class="card-head">
          <span class="card-cn">这个</span>
          <span class="card-py">zhège</span>
          <span class="card-en">this; this one</span>
        </div>
        <div class="tags"><span class="tag tag-n">PRON 代词 dàicí</span></div>
        <div class="card-def">
          这 zhè (this) + 个 ge. The demonstrative "this one," with 个 supplying the classifier slot. Its pair is 那个 nàge (that one). Often heard as a filler too, like an English "um."
        </div>
        <div class="examples">
          <div class="example">
            <div class="ex-cn">这个多少钱？</div>
            <div class="ex-py">Zhège duōshao qián?</div>
            <div class="ex-en">How much is this one?</div>
          </div>
        </div>
      </div>

      <!-- 几个 -->
      <div class="card c-teal">
        <div class="card-head">
          <span class="card-cn">几个</span>
          <span class="card-py">jǐ ge</span>
          <span class="card-en">a few; several; how many</span>
        </div>
        <div class="tags"><span class="tag tag-n">MW 量词 liàngcí</span></div>
        <div class="card-def">
          几 jǐ (how many; a few) + 个 ge. Either a question ("how many?") or a vague small number ("a few"), depending on context: 几个人 "how many people?" or "a few people."
        </div>
        <div class="examples">
          <div class="example">
            <div class="ex-cn">你有几个问题？</div>
            <div class="ex-py">Nǐ yǒu jǐ ge wèntí?</div>
            <div class="ex-en">How many questions do you have?</div>
          </div>
        </div>
        <div class="note">
          <span class="note-label">辨析 biànxī · 个 vs. 本 / 张 / 只</span>
          <strong>个</strong> is the catch-all classifier; the others are specific. Use <strong>本</strong> běn for bound books (一本书), <strong>张</strong> zhāng for flat sheets like paper or tables (一张纸), and <strong>只</strong> zhī for many animals and one of a pair (一只猫). When a specific classifier exists, it is preferred; 个 is the safe default when none comes to mind.
        </div>

    <div class="scholar" data-glyph="个" style="margin-top:2.5rem;">
      <div class="scholar-label">记忆法 jìyìfǎ · Master Retention Image</div>
      <p>See one stalk of bamboo standing alone, a single leaf drooping from it. That lone stalk is 个: one piece, picked out from the grove. To count is to pull out one stalk at a time, which is why 个 is the measure word you reach for when nothing more specific fits: 一个人, 一个问题, 一个想法.</p>
      <p>Now let the stalk become a person. The same act of singling out one unit gives you 个人 (the individual), 个性 (what makes that one person distinct), 个子 (the build of that single frame). Wherever 个 appears, look for the one piece set apart from the many: counted as a unit, or standing on its own as an individual.</p>
