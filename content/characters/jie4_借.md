    <span class="section-anchor" id="etymology"></span>
    <div class="section-head"><span class="sh-cn">字源</span><span class="sh-py">zìyuán</span><span class="sh-en">Etymology &amp; Structure</span><span class="sh-rule"></span></div>
    <div class="scholar" data-glyph="借">
      <div class="scholar-label">字源洞见 zìyuán dòngjiàn · Etymological Insight</div>
      <p>借 = 亻 + 昔. The left side is 亻, the standing form of 人 (rén), a person seen from the side. The right side is 昔 (xī), which supplies the sound. This is a textbook pictophonetic build: a human radical fixes the meaning in the world of people and exchange, and 昔 carries the pronunciation, drifting from <em>xī</em> to <em>jiè</em> over the centuries the way phonetic components often do.</p>
      <p>昔 itself once pictured layered, sun-dried strips of meat above a sun, the old graph for "former days, of old." That ancient sense leaves a faint residue in 借: a thing handed over now is owed back later, a transaction stretched across time. The person stands beside the marker of elapsed days, holding something that is theirs only on loan.</p>
      <p>The Shuōwén Jiězì glosses 借 as 假 (jiǎ), "to provisionally have," catching the heart of it. What you 借 is never permanently yours; you hold it on credit against a future return. From that single root the word swings both directions: the borrower 借 the thing in, the lender 借 the thing out, and only the surrounding words tell you which way the object moves.</p>
      <p>Japanese inherits the character as 借 (read <em>shaku</em> or <em>ka-riru</em>, "to borrow"), keeping the same two-sided ledger logic in compounds like 借金 (debt). The Chinese glyph never split borrow from lend into separate characters, so the doubled meaning is original, not a modern blur.</p>

    <span class="section-anchor" id="loan"></span>
    <div class="section-head"><span class="sh-cn">借贷</span><span class="sh-py">jièdài</span><span class="sh-en">Borrowing &amp; Lending</span><span class="sh-rule"></span></div>
    <div class="pattern">
      <p>One verb, two directions. 我借你的书 means "I borrow your book," while 我借你一本书 means "I lend you a book." The grammar, not the verb, sorts out who gives and who receives: 向/跟 marks the source you borrow <em>from</em>, while 给 or a direct recipient marks who you lend <em>to</em>.</p>
    </div>

    <span class="section-anchor" id="leverage"></span>
    <div class="section-head"><span class="sh-cn">凭借</span><span class="sh-py">píngjiè</span><span class="sh-en">Using &amp; Pretext</span><span class="sh-rule"></span></div>
    <div class="scholar" data-glyph="借">
      <div class="scholar-label">语义延伸 yǔyì yánshēn · Semantic Extension</div>
      <p>Stretch "to take on loan" one step and it becomes "to make use of," to lean on something that is not your own. You 借 the wind to fill a sail, 借 an occasion to make a point, 借 someone else's strength to lift your own weight. From there it slides once more into 借口, the borrowed mouth: an excuse you put on like a coat that does not belong to you.</p>
    </div>

    <div class="scholar" data-glyph="借" style="margin-top:2.5rem;">
      <div class="scholar-label">记忆法 jìyìfǎ · Master Retention Image</div>
      <p>Picture a lone figure (亻) standing at a counter at dusk, the day's sun (the bottom of 昔) already low behind a stack of dried, layered slips (the strips piled in 昔). On the counter sits something not theirs: a book, a coin, an umbrella. They slide it across. Whether it travels toward them or away depends on which way they are facing, and that ambiguity <em>is</em> the character.</p>
      <p>Hold the picture of elapsed time baked into 昔, "former days," and 借 always means a thing held against the clock. The umbrella borrowed at dusk must come back; the wind borrowed for the sail blows on only a while; the borrowed mouth of 借口 lasts exactly as long as the lie holds.</p>
      <p>Run the compounds off that single image and they line up: 借钱 slides money over the counter, 借助 reaches across it for a tool, 借鉴 props a mirror on it to study, and 借刀杀人 hands someone else the knife from your side of the table while your own hands stay empty.</p>
