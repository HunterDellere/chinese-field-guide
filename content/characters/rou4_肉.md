    <div class="section-head"><span class="sh-cn">字源</span><span class="sh-py">zìyuán</span><span class="sh-en">Etymology &amp; Structure</span><span class="sh-rule"></span></div>
    <div class="scholar" data-glyph="肉">
      <div class="scholar-label">字源洞见 zìyuán dòngjiàn · Etymological Insight</div>
      <p>The whole glyph is the picture: a slab of butchered meat. The outer frame is the cut of flesh, and the two short strokes inside are the marbling, the seams of fat and grain that run through muscle. Hold a piece of pork belly to the light and you see exactly what the oracle-bone scribe drew, the striations packed inside a rounded outline. 肉 is its own radical, classifier 130, the head of a family that covers every soft, fleshy part of an animal body.</p>
      <p>The seal script kept the slab whole and upright, the way the character still stands today when it means meat as a noun. But scribes who had to fit 肉 onto the left edge of a compound flattened and narrowed it until it became 月, the so-called flesh-moon. This 月 is a different beast from the 月 that means moon, though the two converged in shape. When you see 月 hugging the left side of a character about the body, it is this meat radical: 胖 (pàng, fat), 脸 (liǎn, face), 腿 (tuǐ, leg), 肝 (gān, liver), 胃 (wèi, stomach). Almost the entire vocabulary of human anatomy is built on this squeezed slab of meat.</p>
      <p>From the literal cut of flesh the sense fanned outward. It became flesh on a living body as opposed to bone (血肉, xuèròu, blood and flesh), then the soft yielding part of anything: the pulp of a peach (果肉, guǒròu), the meat of a walnut. The Shuōwén defines 肉 plainly as the flesh of birds and beasts, and every later sense is a step out from that knife-edge meaning.</p>
      <p>Japanese borrowed the character as 肉 (niku), where it still means meat, and the radical 月 / ⺼ does the same anatomical labor across Sino-Japanese vocabulary. The slab survives wherever the body is written.</p>

    <span class="section-anchor" id="kinds"></span>
    <div class="section-head"><span class="sh-cn">肉类</span><span class="sh-py">ròulèi</span><span class="sh-en">Kinds of Meat</span><span class="sh-rule"></span></div>
    <div class="pattern">
      <p>Name an animal, append 肉, and you have its meat. 牛 (niú, cow) plus 肉 is beef; 鸡 (jī, chicken) plus 肉 is chicken meat. The pattern is fully productive, which is why a beginner who knows the animals already knows the butcher's counter.</p>

    <span class="section-anchor" id="body"></span>
    <div class="section-head"><span class="sh-cn">血肉</span><span class="sh-py">xuèròu</span><span class="sh-en">Flesh &amp; Body</span><span class="sh-rule"></span></div>
    <div class="scholar" data-glyph="肉">
      <div class="scholar-label">引申义 yǐnshēnyì · Extended Sense</div>
      <p>Lifted off the cutting board, 肉 becomes the living flesh of a body, paired with 骨 (gǔ, bone) and 血 (xuè, blood) to mark the physical, mortal self against the spirit or the mind. This is the 肉 of 肉体 and 肉眼, the side of a person that can be touched, that ages, that dies.</p>
    </div>

    <div class="scholar" data-glyph="肉" style="margin-top:2.5rem;">
      <div class="scholar-label">记忆法 jìyìfǎ · Master Retention Image</div>
      <p>Set a thick slab of pork belly on the cutting board and look straight down at it. The rounded outline is the edge of the cut; the pale ribbons running through it are the marbling, the fat and grain. Those ribbons are the two strokes locked inside the frame of 肉. The whole character is a single piece of meat seen from above, seams and all.</p>
      <p>Now imagine pressing that slab flat against the left wall of a kitchen so it thins out into a strip. That strip is 月, the flesh-moon. Every body part written with 月 on its left, the 脸 (liǎn, face), the 腿 (tuǐ, leg), the 胃 (wèi, stomach), is the same slab, squeezed sideways to make room. When you meet 月 hugging the left edge of a body character, hear it as 肉: this is flesh, not moon.</p>
      <p>The slab then leaves the kitchen for the orchard. Slice a peach and the soft sweet part is 果肉 (guǒròu), the meat of the fruit. Whether it is muscle on a bone, pulp around a pit, or pork in a bowl, 肉 is always the same thing: the soft, yielding, edible body of a living thing.</p>
