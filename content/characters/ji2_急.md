    <span class="section-anchor" id="etymology"></span>
    <div class="section-head"><span class="sh-cn">字源</span><span class="sh-py">zìyuán</span><span class="sh-en">Etymology &amp; Structure</span><span class="sh-rule"></span></div>
    <div class="scholar" data-glyph="急">
      <div class="scholar-label">字源洞见 zìyuán dòngjiàn · Etymological Insight</div>
      <p>急 stacks two parts vertically: 急 = 刍 (above) + 心 (below). The 心 (xīn) heart sits at the foot of the glyph as the semantic anchor, and the upper element carries the sound. This is a heart with weight pressing down on it, and that pressure is the whole meaning of the character: the squeeze felt in the chest when time runs short.</p>
      <p>The 心 component is the same heart radical that grounds 想 (xiǎng, to think), 念 (niàn, to miss), and 怕 (pà, to fear). When 心 sits at the bottom of a stack rather than the left side, it keeps its full four-dot shape rather than collapsing into the 忄 form. Here it bears the pressure of the upper part literally, the way a chest tightens under stress.</p>
      <p>The sense radiates outward from one root feeling. First the inward state: anxious, worried, the heart racing (着急 zháojí). Then the outward tempo it produces: hasty, hurried, doing things fast because there is no time (急忙 jímáng). Then the situation that forces the pace: pressing, critical, an emergency (紧急 jǐnjí). The same character covers the inner fluster and the outer rush because, in the Chinese reading, they are one event: a heart under a deadline.</p>
      <p>Japanese borrowed the glyph as 急 (kyū / isogu), keeping both the speed sense (急行 kyūkō, express train) and the urgency sense intact. The 急行 of a Tokyo timetable and the 急诊 of a Beijing hospital trace back to the same pressed heart.</p>

        <div class="tags"><span class="tag tag-v">V 动词 dòngcí</span></div>
        <div class="card-def">着 (zháo, to be caught by) + 急 (urgency). To be seized by anxiety. This is the inward state of 急: a fretting heart, not a fast body. It is the most common everyday word for feeling worried over something time-sensitive.</div>

        </div>
        <div class="note"><span class="note-label">辨析 biànxī · 着急 vs. 担心</span>着急 (zháojí) is restless worry tied to the clock or to something happening now; 担心 (dānxīn) is a quieter, longer worry about how things will turn out.</div>

    <div class="scholar" data-glyph="急" style="margin-top:2.5rem;">
      <div class="scholar-label">记忆法 jìyìfǎ · Master Retention Image</div>
      <p>Picture a heart at the bottom of the glyph with a hand and a load stacked on top of it, bearing down. That is the shape of 急 read literally: the 心 heart pinned under weight at the foot of the character, with the upper element pressing it from above. The chest tightens, the pulse quickens, and the body wants to move now.</p>
      <p>Hold that one image of a squeezed heart and every compound falls out of it. Press the heart inward and you get the worry of 着急 (zháojí). Let the pressure spill into the limbs and you get the rush of 急忙 (jímáng). Tighten the whole situation around it and you get 紧急 (jǐnjí), the emergency. Send it to the hospital and you get 急诊 (jízhěn).</p>
      <p>When you read 急, do not picture speed first. Picture the heart that the deadline is squeezing. The speed is only what that heart does when it cannot sit still.</p>
