    <span class="section-anchor" id="etymology"></span>
    <div class="section-head"><span class="sh-cn">字源</span><span class="sh-py">zìyuán</span><span class="sh-en">Etymology &amp; Structure</span><span class="sh-rule"></span></div>
    <div class="scholar" data-glyph="姐">
      <div class="scholar-label">字源洞见 zìyuán dòngjiàn · Etymological Insight</div>
      <p>姐 is a pictophonetic built as ⿰女且: the 女 (nǚ, woman) on the left carries the meaning, and 且 (qiě) on the right carries the sound. The kneeling silhouette of 女 marks this as a word about a woman; 且, a stacked altar-table of offerings in the old graphs, is borrowed purely for its reading and lends nothing of its own ancestral sense to the gloss.</p>
      <p>The Shuōwén Jiězì lists 姐 under the 女 section and glosses it as a Shǔ (Sichuan) dialect word for mother. That regional "mother" reading faded; over later centuries the spoken language pulled 姐 toward "elder sister," and that is the meaning a child learns first today in 姐姐 (jiějie).</p>
      <p>From the concrete tie of one older girl in a household, the word widened outward. By the late imperial period 姐 had become a polite handle for any young woman, the root of 小姐 (xiǎojiě, miss) and of the warm street address 大姐 (dàjiě) thrown at a woman a little older than oneself. The semantic 女 anchors every one of these to the same idea: a female person, addressed with a touch of respect.</p>
    </div>

    <span class="section-anchor" id="address"></span>
    <div class="section-head"><span class="sh-cn">称呼</span><span class="sh-py">chēnghu</span><span class="sh-en">Forms of Address</span><span class="sh-rule"></span></div>
    <div class="scholar" data-glyph="姐">
      <div class="scholar-label">用法 yòngfǎ · Usage Note</div>
      <p>姐 is one of Chinese's busiest address words. Attach it to a surname and you get 王姐 (Wáng jiě, Sister Wang) for an older female colleague; attach it to a young woman's status and you get 小姐 (xiǎojiě). Online and among friends, women now call themselves 姐 to project confidence, as in 姐不缺钱 (jiě bù quē qián, this lady isn't short on cash).</p>
    </div>
    <div class="cards">
      <div class="card c-violet">
        <div class="card-head"><span class="card-cn">小姐</span><span class="card-py">xiǎojiě</span><span class="card-en">miss; young lady</span></div>
        <div class="tags"><span class="tag tag-n">N 名词 míngcí</span></div>
        <div class="card-def">小 (small, young) plus 姐 is the classic polite title for a young, unmarried woman, like English "Miss." Register matters: in some mainland contexts a bare 小姐 can sound off, so service settings often prefer 美女 (měinǚ) or a surname plus 姐.</div>
        <div class="examples">
          <div class="example"><div class="ex-cn">这位是新来的张小姐。</div><div class="ex-py">Zhè wèi shì xīn lái de Zhāng xiǎojiě.</div><div class="ex-en">This is Miss Zhang, who just joined.</div></div>
        </div>
        <div class="note"><span class="note-label">辨析 biànxī · register</span> As a standalone address, 小姐 carries unwanted overtones in parts of the mainland; pairing it with a surname (张小姐) keeps it safely polite.</div>

    <div class="scholar" data-glyph="姐" style="margin-top:2.5rem;">
      <div class="scholar-label">记忆法 jìyìfǎ · Master Retention Image</div>
      <p>Picture the kneeling 女 on the left as a young woman seated at the head of the family table. To her right stands 且, the old stacked altar of offering dishes, plate balanced on plate. She is the eldest daughter, the one trusted to set out the ancestral offerings before anyone else may eat.</p>
      <p>Hold that scene: woman beside the stacked altar means the senior girl, the 姐姐. From that single image every compound falls into place. Make her the biggest of the daughters and you get 大姐; shrink her to a polite stranger and she becomes 小姐; lift her into the sky beside the drink cart and she is 空姐. The woman radical never moves, and neither does the sense of a female person addressed with quiet respect.</p>
