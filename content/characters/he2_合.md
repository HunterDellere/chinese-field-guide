    <!-- ── ETYMOLOGY ── -->
    <span class="section-anchor" id="etymology"></span>
    <div class="section-head">
      <span class="sh-cn">字源</span>
      <span class="sh-py">zìyuán</span>
      <span class="sh-en">Etymology &amp; Structure</span>
      <span class="sh-rule"></span>
    </div>

    <div class="scholar" data-glyph="合">
      <div class="scholar-label">字源洞见 zìyuán dòngjiàn · Etymological Insight</div>
      <p><strong>合 hé pictures a lid coming down over the mouth of a vessel.</strong> The top element is 亼 (jí), an inverted, roof-like shape read as a cover, and below it sits 口 (kǒu), the round opening of a container drawn as a mouth. Set one over the other and you see a lid settling onto a jar so that the two edges meet and seal. The early sense is exactly that: two parts brought together so they fit and close. The simplified and traditional forms are identical; there is no separate traditional glyph.</p>
      <p>From that one image of edges meeting, three related senses spread across thousands of words:</p>
      <p><strong>1. To join, to combine.</strong> Two things brought into one: 合作 hézuò (to cooperate), 结合 jiéhé (to combine; to integrate), 联合 liánhé (to unite), 合并 hébìng (to merge).<br>
      <strong>2. To suit, to match, to fit.</strong> When the lid sits true on the jar, the parts agree: 合适 héshì (suitable), 适合 shìhé (to suit), 符合 fúhé (to conform to), 合理 hélǐ (reasonable).<br>
      <strong>3. To close; the whole.</strong> The closing motion itself, and the sum it produces: 合上 héshàng (to close up), 合眼 héyǎn (to close the eyes), 合计 héjì (to total up).</p>
      <p>The thread running through every sense is two edges meeting cleanly. Whether the parts are people (cooperation), measurements (a good fit), an argument (reasonable), or eyelids (shutting), 合 is always the moment when separate things come together and agree.</p>

    <!-- ── CLOSE ── -->
    <span class="section-anchor" id="close"></span>
    <div class="section-head">
      <span class="sh-cn">合并</span>
      <span class="sh-py">hébìng</span>
      <span class="sh-en">Close &amp; Merge into a Whole</span>
      <span class="sh-rule"></span>

    <div class="scholar" data-glyph="合">
      <div class="scholar-label">语义洞见 yǔyì dòngjiàn · Semantic Insight</div>
      <p>The most literal sense of 合 is the physical act of closing: bringing two edges together until they meet. 合上 héshàng means to close something that opens along a seam, a book or a lid or a door: 合上书 "to close the book." 合眼 héyǎn is to close the eyes, often a gentle way of saying to fall asleep or, in mourning, to pass away peacefully.</p>
      <p>From closing comes merging, and from merging comes the idea of a whole made of parts. 合并 hébìng joins two separate units into one: two companies, two departments, two cells. 综合 zōnghé gathers many strands into a single comprehensive view. Even 合唱 héchàng, a chorus, is voices closed together into one sound. Across all of these, 合 carries the same motion the etymology shows: parts drawn in until they form a single, sealed whole.</p>
    </div>

    <!-- RETENTION IMAGE -->
    <div class="scholar" data-glyph="合" style="margin-top:2.5rem;">
      <div class="scholar-label">记忆法 jìyìfǎ · Master Retention Image</div>
      <p>See a lid, 亼, settling down onto the mouth of a jar, 口, until the two rims meet and seal. That single closing motion is the whole character. Everything 合 means is parts coming together until they fit.</p>
      <p>When the parts are people, the meeting is cooperation (合作). When the parts are a thing and its setting, it is a good fit (合适). When the parts are a claim and a standard, it is conformity (符合). When the parts are two units, it is a merger (合并). And when the parts are simply two edges of a book, it is the plain act of closing (合上). Whenever you meet 合, ask what two things are being brought together, and you will have its sense.</p>
