    <!-- ── ETYMOLOGY ── -->
    <span class="section-anchor" id="etymology"></span>
    <div class="section-head">
      <span class="sh-cn">字源</span>
      <span class="sh-py">zìyuán</span>
      <span class="sh-en">Etymology &amp; Structure</span>
      <span class="sh-rule"></span>
    </div>

    <div class="scholar" data-glyph="动">
      <div class="scholar-label">字源洞见 zìyuán dòngjiàn · Etymological Insight</div>
      <p><strong>The traditional form 動 = 重 (zhòng, the phonetic) + 力 (lì, force).</strong> The right-hand 力 is the picture of a plough or a flexed arm, the radical of strength and effort; the left-hand 重 supplies the sound. Force applied to a heavy thing makes it move, and that is the heart of the character: 动 is what happens when strength is brought to bear. The simplified form 动 keeps 力 on the right and reduces the left to 云, but the meaning is unchanged: the radical of strength tells you this character is about things being made to move.</p>
      <p>From the single idea of force-producing-motion, the meanings spread:</p>
      <p><strong>1. To move, to act physically.</strong> The literal sense: 运动 yùndòng (to exercise; sport; a movement), 活动 huódòng (activity), 动作 dòngzuò (a movement, an action).<br>
      <strong>2. To stir, to be moved within.</strong> Motion of the heart rather than the body: 感动 gǎndòng (to be moved, touched), 激动 jīdòng (stirred up, excited).<br>
      <strong>3. To set in motion, to drive.</strong> The starting force: 动力 dònglì (motive power, drive), 发动 fādòng (to launch, to start an engine).<br>
      <strong>4. The grammatical "action word."</strong> Because 动 is action itself, the verb is named after it: 动词 dòngcí (verb), literally "action-word."</p>
      <p>The thread is force becoming movement. Whether the thing moved is a body, a heart, a machine, or a sentence, 动 is the moment stillness gives way to motion under some applied strength.</p>

    <span class="section-anchor" id="stir"></span>
    <div class="section-head">
      <span class="sh-cn">感动</span>
      <span class="sh-py">gǎndòng</span>
      <span class="sh-en">To Stir the Heart</span>
      <span class="sh-rule"></span>

    <div class="scholar" data-glyph="动">
      <div class="scholar-label">语义洞见 yǔyì dòngjiàn · Semantic Insight</div>
      <p>The same force that moves a body can move a heart, and Chinese uses 动 for both. <strong>感动</strong> gǎndòng is to be emotionally moved, the inner stirring you feel at a kindness, a reunion, a piece of music. <strong>激动</strong> jīdòng is a sharper, faster stirring, excitement or agitation that quickens the pulse. And 动人 dòngrén, "moving-people," is the adjective for anything touching or stirring, a story, a melody, a face.</p>
      <p>Notice how the body-language and the heart-language stay parallel. A thing that does not move is 不动 (motionless); a heart that will not be stirred is 无动于衷 wú dòng yú zhōng, "unmoved within." In both registers, 动 marks the line between stillness and being set going, whether the push comes from the world outside or the feeling inside.</p>
    </div>

    <div class="scholar" data-glyph="动" style="margin-top:2.5rem;">
      <div class="scholar-label">记忆法 jìyìfǎ · Master Retention Image</div>
      <p>Look at the right side of 动: it is 力, the flexed arm of strength. The whole character is force applied until something gives way and moves. Bring that force to a body and you get sport and activity (运动, 活动). Bring it to a heart and you get the inner stir of being moved (感动, 激动). Bring it to a machine or a goal and you get the drive that pushes it forward (动力).</p>
      <p>The opposite of 动 is 静, stillness. Hold the two in mind as a pair: everything is either at rest or being moved, and 动 is always the moment the strength on the right side wins and motion begins.</p>
