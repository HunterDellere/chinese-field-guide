    <span class="section-anchor" id="etymology"></span>
    <div class="section-head"><span class="sh-cn">字源</span><span class="sh-py">zìyuán</span><span class="sh-en">Etymology &amp; Structure</span><span class="sh-rule"></span></div>
    <div class="scholar" data-glyph="湖">
      <div class="scholar-label">字源洞见 zìyuán dòngjiàn · Etymological Insight</div>
      <p>湖 = 氵 + 胡. The three water-drops of 氵 on the left supply the meaning, and 胡 (hú) on the right supplies the sound. This is a textbook pictophonetic build: the radical tells you it is water, the phonetic tells you to say <em>hú</em>. The two halves still rhyme cleanly today, which is why the character has stayed easy to read across two thousand years of sound change.</p>
      <p>The phonetic 胡 itself began as 月 (flesh) under 古 (old), originally the dewlap of dangling flesh under an ox's jaw. In 湖 none of that animal sense carries over; 胡 is borrowed purely for its pronunciation, the way a stamp is pressed for its shape rather than its picture.</p>
      <p>The water radical points to the defining quality of a lake: it is still water held in a basin, distinct from the running thread of a river. Shuōwén glosses 湖 as a great pooled water, and the early sense is precisely that, a broad standing surface that gathers what the rivers bring down. Dongting and the lakes of the Yangzi plain are the model the word was built on.</p>
      <p>The same graph crossed into Japanese as <em>ko</em> (湖, as in 琵琶湖 Lake Biwa) and Korean as <em>ho</em>, holding the lake sense intact in both.</p>

    <div class="section-head"><span class="sh-cn">地名</span><span class="sh-py">dìmíng</span><span class="sh-en">Places &amp; the Wider World</span><span class="sh-rule"></span></div>
    <div class="scholar" data-glyph="湖">
      <div class="scholar-label">用法 yòngfǎ · How the word travels</div>
      <p>湖 anchors a cluster of proper names. Two great Yangzi provinces are named for their position against Dongting Lake (洞庭湖): 湖北 (Húběi, north of the lake) and 湖南 (Húnán, south of the lake). The character that means a single body of still water came to map an entire region of China.</p>
      <p>From geography the word reaches into metaphor. 江湖 (jiānghú), literally rivers-and-lakes, names the floating world beyond the reach of court and family: the wandering swordsmen, healers, and outlaws of the martial-arts imagination, and by extension any unruly social arena.</p>
    </div>

    <div class="scholar" data-glyph="湖" style="margin-top:2.5rem;">
      <div class="scholar-label">记忆法 jìyìfǎ · Master Retention Image</div>
      <p>Picture three drops of water (氵) running down the left, then pooling against the still bulk of 胡 on the right until they spread into a flat, glassy basin. The radical is the supply, the phonetic is the body the water fills, and the lake is what stands there at dawn, mirror-flat, holding the mountains upside down.</p>
      <p>Hold that mirror surface and the compounds follow it outward. The 湖水 is the water in the basin; the 湖面 is the mirror itself; the 湖畔 is where you stand to look. Step further and the still lake becomes the 江湖, rivers-and-lakes, the wandering world a swordsman drifts across with no shore to call home.</p>
