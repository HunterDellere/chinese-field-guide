    <span class="section-anchor" id="etymology"></span>
    <div class="section-head"><span class="sh-cn">字源</span><span class="sh-py">zìyuán</span><span class="sh-en">Etymology &amp; Structure</span><span class="sh-rule"></span></div>
    <div class="scholar" data-glyph="哥">
      <div class="scholar-label">字源洞见 zìyuán dòngjiàn · Etymological Insight</div>
      <p>哥 = 可 + 可, the same element written twice and stacked vertically. The lower 可 sits beneath the upper one, so the glyph reads like an echo: one breath of sound returned a second time. Each 可 already contains a 口 (mouth), which is why the official radical of the whole character is 口.</p>
      <p>可 began as a phonetic loan. In the seal script it depicts a curved breath rising past a mouth, and it was borrowed first to write the sound now heard in 歌 (gē, to sing). Stack two of them and you get the older written form of that song-sound. For a long stretch 哥 was simply a way of writing what we now spell 歌, the act of chanting or singing, before 歌 took on its own 欠 (gaping mouth) signific and split off.</p>
      <p>The kinship sense arrived later and won. By the Tang dynasty 哥 had been pulled into spoken address for an older male, and the doubling fit the use: a sound you call out, repeated, the way a child calls 哥哥 (gēge) across a courtyard. The character that once stored a song now stores the warmest title in the household.</p>
      <p>Japanese keeps the older trail visible. 哥 survives there chiefly inside 哥薩克 and a handful of literary spellings, while everyday "elder brother" went to 兄. Mandarin took the opposite road and let the doubled 可 own the family.</p>

    <div class="section-head"><span class="sh-cn">兄弟</span><span class="sh-py">xiōngdì</span><span class="sh-en">The Sibling Words</span><span class="sh-rule"></span></div>
    <div class="pattern">哥 ranks siblings by age. 哥哥 is any elder brother; prefix a number and you fix his exact place in the line. 大哥 is the eldest, 二哥 the second, 三哥 the third, counting down toward 弟弟 (dìdi), the younger brother.</div>

    <span class="section-anchor" id="address"></span>
    <div class="section-head"><span class="sh-cn">称呼</span><span class="sh-py">chēnghu</span><span class="sh-en">Social Titles</span><span class="sh-rule"></span></div>
    <div class="scholar" data-glyph="哥">
      <div class="scholar-label">用法 yòngfǎ · How It Works</div>
      <p>哥 reaches far outside blood kinship. Chinese address strangers as relatives constantly, and a young man slightly older than you is a 哥 by courtesy. Attach it to a surname, 王哥 (Wáng gē), and you have a warm, deferential way to call a coworker. Attach it to a quality, 帅哥 (shuàigē), and you have a compliment you can shout across a street.</p>

    <div class="scholar" data-glyph="哥" style="margin-top:2.5rem;">
      <div class="scholar-label">记忆法 jìyìfǎ · Master Retention Image</div>
      <p>Picture a younger child standing at the bottom of a stairwell, calling up: 哥! 哥! The cry goes out once, and a second time, an echo. That doubled call is the glyph: two 可 stacked one above the other, the lower one answering the upper, the small 口 mouth on each one open and sounding.</p>
      <p>Now let the echo widen. The same call climbs out of the stairwell and into the street, where any older boy answers to it: the handsome stranger becomes 帅哥, the man behind the counter becomes 王哥, the leader of the pack becomes 大哥. Every one of these is the same doubled cry aimed at a wider circle. 哥 is the sound you send upward to someone older, repeated until it lands.</p>
