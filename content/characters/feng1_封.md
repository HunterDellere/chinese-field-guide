    <span class="section-anchor" id="etymology"></span>
    <div class="section-head"><span class="sh-cn">字源</span><span class="sh-py">zìyuán</span><span class="sh-en">Etymology &amp; Structure</span><span class="sh-rule"></span></div>
    <div class="scholar" data-glyph="封">
      <div class="scholar-label">字源洞见 zìyuán dòngjiàn · Etymological Insight</div>
      <p>封 sets 圭 (guī) beside 寸 (cùn): ⿰圭寸. The 寸 on the right is a hand with a wrist-pulse marked, the standard graph for a deliberate manual act. The left side traces back through doubled 土 (tǔ, earth): the seal and bronze forms show a hand patting soil into a raised mound. The character records a single physical gesture, heaping earth by hand to mark and close a boundary.</p>
      <p>The oldest sense is territorial. To 封 a piece of land was to raise a mound of packed earth along its edge, often planting a tree on top, so that the line of ownership stood visible on the ground. From that mound came the political sense: a ruler who granted territory was said to 封 a lord, fixing the borders of a fief and the rank that came with it. 分封 (fēnfēng), the carving of the Zhōu realm into hereditary domains, is built on exactly this image.</p>
      <p>The everyday modern sense, to seal shut, is the same gesture turned inward. Where the feudal lord closed off a border with earth, an ordinary writer closes off a letter with a flap of paper. 封 covers both the act (to seal, to block) and the object that holds the sealing (信封, envelope; one 封 of mail). The measure-word use, 一封信 (yì fēng xìn, one letter), preserves the idea that each sealed packet is a single closed unit.</p>
      <p>Japanese keeps the graph as 封 (fū / hō), used in 封筒 (envelope) and 封建 (feudal). Korean reads it 봉 (bong) in 봉투 (envelope) and 밀봉 (hermetic seal). Across all three, the thread holds: a hand closing something off so it stays shut.</p>

    <span class="section-anchor" id="conferring"></span>
    <div class="section-head"><span class="sh-cn">册封</span><span class="sh-py">cèfēng</span><span class="sh-en">Conferring Rank</span><span class="sh-rule"></span></div>
    <div class="scholar" data-glyph="封">
      <div class="scholar-label">语境 yǔjìng · Historical Sense</div>
      <p>The feudal sense is alive in formal and historical Chinese. To 封 someone is to grant them a title, a fief, or a rank from above. 分封制 (fēnfēng zhì) names the Zhōu system of parcelling out land to kinsmen and allies; 册封 (cèfēng) is the formal investiture of a noble or consort by imperial decree. The grammar still carries the old direction: rank flows downward from the one who 封s to the one who is 封ed.</p>

    <div class="scholar" data-glyph="封" style="margin-top:2.5rem;">
      <div class="scholar-label">记忆法 jìyìfǎ · Master Retention Image</div>
      <p>See a lord kneeling at the edge of his new land at dusk. He scoops up a double handful of damp earth, 圭 of soil, and pats it into a low mound along the border with his own hand, 寸. The cool grit packs under his palm; he presses until the line of ownership stands raised and unmistakable. That heap of earth, closed by hand, is 封.</p>
      <p>Now shrink the gesture. The same hand that mounded a border later licks a flap and presses it down over a letter: 信封, the envelope. The same closing motion shuts a road (封闭), locks down a city (封锁), and squeezes a jar airtight (密封). Whether the thing sealed is a kingdom's edge or a single sheet of paper, 封 is always one hand pressing a boundary shut.</p>
      <p>Hold the two extremes together and the character locks in: a fief granted from above and an envelope licked at the kitchen table are the same verb. To 封 is to close something off so it stays closed, then hand it on.</p>
