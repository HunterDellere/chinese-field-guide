    <div class="section-head">
      <span class="sh-cn">字源</span>
      <span class="sh-py">zìyuán</span>
      <span class="sh-en">Etymology &amp; Structure</span>
      <span class="sh-rule"></span>
    </div>

    <div class="scholar" data-glyph="千">
      <div class="scholar-label">字源洞见 zìyuán dòngjiàn · Etymological Insight</div>
      <p>千 was built, in the oracle-bone script, from the graph for a person (人 rén) with a single horizontal stroke drawn across it. The stroke marked the value: one stroke across the person-graph meant one thousand. The system extended: two strokes across the figure could mark two thousand, three strokes three thousand, a compact tally written onto a human form borrowed for its sound, since 人 rén and 千 qiān were close in pronunciation in the old language.</p>
      <p>Over time the person-graph at the bottom was simplified, and the modern character settled into three strokes: a short slanting top stroke (撇 piě), a long horizontal, and a vertical descending through it. The conventional radical is 十 (ten), reflecting both the visual form and the natural place of a thousand in the decimal ladder of 十, 百, 千, 万 (ten, hundred, thousand, ten-thousand).</p>
      <p>That ladder is worth holding in view. Chinese counts in powers that group differently from English: 十 (ten), 百 (hundred), 千 (thousand), and then 万 (ten-thousand) as a distinct unit, after which large numbers are reckoned in multiples of 万 rather than thousand. 千 is thus the last of the small units before the system shifts its base, the highest order still counted the familiar way.</p>
    </div>

    <span class="section-anchor" id="vastness"></span>
    <div class="section-head">
      <span class="sh-cn">千的浩大</span>
      <span class="sh-py">qiān de hàodà</span>
      <span class="sh-en">The Poetics of a Thousand — Distance, Time, Multitude</span>
      <span class="sh-rule"></span>
    </div>

    <div class="scholar" data-glyph="里">
      <div class="scholar-label">虚数 xūshù · a thousand as "vast" rather than a literal count</div>
      <p>In classical and literary Chinese, 千 rarely means exactly one thousand. It is a 虚数 xūshù, a figurative number, used to evoke vastness, distance, or multitude beyond easy counting. When a poet writes 千山 (a thousand mountains) or 千里 (a thousand li), the point is not arithmetic but immensity: ranges without end, a journey beyond the horizon.</p>
      <p>Three domains draw on this most. In space, 千里 (a thousand li) is the standard unit of great distance: 千里之行，始于足下 ("a journey of a thousand li begins beneath one's feet"), the famous line from the Daodejing. In time, 千秋 (a thousand autumns) and 千古 (a thousand antiquities) reach across the ages: 千秋万代 (a thousand autumns, ten thousand generations) means forever, down all the dynasties to come. In multitude, 千 stacks against 万 (ten thousand) in countless paired idioms to mean simply innumerable.</p>
      <p>The pairing 千...万... is one of the most productive patterns in the language: 千变万化 (a thousand changes and ten thousand transformations, endlessly varied), 千军万马 (a thousand armies and ten thousand horses, an overwhelming host), 千辛万苦 (a thousand hardships and ten thousand toils, every kind of suffering). In each, the two large numbers together mean "beyond counting," and 千 supplies the first, slightly lesser magnitude against 万's greater one.</p>
    </div>

      <div class="cy">
        <span class="cy-cn">千变万化</span>
        <span class="cy-py">qiān biàn wàn huà</span>
        <span class="cy-en">"a thousand changes, ten thousand transformations" — endlessly changing; infinitely varied</span>
        <span class="cy-note">千变 (a thousand changes) and 万化 (ten thousand transformations) together mean change without limit. Used of anything in constant flux: shifting clouds, the turns of fortune, the variety of a skilled performer's repertoire. The 千...万... pattern multiplies the two great numbers to mean simply innumerable, with 千 the lesser magnitude paired against 万's greater.</span>
      </div>

      <div class="cy">
        <span class="cy-cn">千军万马</span>
        <span class="cy-py">qiān jūn wàn mǎ</span>
        <span class="cy-en">"a thousand armies, ten thousand horses" — an overwhelming force; a vast host</span>
        <span class="cy-note">千军 (a thousand armies) and 万马 (ten thousand horses): a military host so large it cannot be counted. The phrase pictures an unstoppable onrush of force and is applied to any overwhelming mass, an army, a flood of competitors, a tide of demand. 千军万马过独木桥 (a vast host crossing a single-log bridge) is the stock image for fierce competition through one narrow gate, such as the college entrance exam.</span>
      </div>

      <div class="cy">
        <span class="cy-cn">千辛万苦</span>
        <span class="cy-py">qiān xīn wàn kǔ</span>
        <span class="cy-en">"a thousand pains, ten thousand toils" — untold hardships; every kind of suffering</span>
        <span class="cy-note">千辛 (a thousand bitternesses) and 万苦 (ten thousand toils): hardship piled on hardship beyond counting. Used to describe the immense effort and suffering endured to reach a goal: 历尽千辛万苦 (to have gone through every kind of hardship). The pairing emphasizes that the difficulties were not one or two but innumerable, the full measure of what could be borne.</span>
      </div>

      <div class="cy">
        <span class="cy-cn">千钧一发</span>
        <span class="cy-py">qiān jūn yī fà</span>
        <span class="cy-en">"a thousand jun hanging by one hair" — a moment of extreme peril; hanging by a thread</span>
        <span class="cy-note">千钧 (a thousand jun, an enormous weight, the 钧 being an ancient heavy measure) suspended by 一发 (a single hair). The image is of a crushing weight held by the thinnest possible thread, an instant of utmost danger in which the slightest thing decides catastrophe or escape. 千钧一发之际 (at the critical hair-trigger moment) marks the razor's edge between disaster and survival. Nine and ten cluster around probability; here 千 supplies sheer crushing magnitude.</span>

    <div class="scholar" data-glyph="千">
      <div class="scholar-label">记忆法 jìyìfǎ · Master Retention Image</div>
      <p>Beneath the modern three strokes is an old picture: a person 人 with a single line drawn across, one stroke meaning one thousand, a value written onto a human figure borrowed for its sound. The character sits in the counting ladder 十百千万, the last unit before the system shifts to a base of ten-thousand.</p>
      <p>But the living force of 千 is poetic, not arithmetic. A thousand mountains, a thousand li, a thousand autumns: the number is a 虚数, a figure for vastness too great to count. Above all, hold the pattern 千...万..., where thousand pairs with ten-thousand to mean simply beyond number: 千变万化 endless change, 千军万马 a boundless host, 千辛万苦 untold hardship. When you meet 千 in literature, read it as "countless," not as 1,000.</p>
