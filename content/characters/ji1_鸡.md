    <span class="section-anchor" id="etymology"></span>
    <div class="section-head"><span class="sh-cn">字源</span><span class="sh-py">zìyuán</span><span class="sh-en">Etymology &amp; Structure</span><span class="sh-rule"></span></div>
    <div class="scholar" data-glyph="鸡">
      <div class="scholar-label">字源洞见 zìyuán dòngjiàn · Etymological Insight</div>
      <p>鸡 = 又 + 鸟. The right side 鸟 (niǎo, bird) is the semantic component, the squat bird-radical that you also find in 鸭 (yā, duck) and 鹅 (é, goose). The left side 又 (yòu) is a simplified stand-in: it has no meaning of its own here, just a few quick strokes filling the slot where the traditional character carried far more.</p>
      <p>The traditional form is 雞, and there the left side is 奚 (xī), a phonetic component that once supplied the rough sound of the word. Shuōwén Jiězì files 雞 under the 隹 (zhuī, short-tailed bird) radical and calls it 知時畜也, the creature that knows the hour, the cock whose crow announces dawn. When the character was streamlined for everyday writing, the busy 奚 was dropped and the plain hand-shape 又 was slotted in, the same labor-saving move that turned 漢 into 汉 and 觀 into 观.</p>
      <p>So the modern glyph holds a small history of pruning. The bird stays on the right, anchoring the meaning. The left half went from a full sound-and-sense element to a stroke-economical placeholder. You read 鸡 not by sounding out its parts but by recognizing the bird and remembering the word.</p>
      <p>Japanese kept the old bird in 鶏 (kei / niwatori) and Korean reads 雞 as 계 (gye), so the traditional 雞 still crows across the region even where the mainland glyph has gone lean.</p>

    <div class="pattern">
      <p>公 (gōng, male) and 母 (mǔ, female) are the all-purpose sex markers for animals. Prefix either onto 鸡 and you sex the bird: 公鸡 the rooster, 母鸡 the hen. The same pair gives you 公牛 / 母牛 (bull / cow) and 公猫 / 母猫 (tomcat / queen).</p>

    <div class="scholar" data-glyph="鸡" style="margin-top:2.5rem;">
      <div class="scholar-label">记忆法 jìyìfǎ · Master Retention Image</div>
      <p>Picture a farmyard at first light. On the right of the character squats the 鸟, the plump bird with its short curled tail, scratching at the dirt. That bird is the whole meaning of 鸡, and it never leaves the right side of the glyph.</p>
      <p>On the left, where the traditional 雞 once kept the busy phonetic 奚, the simplified form set down a single open hand, 又, the hand of the farmer reaching into the coop. No sound, no story, just a few quick strokes so you can write the word before the rooster finishes crowing. Hand on the left, bird on the right: the farmer and the fowl.</p>
      <p>From that one bird the day's vocabulary hatches. Reach into the coop for a 鸡蛋, carry the 鸡肉 to the kitchen, sex the flock into 公鸡 and 母鸡, and watch a 母鸡 lead her line of 小鸡 across the yard while the 公鸡 announces the hour.</p>
