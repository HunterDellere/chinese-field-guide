    <span class="section-anchor" id="etymology"></span>
    <div class="section-head"><span class="sh-cn">字源</span><span class="sh-py">zìyuán</span><span class="sh-en">Etymology &amp; Structure</span><span class="sh-rule"></span></div>
    <div class="scholar" data-glyph="送">
      <div class="scholar-label">字源洞见 zìyuán dòngjiàn · Etymological Insight</div>
      <p>送 wraps two pieces around each other: 辶 (chuò), the walking radical that puts feet and a road under everything it touches, and the enclosed element 关, which here supplies the body of a figure with raised arms. The character is meaning-built rather than sound-built; the two parts together stage a scene rather than spelling out a pronunciation. Read the glyph as that scene. Someone stands with hands lifted, gesturing at a traveler whose feet 辶 have already turned toward the road. The whole character is the moment of a person waving 关 at another who is walking away.</p>
      <p>The Shuōwén Jiězì glosses 送 as 遣 (qiǎn), to dispatch or send forth, and classifies it under 辵 (the full form of 辶). Movement is the load-bearing idea. Whatever 送 acts on departs from where the speaker stands: a guest steps out the gate, a parcel leaves the warehouse, a gift crosses from your hands to another's. The walking radical drives the whole meaning.</p>
      <p>From that root, the senses fan out by following the direction of travel. The most concrete is physical accompaniment, walking a guest to the door or the station, 送 someone off. Loosen the body of the actor and you get conveyance, 送 a letter or a meal across town. Loosen it further toward intention and you reach the warm idiom of giving, where 送 someone a present means handing across a thing that will not come back. Every layer keeps the original vector: something leaves you and arrives elsewhere.</p>
      <p>Japanese inherits the character in 送る (okuru), to send or to see off, and 送料 (sōryō) for postage. Korean reads it 송 in 운송 (unsong), transport. Across all three writing traditions the falling fourth tone in Mandarin matches the gesture in the glyph: the hand comes down, the traveler is already gone.</p>

    <div class="section-head"><span class="sh-cn">送别</span><span class="sh-py">sòngbié</span><span class="sh-en">Seeing Off &amp; Farewell</span><span class="sh-rule"></span></div>
    <div class="scholar" data-glyph="别">
      <div class="scholar-label">用法 yòngfǎ · Usage</div>
      <p>This is the sense closest to the glyph's picture: you physically accompany someone toward their departure. 送 here pairs with a destination or a verb of leaving, and it carries genuine warmth. To 送 a guest to the door, 送 a friend to the airport, or 送 a child to school is to walk part of the road with them so they do not set out alone.</p>

    <div class="scholar" data-glyph="送" style="margin-top:2.5rem;">
      <div class="scholar-label">记忆法 jìyìfǎ · Master Retention Image</div>
      <p>Stand at a train platform as the doors hiss shut. A friend on the platform lifts both arms 关 and waves; the carriage 辶 is already rolling, feet of the road carrying the traveler off. Freeze that one frame and you have 送: the raised hands above, the walking radical sliding away beneath. The character is the choreography of departure itself.</p>
      <p>Let the platform dissolve and the same gesture replays at every scale. Hands lift to push a wrapped gift across a table, 送礼; hands lift to wave a colleague out the door of the farewell party, 欢送; hands hoist a parcel onto the courier's bike, 送货. In each case something leaves your grasp and travels to someone else. The waving arms are constant; only the distance changes.</p>
      <p>The falling fourth tone seals the image. Say sòng and the pitch drops the way a waving hand drops when the train is finally gone. Whatever 送 touches, you let it go and it arrives somewhere else.</p>
