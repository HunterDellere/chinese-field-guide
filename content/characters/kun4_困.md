    <span class="section-anchor" id="etymology"></span>
    <div class="section-head"><span class="sh-cn">字源</span><span class="sh-py">zìyuán</span><span class="sh-en">Etymology &amp; Structure</span><span class="sh-rule"></span></div>
    <div class="scholar" data-glyph="困">
      <div class="scholar-label">字源洞见 zìyuán dòngjiàn · Etymological Insight</div>
      <p>困 = 囗 + 木: a tree 木 sealed inside the four-walled enclosure 囗 (wéi), the same square that frames 国, 园, and 围. The whole glyph is a compound ideograph, ⿴囗木, with the wall wrapped fully around the wood. A tree needs open ground and sky to spread its roots and crown. Walled in on every side, it cannot grow. That single image carries the entire word.</p>
      <p>The Shuōwén Jiězì reads the seal form along these lines and glosses 困 as the dead, exhausted timber of an old house, wood that has reached the end of its use. From there the sense widens to anything pressed in and unable to move: a person hemmed by walls, an army ringed by the enemy, a household squeezed by want. The wall does not strike the tree. It simply holds it, and the holding is the hardship.</p>
      <p>Concrete senses came first. 困 meant to besiege and to trap, to pin a thing in place. The abstract reading followed: hard-pressed, stuck in difficulty, worn down. The modern colloquial sense of sleepy is the latest layer. A tired body sags and stalls the way a fenced tree stalls, so 困 took on the heaviness of the eyelids that will not stay open.</p>
      <p>Japanese keeps the character as 困る (komaru), to be troubled or at a loss, and 困難 (konnan), difficulty, both holding the original sense of being boxed into a corner with no easy way out.</p>
    </div>

    <span class="section-anchor" id="hardship"></span>
    <div class="section-head"><span class="sh-cn">困境</span><span class="sh-py">kùnjìng</span><span class="sh-en">Hardship &amp; Being Hemmed In</span><span class="sh-rule"></span></div>
    <div class="pattern">困 in compounds marks the bind itself: 困难 the difficulty, 困境 the trap, 贫困 the squeeze of poverty. In each, the walls press from outside while the subject cannot push back.</div>

    <span class="section-anchor" id="tired"></span>
    <div class="section-head"><span class="sh-cn">犯困</span><span class="sh-py">fànkùn</span><span class="sh-en">Tiredness &amp; Sleepiness</span><span class="sh-rule"></span></div>
    <div class="scholar" data-glyph="困">
      <div class="scholar-label">用法 yòngfǎ · Usage Note</div>
      <p>In spoken Mandarin 困 stands alone as an adjective meaning sleepy: 我困了 (wǒ kùn le), I am getting drowsy. This is the most common everyday use of the bare character. The sense is the eyelid weight of a body that wants to stall, distinct from 累 (lèi), which is physical tiredness or exhaustion. You can be 累 without being 困, and 困 without being 累.</p>
    </div>

    <div class="scholar" data-glyph="困" style="margin-top:2.5rem;">
      <div class="scholar-label">记忆法 jìyìfǎ · Master Retention Image</div>
      <p>Picture a young tree planted in a courtyard, then the courtyard walled up on all four sides with no gate. That is the literal glyph: 木 inside 囗. The roots want to spread but hit brick; the branches reach up but find only a square of sky. The tree is alive and yet it cannot move. Hold that one image, a green thing pinned in a stone box, and you have the root of every meaning.</p>
      <p>Walk it through the compounds. The wall as a problem you cannot get past is 困难. The wall as a whole trap you live inside is 困境. The wall as the squeeze of poverty is 贫困. The wall as an army's ring around a city is 围困. The character never changes shape; only what does the pressing.</p>
      <p>The sleepy sense fits the same picture. When you are 困, your eyelids are the walls, sliding down and boxing in your sight until everything stalls. 我困了 (wǒ kùn le): the tree of you, hemmed by heavy lids, can no longer reach toward the day.</p>
