    <span class="section-anchor" id="etymology"></span>
    <div class="section-head">
      <span class="sh-cn">字源</span>
      <span class="sh-py">zìyuán</span>
      <span class="sh-en">Etymology &amp; Structure</span>
      <span class="sh-rule"></span>
    </div>

    <div class="scholar" data-glyph="城">
      <div class="scholar-label">字源洞见 zìyuán dòngjiàn · Etymological Insight</div>
      <p>城 is a <strong>phono-semantic compound</strong>: 土 tǔ (earth, soil) carries the meaning, and 成 chéng (to complete; to accomplish) carries the sound. The logic is exact and physical. Before stone and brick, a Chinese city was defined by its rampart, and that rampart was rammed earth, layer pounded upon layer until a wall rose high enough to keep an army out. The character names the substance (土) and borrows a near-homophone (成) for the reading. Earth, completed into a wall.</p>
      <p>The radical 土 is the same one that grounds 地 dì (earth, ground), 坐 zuò (to sit), 培 péi (to cultivate), and 墙 qiáng (wall). Whenever 土 sits on the left of a character, you are in the world of soil, the built and shaped ground, the earthworks of agriculture and defense. In 城 it points directly at the construction method: the wall was not quarried or felled, it was packed.</p>
      <p>The most important thing to understand about 城 is that the wall and the city were never two ideas. In classical usage 城 <em>is</em> the wall, and a settlement only counted as a city if it had one. The English split between "city" and "city wall" does not exist here: to have a 城 is to be walled, and to be walled is to be a city. This is why a single character carries both senses without strain, and why so much of Chinese urban history is, quite literally, the history of these earthen rings.</p>
      <p>The phonetic 成 is not silent freight. 成 means to complete, to bring to finished form, and a city wall was among the most demanding completions a pre-modern state could undertake: years of corvée labor, surveyed lines, gates, watchtowers. The character quietly records that a 城 is something <em>achieved</em>, an act of collective will rendered in compacted soil.</p>
    </div>

    <div class="pattern">
      <span class="label">构词规律 gòucí guīlǜ · The three working senses of 城</span>
      <strong>城 as wall</strong> , the defended edge: 城墙 chéngqiáng (city wall) · 城门 chéngmén (city gate) · 长城 Chángchéng (the Great Wall) · 城楼 chénglóu (gate tower)<br>
      <strong>城 as settlement</strong> , the lived city or town: 城市 chéngshì (city) · 城镇 chéngzhèn (towns and cities) · 县城 xiànchéng (county seat) · 进城 jìnchéng (to go into town)<br>
      <strong>城 as enclosure</strong> , any walled complex: 古城 gǔchéng (ancient city) · 城堡 chéngbǎo (castle) · 围城 wéichéng (a besieged or encircled city)<br>
      The same character runs from the literal rampart to the abstract metropolis. When 城 names a wall, look for 墙, 门, 楼; when it names a settlement, look for 市, 镇, 区. The thread that connects them is the boundary: a 城 is always something with an inside and an outside.
    </div>

      <div class="card c-red">
        <div class="card-head">
          <span class="card-cn">城墙</span>
          <span class="card-py">chéngqiáng</span>
          <span class="card-en">city wall; rampart</span>
        </div>
        <div class="tags"><span class="tag tag-n">N 名词 míngcí</span></div>
        <div class="card-def">
          城 chéng (city; wall) + 墙 qiáng (wall). The original sense of 城 made explicit. Where 城 alone once meant the wall, modern Mandarin pairs it with 墙 to specify the physical structure as opposed to the city it encloses. The intact rings at Xi'an and Pingyao are the classic surviving examples, and the phrase carries the whole weight of pre-modern defense: rammed earth faced with brick, wide enough on top to march troops along.
        </div>
        <div class="examples">
          <div class="example">
            <div class="ex-cn">西安的城墙保存得很完整。</div>
            <div class="ex-py">Xī'ān de chéngqiáng bǎocún de hěn wánzhěng.</div>
            <div class="ex-en">The city walls of Xi'an are very well preserved.</div>
          </div>
          <div class="example">
            <div class="ex-cn">我们可以在城墙上骑自行车。</div>
            <div class="ex-py">Wǒmen kěyǐ zài chéngqiáng shàng qí zìxíngchē.</div>
            <div class="ex-en">We can ride bicycles on top of the city wall.</div>
          </div>
        </div>
        <div class="note">
          <span class="note-label">辨析 biànxī · Comparison</span>
          城墙 chéngqiáng is specifically a city wall, defensive and monumental. 墙 qiáng alone is any wall, including the one in your living room. 围墙 wéiqiáng is an enclosing wall around a yard or compound. Only 城墙 implies a whole settlement defended behind it.
        </div>
      </div>

      <!-- 长城 -->
      <div class="card c-ochre">
        <div class="card-head">
          <span class="card-cn">长城</span>
          <span class="card-py">Chángchéng</span>
          <span class="card-en">the Great Wall (lit. "the long wall")</span>
        </div>
        <div class="tags"><span class="tag tag-n">N 专有名词 proper noun</span></div>
        <div class="card-def">
          长 cháng (long) + 城 chéng (wall). The name says exactly what it is: the long wall. Not a single structure but a system of fortifications built and rebuilt across many dynasties along the northern frontier, joining and extending older walls into the line most travelers know from the Ming-era brickwork near Beijing. The full formal name is 万里长城 Wànlǐ Chángchéng, the "ten-thousand-li long wall."
        </div>
        <div class="examples">
          <div class="example">
            <div class="ex-cn">长城是中国最有名的古迹之一。</div>
            <div class="ex-py">Chángchéng shì Zhōngguó zuì yǒumíng de gǔjì zhī yī.</div>
            <div class="ex-en">The Great Wall is one of China's most famous historic sites.</div>
          </div>
          <div class="example">
            <div class="ex-cn">不到长城非好汉。</div>
            <div class="ex-py">Bù dào Chángchéng fēi hǎohàn.</div>
            <div class="ex-en">You are not a true hero until you have climbed the Great Wall.</div>
          </div>
        </div>
      </div>

      <!-- 城门 -->
      <div class="card c-teal">
        <div class="card-head">
          <span class="card-cn">城门</span>
          <span class="card-py">chéngmén</span>
          <span class="card-en">city gate</span>
        </div>
        <div class="tags"><span class="tag tag-n">N 名词 míngcí</span></div>
        <div class="card-def">
          城 chéng (city; wall) + 门 mén (gate; door). The controlled opening in the wall, the single point where the inside and outside of a city legally met. City gates were closed at night, manned by guards, and named individually; a walled capital might have a dozen, each a landmark in its own right. The gate is where taxes were collected, travelers questioned, and, in war, where the decisive struggle was fought.
        </div>
        <div class="examples">
          <div class="example">
            <div class="ex-cn">天黑以后城门就关了。</div>
            <div class="ex-py">Tiān hēi yǐhòu chéngmén jiù guān le.</div>
            <div class="ex-en">After dark the city gates were closed.</div>
          </div>
          <div class="example">
            <div class="ex-cn">敌人攻破了城门。</div>
            <div class="ex-py">Dírén gōngpò le chéngmén.</div>
            <div class="ex-en">The enemy broke through the city gate.</div>
          </div>
        </div>
        <div class="note">
          <span class="note-label">文化 wénhuà · Culture</span>
          A folk saying warns 城门失火，殃及池鱼: when the city gate catches fire, the fish in the moat suffer too. People drew water from the moat to fight the blaze, draining it and stranding the fish. The line is a vivid figure for innocent bystanders harmed by a disaster not their own.
        </div>

      <!-- 城市 -->
      <div class="card c-violet">
        <div class="card-head">
          <span class="card-cn">城市</span>
          <span class="card-py">chéngshì</span>
          <span class="card-en">city</span>
        </div>
        <div class="tags"><span class="tag tag-n">N 名词 míngcí</span></div>
        <div class="card-def">
          城 chéng (walled settlement) + 市 shì (market). The most ordinary modern word for "city," and a fossil of how cities formed: a wall (城) for defense, a market (市) for exchange. The two together make the thing. 城市 is the everyday term across speech and writing, from 大城市 (big city) to 城市化 chéngshìhuà (urbanization), the single most consequential demographic story of modern China.
        </div>
        <div class="examples">
          <div class="example">
            <div class="ex-cn">上海是一个国际化的大城市。</div>
            <div class="ex-py">Shànghǎi shì yī gè guójìhuà de dà chéngshì.</div>
            <div class="ex-en">Shanghai is an international metropolis.</div>
          </div>
          <div class="example">
            <div class="ex-cn">越来越多的年轻人搬到城市生活。</div>
            <div class="ex-py">Yuèláiyuè duō de niánqīngrén bāndào chéngshì shēnghuó.</div>
            <div class="ex-en">More and more young people are moving to cities to live.</div>
          </div>
        </div>
        <div class="note">
          <span class="note-label">辨析 biànxī · Comparison</span>
          城市 chéngshì is the neutral, all-purpose word for "city." 都市 dūshì leans literary and glamorous, evoking the bustle and sophistication of a great metropolis (都市生活, city life with a cosmopolitan shine). You live in a 城市; you romanticize the 都市.
        </div>
      </div>

A collective term spanning the whole range of urban settlement, from large cities down to market towns, used especially in contrast to the countryside (农村 nóngcūn). It appears constantly in policy and statistics: 城镇人口 (urban population), 城镇居民 (urban residents), the dividing line in China's vast rural-to-urban transformation.

            <div class="ex-en">The urban population has now surpassed the rural population.</div>

The administrative town at the center of a county, historically walled, the seat of the local magistrate. 县城 remains a meaningful unit of Chinese life today: the modest urban center where rural residents go for schools, hospitals, and government offices, a rung between the village and the big city.

      <div class="card c-sienna">

The word carries a famous second life as the title of Qian Zhongshu's novel 《围城》, whose central image, that marriage is a besieged fortress, those outside want in and those inside want out, has entered the language as a figure for any institution that looks better from the far side of its walls.

        <div class="examples">
          <div class="example">
            <div class="ex-cn">婚姻就像一座围城。</div>

          </div>
        </div>
        <div class="note">
          <span class="note-label">文化 wénhuà · Culture</span>
          The "besieged city" image from Qian Zhongshu's novel is now proverbial: 城外的人想进去，城里的人想出来 (those outside the wall want in, those inside want out). It is quoted about marriage, careers, and any longed-for place that disappoints once entered.

    <span class="section-anchor" id="chengyu"></span>
    <div class="section-head">
      <span class="sh-cn">成语</span>
      <span class="sh-py">chéngyǔ</span>
      <span class="sh-en">Idioms &amp; Set Phrases</span>
      <span class="sh-rule"></span>
    </div>

    <div class="chengyu-grid">

      <div class="cy">
        <span class="cy-cn">万里长城</span>
        <span class="cy-py">wàn lǐ cháng chéng</span>
        <span class="cy-en">the ten-thousand-li Great Wall: the Great Wall of China</span>
        <span class="cy-note">万 wàn = ten thousand; 里 lǐ = the Chinese mile (a traditional unit of distance); 长城 = the long wall. The full ceremonial name of the Great Wall, with 万里 standing for "immense in length" rather than an exact measurement. The phrase is also used figuratively for an impregnable defense or a person who shields the nation, as in 钢铁长城 (a Great Wall of steel), praise for the armed forces.</span>
      </div>

      <div class="cy">
        <span class="cy-cn">众志成城</span>
        <span class="cy-py">zhòng zhì chéng chéng</span>
        <span class="cy-en">united wills form a city wall: unity is strength</span>
        <span class="cy-note">众 zhòng = the many; 志 zhì = will, resolve; 成 chéng = to form, to complete; 城 chéng = a city wall. When the will of the many is joined, it becomes a rampart no force can breach. The idiom turns the earthen wall into a metaphor for solidarity: a community bound by common purpose is itself a fortress. It is a staple of speeches in moments of national crisis or collective effort.</span>

      <div class="scholar-label">记忆法 jìyìfǎ · Master Retention Image</div>
      <p>See the character build itself: 土 on the left is the heaped soil; 成 on the right gives the sound and, conveniently, the meaning "to complete." A 城 is earth completed into a wall. Hold that single picture and every sense of the character falls into place, because in Chinese the wall and the city were never separated. To have a wall was to be a city; to be a city was to be walled.</p>
      <p>When 城 appears in a compound, ask which face of the wall it shows. In 城墙 and 城门 it is the literal rampart and its gate. In 城市 and 县城 it is the settlement the wall enclosed, the lived city. In 城堡 and 围城 it is the idea of enclosure stretched outward, to a fortress or to a siege. The earthen ring is always there, sometimes as stone underfoot, sometimes only as a figure of speech.</p>
      <p>And the wall keeps turning into metaphor. 众志成城: joined wills become a rampart. 倾国倾城: a beauty that topples cities. 围城: the fortress everyone outside wants in, and everyone inside wants out. Once you see 城 as a boundary with an inside and an outside, these idioms stop being a list to memorize and become variations on a single, very old image: people gathered behind a wall of their own making.</p>
