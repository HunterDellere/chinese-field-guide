    <span class="section-anchor" id="etymology"></span>
    <div class="section-head">
      <span class="sh-cn">字源</span>
      <span class="sh-py">zìyuán</span>
      <span class="sh-en">Etymology &amp; Structure</span>
      <span class="sh-rule"></span>
    </div>

    <div class="scholar" data-glyph="活">
      <div class="scholar-label">字源洞见 zìyuán dòngjiàn · Etymological Insight</div>
      <p><strong>活 is a pictophonetic compound: 氵 (the water radical) carries the meaning, and 舌 shé (tongue) supplies the sound.</strong> On the left runs the three-drop water radical 氵, the standard form of 水 shuǐ when it stands beside another component. On the right sits 舌, the tongue. The water is the heart of the character; the tongue was borrowed for its old pronunciation, close to the sound that became huó.</p>
      <p>The original image was a stream in motion. In the Shuōwén the graph is glossed in terms of flowing water, the gurgle and rush of a current that never stands still. A river runs, a spring bubbles, water moves: and moving water is the oldest emblem a writing system could reach for when it wanted to say <em>alive</em>. What flows is living; what sits stagnant is dead. From that single perception the whole character grows.</p>
      <p>From flowing water two families of meaning open out. <strong>First, to live, to be alive.</strong> The thing that breathes and moves is 活 as against 死 sǐ (dead): 活着 huózhe (to be living), 生活 shēnghuó (to live, life), 活下去 huó xiàqù (to go on living). <strong>Second, living, lively, animated.</strong> Not bare survival but the quality of liveliness, the quickness of something that flows freely: 活泼 huópo (lively), 灵活 línghuó (nimble, flexible), 活力 huólì (vitality).</p>
      <p>A third, very concrete sense grew in everyday northern speech: 活 as a piece of manual work, a job, the task in front of your hands. 干活 gànhuó means to do work, and 活儿 huór (with the retroflex ending) is the work itself: the carpenter's 活儿, the day's 活儿. The thread is the same flow of energy, now poured into the hands and the task. Japanese took the character as 活 (katsu) for liveliness and activity, surviving in 活動 (katsudō, activity) and 生活 (seikatsu, daily life).</p>
    </div>

        <div class="card-head">
          <span class="card-cn">生活</span>
          <span class="card-py">shēnghuó</span>
          <span class="card-en">life; to live; daily living</span>
        </div>
        <div class="tags"><span class="tag tag-vn">V/N 动名词 dòng-míngcí</span></div>
        <div class="card-def">
          生 shēng (to be born, to grow) + 活 huó (to live). The pairing of two life-words covers both the verb (to live, to make a life) and the noun (life as it is lived day to day). This is the everyday word for "life," distinct from 命 mìng, the life that can be lost.
        </div>
        <div class="examples">
          <div class="example">

            <div class="ex-py">Tāmen zài Běijīng shēnghuó le shí nián.</div>
            <div class="ex-en">They have lived in Beijing for ten years.</div>
          </div>
          <div class="example">
            <div class="ex-cn">这里的生活很安静。</div>
            <div class="ex-py">Zhèlǐ de shēnghuó hěn ānjìng.</div>
            <div class="ex-en">Life here is very quiet.</div>
          </div>
        </div>
        <div class="note">
          <span class="note-label">辨析 biànxī · 生活 vs. 生命</span>
          <strong>生活</strong> is life as you live it: your routines, your circumstances, your daily existence. <strong>生命</strong> shēngmìng is life as the thing itself, the spark that can be saved or lost. You improve your 生活; you risk your 生命.
        </div>
      </div>

            <div class="ex-py">Zhǐyào huózhe, jiù yǒu xīwàng.</div>
            <div class="ex-en">As long as you are alive, there is hope.</div>
          </div>

    <!-- ── LIVELY & MOVING ── -->
    <span class="section-anchor" id="lively"></span>
    <div class="section-head">
      <span class="sh-cn">活泼</span>
      <span class="sh-py">huópo</span>
      <span class="sh-en">Lively &amp; Moving: What Stirs and Flows</span>
      <span class="sh-rule"></span>
    </div>

    <div class="scholar" data-glyph="活">
      <div class="scholar-label">语义洞见 yǔyì dòngjiàn · Semantic Insight</div>
      <p>The second family carries the water image forward into the quality of liveliness. A current that runs freely is quick, supple, full of energy, and 活 lends all of this to character and motion. 活泼 huópo describes a lively, animated person, most often a child, the opposite of dull or withdrawn. 灵活 línghuó is the same freedom applied to the body or the mind: nimble, adaptable, able to change course like water finding a new channel.</p>
      <p>From there it powers a cluster of vitality words. 活力 huólì is energy and vigor, the vitality of a person or a city; 活跃 huóyuè is to be active, brisk, animated, the verb for a lively atmosphere or a busy market. Even 活动 huódòng, the everyday word for an activity or event, is built from 活 (lively) plus 动 (to move): a stirring, a thing in motion. Wherever Chinese wants to name energy in flow rather than mere existence, 活 is the character that does it.</p>
    </div>

    <div class="section-head">
      <span class="sh-cn">干活</span>
      <span class="sh-py">gànhuó</span>
      <span class="sh-en">Work &amp; the Job</span>
      <span class="sh-rule"></span>

    <div class="scholar" data-glyph="活">
      <div class="scholar-label">语义洞见 yǔyì dòngjiàn · Semantic Insight</div>
      <p>The third sense is purely colloquial and very Chinese: 活 as a piece of manual work, the task your hands take on. In everyday northern speech 活儿 huór (with the retroflex 儿 ending) is the work itself, the concrete job: a tailor's 活儿, the farmer's 活儿, a hard day's 活儿. To do work is 干活 gànhuó, where 干 gàn means to do or to labor. This is the language of the workshop and the field, not the office, and it keeps the old energy of the character: living force poured into the hands.</p>
    </div>

      <p>Picture a stream running over a tongue. On the left, the three drops of the water radical 氵 are the current itself, splashing downhill. On the right, 舌 the tongue is the rock the water tumbles across, the part that was only ever borrowed for its sound. Hear the gurgle: that rushing, living water is the whole meaning. What flows is alive; what sits still is dead.</p>
      <p>Now follow the water. Let it pool into a life and you have 生活, daily living. Let it run quick and bright and it becomes 活泼 (lively) and 灵活 (nimble), water finding any channel. Let it stir a crowd into motion and it is 活动, an activity. And let it pour down into the hands at a workbench and it is 活儿, the day's work, 干活. The drops on the left never stop moving: every sense of 活 is some shape of that flow, from being alive, to being lively, to the labor your hands are alive with.</p>
