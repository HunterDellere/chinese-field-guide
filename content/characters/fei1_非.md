    <!-- ── ETYMOLOGY ── -->
    <span class="section-anchor" id="etymology"></span>
    <div class="section-head">
      <span class="sh-cn">字源</span>
      <span class="sh-py">zìyuán</span>
      <span class="sh-en">Etymology &amp; Structure</span>
      <span class="sh-rule"></span>
    </div>

    <div class="scholar" data-glyph="非">
      <div class="scholar-label">字源洞见 zìyuán dòngjiàn · Etymological Insight</div>
      <p><strong>非 fēi is a pictograph of two wings spreading in opposite directions.</strong> The oracle-bone and bronze forms show a pair of wings, or the two sides of a bird in flight, pulling away from a central spine to left and right. The two halves face apart, and from that image of things turned against each other comes the core idea: to oppose, to be contrary, and so to negate. 非 is itself a Kangxi radical, the head of its own small family of characters, and it keeps its symmetry in the modern shape: a vertical stem flanked by three short strokes mirrored on each side.</p>
      <p>From "set against, contrary" the character spreads into three living senses:</p>
      <p><strong>1. Not, non-, the bare negator.</strong> 非 negates a noun or a quality, often in a formal or written register: 非常 fēicháng (extremely, literally not-ordinary), 并非 bìngfēi (is not in fact), 非法 fēifǎ (illegal, not-lawful).<br>
      <strong>2. Wrong, fault, what is not right.</strong> The moral edge of negation, the thing that fails the standard: 是非 shìfēi (right and wrong), 不分是非 (unable to tell right from wrong), 文过饰非 (to gloss over one's faults).<br>
      <strong>3. Africa (in transliteration).</strong> Used purely for its sound to render the continent: 非洲 Fēizhōu (Africa), 南非 Nánfēi (South Africa). Here the meaning is set aside and only the syllable fēi remains.</p>
      <p>The thread is opposition. Whether 非 stands against a fact (it is not so), against a standard (this is wrong), or against an ordinary baseline (not-ordinary, hence extreme), the two wings are always pulling apart from the thing they negate.</p>
    </div>

    <!-- ── INTENSIFIER ── -->
    <span class="section-anchor" id="intensifier"></span>
    <div class="section-head">
      <span class="sh-cn">非常</span>
      <span class="sh-py">fēicháng</span>
      <span class="sh-en">Not-Ordinary: the Intensifier 非</span>
      <span class="sh-rule"></span>
    </div>

    <div class="scholar" data-glyph="非">
      <div class="scholar-label">语义洞见 yǔyì dòngjiàn · Semantic Insight</div>
      <p>The most common word a learner meets with 非 is one where the negation has flipped into emphasis. <strong>非常</strong> fēicháng is literally 非 (not) plus 常 (ordinary, constant): "not ordinary," and so "extraordinarily, extremely." A thing that is not ordinary is by implication far off the usual scale, and Chinese reads that distance as intensity. 非常好 means "extremely good," 非常感谢 "thanks very much."</p>
      <p>This makes 非常 a near-neighbor of the everyday intensifiers 很 hěn (very) and 太 tài (too). 很 is the neutral, almost grammatical "very" that props up most adjectives; 非常 is stronger and a touch more emphatic; 太 carries an edge of excess, often "too much." For the full contrast between excess and degree, see the <a href="../../pages/characters/tai4_太.html">太 entry</a>. Read 非常 always as the wings of 非 spreading away from the ordinary: the further from common, the more extreme.</p>
    </div>

        <div class="card-head">
          <span class="card-cn">非常</span>
          <span class="card-py">fēicháng</span>

        <div class="examples">

          </div>
          <div class="example">
            <div class="ex-cn">非常感谢你的帮助。</div>
            <div class="ex-py">Fēicháng gǎnxiè nǐ de bāngzhù.</div>
            <div class="ex-en">Thank you very much for your help.</div>
          </div>
        </div>
        <div class="note">
          <span class="note-label">辨析 biànxī · 非常 vs. 很 vs. 太</span>
          <strong>很</strong> hěn is the neutral default "very," needed to prop up most adjectives in a plain sentence. <strong>非常</strong> is genuinely stronger, "extremely," and adds emphasis. <strong>太</strong> tài leans toward excess, "too," and often pairs with 了: 太贵了 "too expensive." Reach for 非常 when you mean a high degree without the note of "too much."

    <!-- ── NEGATOR ── -->
    <span class="section-anchor" id="negation"></span>
    <div class="section-head">
      <span class="sh-cn">非法</span>
      <span class="sh-py">fēifǎ</span>
      <span class="sh-en">The Negator: Not, Unless, Non-</span>
      <span class="sh-rule"></span>
    </div>

    <div class="scholar" data-glyph="非">
      <div class="scholar-label">语法洞见 yǔfǎ dòngjiàn · Grammatical Insight</div>
      <p>Beyond the everyday 不 bù and 没 méi, Chinese has a more formal negator in 非. It denies category or identity rather than action: 非 X means "not (a) X." As a prefix it builds the equivalents of English un-, il-, and non-: 非法 fēifǎ (illegal), 非正式 fēizhèngshì (informal), 非主流 fēizhǔliú (non-mainstream).</p>
      <p>Two grammatical patterns are worth holding onto. <strong>除非</strong> chúfēi means "unless," literally "except if not": 除非你去，否则我也不去 "unless you go, I won't go either." And the classical frame <strong>非…不可</strong> fēi…bùkě, "if not... then not allowed," expresses strong necessity: 非去不可 "simply must go, there is no not-going." In both, 非 sets up an opposition that the rest of the sentence resolves, the two wings of negation framing a single inescapable conclusion.</p>
    </div>

    <!-- RETENTION IMAGE -->
    <div class="scholar" data-glyph="非" style="margin-top:2.5rem;">
      <div class="scholar-label">记忆法 jìyìfǎ · Master Retention Image</div>
      <p>See the two wings of 非 spreading apart, three strokes mirrored on the left, three on the right, pulling away from the spine in opposite directions. That image of things set against each other is the whole character. 非 is whatever stands opposed to the thing named.</p>
      <p>Set it against a fact and it is plain denial: 并非如此, that is not so. Set it against a standard and it is fault: 是非, right and wrong. Set it against the ordinary and the opposition reads as distance, and distance reads as intensity: 非常, not-ordinary, hence extreme. And in 除非 and 非…不可, those same wings frame the one path left open, the exception or the necessity. Whenever you meet 非, find the thing it is turned against, and its sense will follow.</p>
