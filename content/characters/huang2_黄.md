    <span class="section-anchor" id="etymology"></span>
    <div class="section-head">
      <span class="sh-cn">字源</span>
      <span class="sh-py">zìyuán</span>
      <span class="sh-en">Etymology &amp; Structure</span>
      <span class="sh-rule"></span>
    </div>

    <div class="scholar" data-glyph="黄">
      <div class="scholar-label">字源洞见 zìyuán dòngjiàn · the jade-pendant figure</div>
      <p>黄 is its own radical, an eleven-stroke pictograph that the modern eye can no longer parse into parts. The traditional reading, preserved in the seal forms, sees a standing human figure wearing a jade 佩 (pèi) pendant at the waist: the wide central belt of the character is the pendant hung from the sash of a person of rank. The jade worn against the body is the source of the color word, the warm honey-yellow of carved nephrite catching light at the hip of someone important enough to wear it.</p>
      <p>The Shuōwén Jiězì gives the canonical gloss in four characters: 地之色也 (dì zhī sè yě), "the color of the earth." This is the loess color, the fine wind-blown yellow soil of the North China Plain where the civilization first took root. The Shuōwén places 黄 at the center of the Five Phases system, the color of the center direction (中央, zhōngyāng), set against the white of the west, the black of the north, the green-blue of the east, and the red of the south. Center is earth, earth is yellow, and the ruler who governs from the center claims the color as his own.</p>
      <p>From the soil-and-jade root the meaning ran two directions. Downward into the everyday: ripe grain is yellow, autumn leaves go yellow, an egg yolk is yellow, withered plants turn yellow. Upward into power and value: the metal 金 (jīn) most prized is the yellow one, gold, so 黄金 (huángjīn) became the word for gold and for anything at its peak worth. The same character names the dust on a farmer's feet and the dragon robe of the Son of Heaven.</p>
      <p>Japanese inherits 黄 with the readings <em>kō</em> and <em>ki</em>. Korean keeps 황 (hwang), also one of the common surnames. Vietnamese reads <em>hoàng</em>. Across East Asia the character anchors a shared vocabulary of yellow: 黄帝 (Huángdì, the Yellow Emperor), 黄昏 (huánghūn, dusk), 黄铜 (huángtóng, brass). As a Chinese surname 黄 is among the most common in the south, especially Guangdong and Fujian, and travels in the diaspora as Wong, Huang, or Ng.</p>
    </div>

    <!-- ── COLOR ── -->
    <span class="section-anchor" id="color"></span>
    <div class="section-head">
      <span class="sh-cn">黄色</span>
      <span class="sh-py">huángsè</span>
      <span class="sh-en">Yellow as Color · loess, emperor, taboo</span>
      <span class="sh-rule"></span>
    </div>

    <div class="scholar" data-glyph="帝">
      <div class="scholar-label">yellow in the Chinese imperial order</div>
      <p>Yellow was the emperor's color, and for long stretches of the imperial period it was his alone. From the Sui and Tang onward, a specific bright yellow, 明黄 (mínghuáng), was reserved for the imperial household; a commoner who wore it risked a charge of treason. The dragon robe, the roof tiles of the Forbidden City, the imperial edicts on yellow silk all coded the same claim: the man at the center governs by the color of the center, the color of earth itself. The founding myth reaches back to 黄帝, the Yellow Emperor, the legendary ancestor from whom the Han people trace their descent.</p>
      <p>The modern color word 黄色 (huángsè) carries a second meaning that has nothing to do with emperors: pornographic, obscene, "blue" in the English sense. 黄色电影 (huángsè diànyǐng) is a dirty film; 扫黄 (sǎo huáng), "sweeping out the yellow," is the official term for an anti-vice crackdown. The slang traces to the late nineteenth and early twentieth century, when cheap sensational fiction and yellow journalism (itself a borrowing) attached the color to the lurid. A learner who praises a Chinese friend's "黄色衬衫" meaning a yellow shirt will be understood, but the bare phrase 黄色 in the wrong context lands very differently.</p>
      <p>Three distinctions worth holding. 黄 (huáng) is the bare color word and the surname. 黄色 (huángsè) is the noun "the color yellow," and separately the adjective "obscene." 金黄 (jīnhuáng, "gold-yellow") and 嫩黄 (nènhuáng, "tender yellow") are intensified or softened shades. Ripe wheat described as 金黄 is being praised; a sickly complexion described as 蜡黄 (làhuáng, "wax-yellow") is being pitied. The base character is neutral; the compounds carry the charge.</p>
    </div>

    <!-- ── GOLD ── -->
    <span class="section-anchor" id="gold"></span>
    <div class="section-head">
      <span class="sh-cn">黄金</span>
      <span class="sh-py">huángjīn</span>
      <span class="sh-en">Gold &amp; Value · the metal &amp; the prime</span>
      <span class="sh-rule"></span>
    </div>

    <div class="pattern">
      <span class="label">黄 + 金 · yellow metal, and what is most precious</span>
      <p>黄金 names literal gold, the yellow metal: 一克黄金 (yì kè huángjīn), one gram of gold. From the metal the compound extended into a metaphor for anything at its peak of value or unrepeatable opportunity, and this metaphorical use is now everywhere in modern Chinese:</p>
      <ul>
        <li>黄金时间 (huángjīn shíjiān): prime time, the golden hours.</li>
        <li>黄金周 (huángjīn zhōu): "golden week," the week-long national holidays.</li>
        <li>黄金年龄 (huángjīn niánlíng): the prime of life, peak years.</li>
        <li>黄金搭档 (huángjīn dādàng): a golden pairing, an ideal partnership.</li>
        <li>黄金地段 (huángjīn dìduàn): prime real estate.</li>
      </ul>
      <p>The logic is the same one English reaches for with "golden": the best, the most valuable, the not-to-be-wasted. Where English says "the golden age," Chinese says 黄金时代 (huángjīn shídài). The color and the metal fuse into a single idea of peak worth, and 黄 carries it into dozens of fixed phrases.</p>
    </div>

    <!-- ── RIVER ── -->
    <span class="section-anchor" id="river"></span>
    <div class="section-head">
      <span class="sh-cn">黄河</span>
      <span class="sh-py">huánghé</span>
      <span class="sh-en">The Yellow River · the mother river</span>
      <span class="sh-rule"></span>
    </div>

    <div class="scholar" data-glyph="河">
      <div class="scholar-label">黄河 and the cradle of the civilization</div>
      <p>黄河, the Yellow River, takes its name from the color of the water itself. As it cuts through the loess plateau of the northwest it picks up enormous loads of fine yellow silt, more than any other major river on earth, until the water runs the thick brown-gold of the soil it carries. That silt built the North China Plain over millennia, laying down the fertile ground on which the earliest Chinese states rose. For this reason 黄河 is called 母亲河 (mǔqīn hé), the mother river, the cradle of Chinese civilization.</p>
      <p>The same silt makes the river deadly. As it slows on the flat plain the suspended loess settles, raising the riverbed year by year until in places it runs above the surrounding fields, held in only by dikes. When the dikes break the floods are catastrophic, and the Yellow River has earned a second name, 中国的悲哀 (Zhōngguó de bēi'āi), "China's sorrow," for the death tolls of its historic floods. A river that gives the soil and takes the lives, both in the same yellow water: the character names the gift and the grief at once. See <a href="../geography/topic_changjiang.html">长江 the Yangtze</a> for its great southern counterpart.</p>
    </div>

    <!-- ── FAIL ── -->
    <span class="section-anchor" id="fail"></span>
    <div class="section-head">
      <span class="sh-cn">黄了</span>
      <span class="sh-py">huáng le</span>
      <span class="sh-en">To Fall Through · the plan collapses</span>
      <span class="sh-rule"></span>
    </div>

    <div class="pattern">
      <span class="label">黄 as verb · "to fall through, to fail, to fizzle out"</span>
      <p>In colloquial Mandarin 黄 works as a verb meaning a plan, deal, relationship, or scheme has collapsed before completing. The image is botanical: a green shoot that yellows and withers instead of bearing fruit. The construction is almost always 黄了 (huáng le), with the completed-action particle:</p>
      <ul>
        <li>生意黄了 (shēngyi huáng le): the business deal fell through.</li>
        <li>这事儿黄了 (zhè shìr huáng le): this thing fizzled out.</li>
        <li>他们俩黄了 (tāmen liǎ huáng le): the two of them broke up.</li>
        <li>计划黄了 (jìhuà huáng le): the plan collapsed.</li>
      </ul>
      <p>The slang is fully informal and very common in northern speech. It carries a shrug of resignation rather than blame: the thing simply did not come to fruit, like a crop that yellowed in the field. A learner who hears 黄了 should reach for "fell through" or "didn't pan out," not for any sense of the color.</p>
    </div>

    <span class="section-anchor" id="chengyu"></span>
    <div class="section-head">
      <span class="sh-cn">成语</span>
      <span class="sh-py">chéngyǔ</span>
      <span class="sh-en">Idioms &amp; Set Phrases</span>
      <span class="sh-rule"></span>

    <div class="chengyu-grid">
      <div class="cy">
        <div class="cy-cn">黄粱一梦</div>
        <div class="cy-py">huáng liáng yī mèng</div>
        <div class="cy-en">"a dream during millet": a fleeting illusion of glory</div>
        <div class="cy-note">From a Tang tale by Shěn Jìjì: a poor scholar dozes at an inn while millet cooks, dreams an entire lifetime of wealth and high office, and wakes to find the millet not yet done. The chengyu names any grand ambition that proves an empty dream, the vanity of worldly success measured against the time it takes to boil a pot of grain. The yellow is the 黄粱, the yellow millet.</div>
      </div>
      <div class="cy">
        <div class="cy-cn">黄道吉日</div>
        <div class="cy-py">huáng dào jí rì</div>
        <div class="cy-en">"yellow-path auspicious day": a lucky day for an undertaking</div>
        <div class="cy-note">黄道 is the ecliptic, the sun's apparent path through the sky, and in the traditional almanac the days when its governing stars are favorable. To pick a 黄道吉日 is to consult the calendar for an auspicious date to marry, open a shop, move house, or break ground. The practice is alive in the modern almanac (黄历 huánglì) consulted before any major event.</div>
      </div>
      <div class="cy">
        <div class="cy-cn">青黄不接</div>
        <div class="cy-py">qīng huáng bù jiē</div>
        <div class="cy-en">"green and yellow do not connect": a gap in the supply</div>
        <div class="cy-note">青 is the green of the unripe new crop, 黄 the yellow of the ripe old grain. The phrase names the lean stretch when the old harvest is eaten but the new is not yet ready, the hungry gap of late spring. Extended to any shortfall between an exhausted resource and the not-yet-arrived replacement: a staffing gap, a funding gap, a generational gap in skills.</div>
      </div>
      <div class="cy">
        <div class="cy-cn">人老珠黄</div>
        <div class="cy-py">rén lǎo zhū huáng</div>
        <div class="cy-en">"person old, pearl yellowed": faded with age, past one's prime</div>
        <div class="cy-note">A pearl that has yellowed with age has lost its luster and its worth; the phrase compares an aging person, traditionally and unkindly a woman, to such a pearl. Sharp and dated in its sexism, it survives mostly as a phrase to be quoted and pushed back against. The yellowing here is the dulling of value over time, the inverse of 黄金's peak.</div>
      </div>
    </div>

    <div class="scholar" data-glyph="黄" style="margin-top:2.5rem;">
      <div class="scholar-label">记忆法 jìyìfǎ · the jade at the waist</div>
      <p>Hold the picture: a standing figure of rank, a broad jade 佩 pendant hung at the belt, the warm honey-yellow of carved nephrite catching the light. That yellow is the color of the carved stone, and it is the color of the loess soil the Shuōwén named, 地之色, the color of the earth. The figure stands at the center of the Five Phases, where earth governs, where the emperor in his yellow robe governs the world from the middle direction.</p>
      <p>From that center the color radiates out through the compounds. Down into the field: ripe grain goes 金黄, the egg's heart is the 蛋黄, the river runs thick with 黄土. Up into power and worth: the yellow metal is 黄金, the peak hours are 黄金时间, the legendary ancestor is 黄帝. And then the dark turn, the botanical one: a green shoot that yellows without fruiting, a plan that 黄了, fell through. The same yellow that crowns the emperor and gilds the wheat also names the crop that withered in the field.</p>
      <p>Couple 黄 with <a href="../characters/he2_河.html">河</a>, <a href="../characters/jin1_金.html">金</a>, and <a href="../characters/se4_色.html">色</a>, and the field completes itself: the mother river carrying its silt, the metal at its peak worth, the color word that spans them, and the jade pendant at the waist of the figure who started it all.</p>
