    <span class="section-anchor" id="etymology"></span>
    <div class="section-head"><span class="sh-cn">字源</span><span class="sh-py">zìyuán</span><span class="sh-en">Etymology &amp; Structure</span><span class="sh-rule"></span></div>
    <div class="scholar" data-glyph="孩">
      <div class="scholar-label">字源洞见 zìyuán dòngjiàn · Etymological Insight</div>
      <p>孩 splits cleanly down the middle: 孩 = 子 + 亥. The left half, 子, is the radical and the meaning: a swaddled infant with arms out, the same glyph that names a "son" or "child" on its own. The right half, 亥, is pure sound. 亥 is the twelfth of the earthly branches and carries no childhood sense; it sits on the right only because in early Chinese it rhymed with the word for a small child. This is the textbook pictophonetic: one side tells you what, one side tells you how it sounds.</p>
      <p>The pairing is old. In the seal script the left-hand 子 keeps its rounded, bundled-infant shape, while the right-hand 亥 holds the branched form the earthly-branch cycle adopted. The Shuōwén Jiězì glosses 孩 with the cooing of a baby, the small open-mouthed laugh of an infant who cannot yet form words. That sensory root, a sound before speech, is why the character first attached to the very youngest.</p>
      <p>From that infant cry the sense widened outward through age: from the babbling baby, to the toddler, to any child, and finally to "children" as a class of people. Modern Mandarin almost never lets 孩 stand alone; it leans on a partner, most often the noun-softening 子 in 孩子 (háizi). The bare glyph survives mostly inside compounds, the way a brick survives inside a wall.</p>

        <div class="tags"><span class="tag tag-n">N 名词 míngcí</span></div>
        <div class="card-def">孩 (child) plus the neutral-tone suffix 子, which turns many one-syllable nouns into stable everyday words. 孩子 is the default word for a child and also the word a parent uses for a son or daughter of any age.</div>

          <div class="example"><div class="ex-cn">他们有两个孩子。</div><div class="ex-py">Tāmen yǒu liǎng gè háizi.</div><div class="ex-en">They have two children.</div></div>
        </div>
        <div class="note"><span class="note-label">辨析 biànxī · 孩子 vs. 儿童</span>孩子 is the warm, conversational word and can mean "my son/daughter." 儿童 (értóng) is the formal, institutional term seen on signs, ticket prices, and laws.</div>
      </div>

    <div class="scholar" data-glyph="孩" style="margin-top:2.5rem;">
      <div class="scholar-label">记忆法 jìyìfǎ · Master Retention Image</div>
      <p>Picture the glyph as a nursery split by a curtain. On the left, 子, stands the child itself: a swaddled baby, arms thrown wide, the exact bundled shape you see in the radical. On the right, 亥, hangs a wooden cradle-tag carved with a calendar branch, a marker with no meaning of its own except to ring out the sound hái when you tap it.</p>
      <p>So every time you read 孩, you meet the baby on the left and tap the sound-tag on the right: 子 shows you a child, 亥 sings you hái. Hold that division and the compounds fall into place. Hang a 小 above the cradle for 小孩, a little one; stand a 男 or 女 at the door for 男孩 and 女孩; and let the soft 子 settle back in for 孩子, the warm everyday word a parent uses for their own.</p>
