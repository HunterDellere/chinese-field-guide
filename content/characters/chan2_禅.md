faq:
  - q: "What does 禅 (chán) mean?"
    a: "As chán it is the Buddhist meditation morpheme, a transliteration of Sanskrit dhyāna via the earlier form 禅那: 坐禅 is seated meditation and 禅宗 is the Chan school, which reached Japan as Zen. The character itself is the altar radical 礻 plus 单 as phonetic."
  - q: "What does the shàn reading of 禅 mean?"
    a: "The character's original job: solemn transfer and sacrifice. 禅让 (shànràng) is the yielding of the throne to a worthy successor, as the sage-king Yao did for Shun, and 封禅 (fēngshàn) names the paired imperial sacrifices at Mount Tai to heaven and earth."
  - q: "What is a 口头禅 (kǒutóuchán)?"
    a: "Literally 'mouth Chan': originally a jab at people who talk enlightenment without practicing it, now the everyday word for a pet phrase or verbal habit. Asking about someone's 口头禅 is asking what they always say."

    <!-- ── ETYMOLOGY ── -->
    <span class="section-anchor" id="etymology"></span>
    <div class="section-head">
      <span class="sh-cn">字源</span>
      <span class="sh-py">zìyuán</span>
      <span class="sh-en">Etymology &amp; Structure</span>
      <span class="sh-rule"></span>
    </div>

    <div class="scholar" data-glyph="禪">
      <div class="scholar-label">字源洞见 zìyuán dòngjiàn · Etymological Insight</div>
      <p>The structure is a clean phono-semantic compound: <strong>禅 = 礻 + 单</strong>. The left side 礻 is the compressed form of 示 (shì), the altar radical, a pictograph read as an altar stand or offerings table, which files the character among sacrifice and spirit words: 神 (shén, spirit), 祖 (zǔ, ancestor), 福 (fú, blessing), 祭 (jì, sacrifice). The right side 单 (dān, whose traditional form is 單) supplies the sound, and simplification of that phonetic is the only difference between 禅 and the traditional 禪.</p>
      <p>The altar is no accident. The character's native business, before Buddhism arrived, was solemn ritual transfer: the sacrifice a ruler performs to earth, and the passing of the throne itself. That a character built for imperial altars ended up naming a meditation school in Kyoto is one of the script's great career changes, and it happened purely through sound, as the next two sections trace.</p>
    </div>

    <!-- ── SHAN READING ── -->
    <span class="section-anchor" id="shan"></span>
    <div class="section-head">
      <span class="sh-cn">禅让与封禅</span>
      <span class="sh-py">shànràng yǔ fēngshàn</span>
      <span class="sh-en">The shàn Reading: Yielded Thrones and Mount Tai</span>
      <span class="sh-rule"></span>
    </div>

    <div class="scholar" data-glyph="让">
      <div class="scholar-label">尧舜之道 Yáo Shùn zhī dào · The Way of Yao and Shun</div>
      <p>Read <strong>shàn</strong>, the character carries its ancient freight. <strong>禅让</strong> (shànràng) is the yielding of the throne to a worthy successor outside one's own line, the act attributed to the sage-kings: Yao passed over his unworthy son to elevate Shun, and Shun in turn elevated Yu the flood-tamer. Whether any of this happened is beyond recovery, but as political theory it mattered immensely: 禅让 became the Confucian ideal of legitimate succession by virtue, invoked (usually cynically) whenever a dynasty's founder stage-managed the last emperor's "voluntary" abdication in his favor. Cao Pi's scripted reception of the Han throne in 220 CE was performed, meticulously, as a 禅让.</p>
      <p><strong>封禅</strong> (fēngshàn) names the rarest imperial rite: the paired sacrifices at Mount Tai, 封 to heaven on the summit, 禅 to earth on a hill below. Only an emperor who believed his achievements merited heaven's direct notice dared perform it; Qin Shi Huang and Han Wudi both climbed the mountain, and the historian Sima Qian's father died grieving that he missed accompanying the rite. The reading survives in exactly these historical compounds, so a learner meets shàn in history books and chán everywhere else.</p>
    </div>

    <!-- ── CHAN READING ── -->
    <span class="section-anchor" id="chan"></span>
    <div class="section-head">
      <span class="sh-cn">禅那</span>
      <span class="sh-py">chánnà</span>
      <span class="sh-en">The chán Reading: Dhyāna Lands on the Altar</span>
      <span class="sh-rule"></span>
    </div>

    <div class="scholar" data-glyph="悟">
      <div class="scholar-label">从梵语到日语 · From Sanskrit to Japanese</div>
      <p>When Buddhist translators needed to render Sanskrit <em>dhyāna</em>, meditative absorption, they reached for sound rather than sense and wrote <strong>禅那</strong> (chánnà), soon clipped to 禅. The choice recruited an altar character for a contemplative practice, and the borrowed syllable became the name of the tradition this guide's 禅宗 entry covers in full: the school of direct pointing at the mind, of Bodhidharma facing the wall and Huineng's sudden enlightenment, which travelled to Korea as Seon, to Vietnam as Thiền, and to Japan as Zen, in each case the local pronunciation of this one character.</p>
      <p>The practice vocabulary stays close: 坐禅 (zuòchán, seated meditation), 禅师 (chánshī, a Chan master), 禅房 (the meditation hall of temple poetry), 参禅 (cānchán, to investigate Chan, the koan-work verb). And the language kept one thoroughly secular souvenir: <strong>口头禅</strong> (kǒutóuchán, "mouth Chan"), coined to mock those who recite enlightenment without practicing it, now the standard word for anyone's pet phrase. That a term of monastic criticism became the word for your colleague's habit of saying 差不多 is very much in the Chan spirit of deflation.</p>
    </div>
