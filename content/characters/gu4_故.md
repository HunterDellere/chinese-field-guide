    <span class="section-anchor" id="etymology"></span>
    <div class="section-head"><span class="sh-cn">字源</span><span class="sh-py">zìyuán</span><span class="sh-en">Etymology &amp; Structure</span><span class="sh-rule"></span></div>

    <div class="scholar" data-glyph="故">
      <div class="scholar-label">字源洞见 zìyuán dòngjiàn · Etymological Insight</div>
      <p>故 is a pictophonetic compound (形声字 xíngshēngzì): <strong>古 gǔ</strong> (old, ancient) carries the sound, and <strong>攵 pū</strong> (the strike radical, a variant of 攴, a hand holding a stick or rod) carries a sense of action done to something. The phonetic 古 is not silent here. It also leans on the page semantically, because 古 itself means "old, of long ago," and that meaning bleeds into nearly every sense 故 takes. The character keeps the same shape in simplified and traditional writing.</p>
      <p>The Shuo Wen Jie Zi, the 2nd-century CE character dictionary, glosses 故 with 使为之也 (shǐ wéi zhī yě): roughly, "that which causes a thing to be done." This is the anchoring sense. A 故 is a cause, the settled reason that makes something happen. From that single root, the other senses fan out. Because a cause is something prior, something already established, 故 slides into "old, former, what was the case before." Because 攵 marks deliberate action upon something, 故 takes on "to do on purpose, deliberately." Used as a connective in classical prose, 故 means "for this reason, hence, therefore," literally pointing back to the cause just stated. And in the gentlest extension, the deceased are 故 because they belong now to what has passed.</p>
      <p>The radical 攵 (also written 攴, called 反文旁 fǎnwénpáng in everyday Chinese because it resembles a reversed 文) appears in a large family of characters involving action, governance, or striking: 教 jiào (to teach), 政 zhèng (governance), 改 gǎi (to change), 攻 gōng (to attack), 收 shōu (to gather in). In each, something is acted upon. Within 故, the radical reminds us that a cause is not a passive fact but a force that makes things happen. In Japanese, 故 (ko in Sino-Japanese reading) survives in 故郷 (kokyō, hometown) and as the prefix 故 before a deceased person's name, exactly as in Chinese 故 plus a name.</p>
    </div>

    <span class="section-anchor" id="reason"></span>
    <div class="section-head"><span class="sh-cn">缘故</span><span class="sh-py">yuángù</span><span class="sh-en">Reason &amp; Cause: Why a Thing Happens or Fails</span><span class="sh-rule"></span></div>

    <div class="scholar" data-glyph="故">
      <div class="scholar-label">故 as cause , the root that holds the rest together</div>
      <p>The oldest and most literal sense of 故 is cause. When you ask 什么缘故 (shénme yuángù, for what reason) you are asking for the 故 behind an event, the thing that made it be. This sense survives intact in modern compounds: a malfunction is a 故障 (gùzhàng), literally an obstruction with a cause; an accident is a 事故 (shìgù), an incident with a cause behind it. The cause sense is also why 故 anchors the fixed phrase 无缘无故 (wú yuán wú gù), "without cause, for no reason at all," used when something happens with no traceable trigger.</p>
    </div>

    <div class="cards">
      <div class="card c-red">
        <div class="card-head"><span class="card-cn">缘故</span><span class="card-py">yuángù</span><span class="card-en">reason; cause; the why behind something</span></div>
        <div class="tags"><span class="tag tag-n">N 名词</span></div>
        <div class="card-def">缘 (yuán, cause, affinity, the edge along which things connect) + 故 (cause, reason). A two-character word for the reason behind an event, slightly more formal and reflective than the everyday 原因 (yuányīn). 缘故 often appears at the end of a clause: 因为天气的缘故 (because of the weather), 不知什么缘故 (for some unknown reason). The 缘 lends a faint sense of circumstance or fate, as if the reason were woven into how things were bound to connect.</div>
        <div class="examples">
          <div class="example"><div class="ex-cn">不知什么缘故，他今天没来上班。</div><div class="ex-py">Bùzhī shénme yuángù, tā jīntiān méi lái shàngbān.</div><div class="ex-en">For some unknown reason, he didn't come to work today.</div></div>
          <div class="example"><div class="ex-cn">因为身体的缘故，她推掉了这次旅行。</div><div class="ex-py">Yīnwèi shēntǐ de yuángù, tā tuīdiào le zhè cì lǚxíng.</div><div class="ex-en">On account of her health, she called off this trip.</div></div>
        </div>
        <div class="note">
          <span class="note-label">辨析 biànxī · 缘故 vs 原因</span>
          原因 (yuányīn) is the neutral, technical word for cause, the one used in science, law, and reports. 缘故 is softer and more conversational, often gestures at circumstance rather than a precise mechanism, and tends to sit at the end of a clause introduced by 因为 or 由于. You write 事故原因 (the cause of the accident) in a report, but say 因为某种缘故 (for a certain reason) when you would rather not spell it out.

        <div class="card-def">故 (cause; here, an underlying fault) + 障 (zhàng, obstruction, barrier). A malfunction in a machine or system: something with a cause behind it that obstructs normal operation. 出故障 (chū gùzhàng) means "to develop a fault." The word is everywhere in modern technical life: 系统故障 (system failure), 机械故障 (mechanical breakdown), 故障代码 (fault code). The 故 here is the diagnostic sense, the reason hidden inside the failure that an engineer must trace.</div>

          <div class="example"><div class="ex-cn">电梯出故障了，我们只能走楼梯。</div><div class="ex-py">Diàntī chū gùzhàng le, wǒmen zhǐ néng zǒu lóutī.</div><div class="ex-en">The elevator broke down, so we had to take the stairs.</div></div>
          <div class="example"><div class="ex-cn">技术人员很快就找到了故障的原因。</div><div class="ex-py">Jìshù rényuán hěn kuài jiù zhǎodào le gùzhàng de yuányīn.</div><div class="ex-en">The technicians quickly located the cause of the malfunction.</div></div>

      <div class="card c-teal">
        <div class="card-head"><span class="card-cn">事故</span><span class="card-py">shìgù</span><span class="card-en">accident; incident; mishap</span></div>
        <div class="tags"><span class="tag tag-n">N 名词</span></div>
        <div class="card-def">事 (shì, matter, affair, event) + 故 (cause, the reason behind it). An accident or untoward incident: literally an event with a cause, usually an unwanted one. 交通事故 (jiāotōng shìgù) is a traffic accident; 安全事故 (ānquán shìgù) is a safety incident. The word frames the event as something to be investigated, traced back to its 故. Compare the unrelated colloquial idiom 没事故 in speech, where people simply say 没事 (méi shì, it's nothing); 事故 proper always implies a reportable mishap.</div>
        <div class="examples">
          <div class="example"><div class="ex-cn">高速公路上发生了一起严重的交通事故。</div><div class="ex-py">Gāosù gōnglù shàng fāshēng le yì qǐ yánzhòng de jiāotōng shìgù.</div><div class="ex-en">A serious traffic accident occurred on the expressway.</div></div>
          <div class="example"><div class="ex-cn">工厂多年来没有发生过任何安全事故。</div><div class="ex-py">Gōngchǎng duōnián lái méiyǒu fāshēng guò rènhé ānquán shìgù.</div><div class="ex-en">The factory has not had a single safety incident in many years.</div></div>

    <span class="section-anchor" id="former"></span>
    <div class="section-head"><span class="sh-cn">故乡</span><span class="sh-py">gùxiāng</span><span class="sh-en">Old &amp; Former: Hometown, Story, Old Friend</span><span class="sh-rule"></span></div>

    <div class="scholar" data-glyph="故">
      <div class="scholar-label">故 as the old and the prior , where the warmth lives</div>
      <p>Because a cause is something prior, 故 readily means "old, former, belonging to before." This is the most emotionally loaded family of the character. 故乡 (gùxiāng, hometown) is the native place one comes from and longs to return to, a word saturated with the literary tradition of homesickness. 故人 (gùrén) is an old friend from a former time. 故事 (gùshì) is a story, literally "old matters," the things that were, retold. And 故宫 (Gùgōng), the Old Palace, is the formal name of the Forbidden City in Beijing, the former imperial residence. In this family 故 overlaps closely with 旧 (jiù, old, worn): 故 leans toward "former, of a prior time," while 旧 leans toward "worn with use." A friend known long ago is 故人; a worn-out coat is 旧.</p>
    </div>

Centuries of poetry rest on this word: the moon seen from a foreign place that calls the wanderer's heart back to the 故乡.         <div class="card-def">故 (old, former) + 事 (shì, matter, affair, event). The character pairing reveals the Chinese intuition that narrative is the retelling of what was, the past made into shape and handed forward. See the companion entry 事 for the wider family of "matter" words.</div>

          <div class="example"><div class="ex-cn">奶奶每天晚上都给我讲一个故事。</div><div class="ex-py">Nǎinai měitiān wǎnshang dōu gěi wǒ jiǎng yí gè gùshì.</div><div class="ex-en">Grandma tells me a story every evening.</div></div>

        <div class="card-def">故 (old, former) + 人 (rén, person). An old friend, specifically one known from an earlier period of life, now perhaps far away or long unseen. 故人 belongs to the literary and poetic register, common in classical verse about parting and reunion; the everyday equivalent is 老朋友 (lǎo péngyou). The same 故 appears in 故旧 (gùjiù, old friends and associates). Note that 故人 can also be a delicate way to refer to someone who has died, drawing on the deceased sense of 故.</div>

        <div class="card-head"><span class="card-cn">故宫</span><span class="card-py">Gùgōng</span><span class="card-en">the Forbidden City; the Old Palace</span></div>

Literally "the Old Palace," the formal Chinese name for the former imperial palace complex in Beijing, known in English as the Forbidden City.     <span class="section-anchor" id="deliberate"></span>
    <div class="section-head"><span class="sh-cn">故意</span><span class="sh-py">gùyì</span><span class="sh-en">Deliberately &amp; Hence: On Purpose, and the Literary Therefore</span><span class="sh-rule"></span></div>

    <div class="scholar" data-glyph="故">
      <div class="scholar-label">故 as will and as connective , the strike radical comes forward</div>
      <p>Two more senses round out the character. First, deliberateness: when 攵, the strike radical, brings its sense of willed action to the fore, 故 means "on purpose, deliberately." 故意 (gùyì) is the everyday adverb for doing something intentionally, and it carries a faint negative charge, since one usually accuses another of doing harm 故意 rather than praising a kindness done 故意. Second, the connective: in classical and formal modern prose, a lone 故 means "therefore, hence," pointing back to a cause just stated. This is the cause sense turned into grammar. A classical argument runs 因为A，故B (because A, therefore B). The literary 故 still surfaces in formal writing, legal language, and set phrases such as 故曰 (gù yuē, hence it is said).</p>
    </div>

    <div class="cards">
      <div class="card c-teal">
        <div class="card-head"><span class="card-cn">故意</span><span class="card-py">gùyì</span><span class="card-en">deliberately; on purpose; intentionally</span></div>
        <div class="tags"><span class="tag tag-adj">Adj 形容词</span></div>
        <div class="card-def">故 (deliberate) + 意 (yì, intention, will, mind). To do something on purpose, with conscious intent. Used adverbially: 他故意迟到 (he was late on purpose), 不是故意的 (didn't mean to, it wasn't intentional). 故意 most often colors an action as deliberately unkind or provocative, so 不是故意的 is the standard apology for an accidental slight. In legal language 故意 (intent) contrasts sharply with 过失 (guòshī, negligence), and the distinction can decide a verdict.</div>
        <div class="examples">
          <div class="example"><div class="ex-cn">对不起，我不是故意的。</div><div class="ex-py">Duìbuqǐ, wǒ búshì gùyì de.</div><div class="ex-en">Sorry, I didn't do it on purpose.</div></div>
          <div class="example"><div class="ex-cn">他故意把话说得含糊，让人摸不清他的意思。</div><div class="ex-py">Tā gùyì bǎ huà shuō de hánhú, ràng rén mō bu qīng tā de yìsi.</div><div class="ex-en">He deliberately spoke vaguely, leaving people unable to grasp his meaning.</div></div>
        </div>
        <div class="note">
          <span class="note-label">辨析 biànxī · 故意 vs 特意</span>
          故意 (gùyì) and 特意 (tèyì) both mean "on purpose," but their feeling is opposite. 故意 usually marks an act done to annoy, provoke, or deceive, so it carries a negative tilt: 他故意气我 (he provoked me on purpose). 特意 means "specially, going out of one's way," and is warm or neutral: 我特意为你做了这道菜 (I made this dish specially for you). Swap them and the tone flips: 特意 frames effort as a gift, 故意 frames intent as a fault.
        </div>
      </div>
      <div class="card c-violet">
        <div class="card-head"><span class="card-cn">故</span><span class="card-py">gù</span><span class="card-en">therefore; hence; for this reason (literary)</span></div>
        <div class="tags"><span class="tag tag-v">Conj 连词</span></div>
        <div class="card-def">A lone 故 in classical and formal prose works as a connective: "therefore, hence." It points back to the cause just stated, the cause sense of the character turned into a logical hinge. The pattern is 因为A，故B (because A, hence B), or simply 故 at the head of a concluding clause. It belongs to written and formal register; in speech one says 所以 (suǒyǐ) instead. The literary 故 survives in fixed expressions like 故曰 (hence it is said) and in the measured prose of essays, contracts, and classical commentary.</div>
        <div class="examples">
          <div class="example"><div class="ex-cn">天行有常，不为尧存，不为桀亡，故应之以治则吉。</div><div class="ex-py">Tiān xíng yǒu cháng, bù wèi Yáo cún, bù wèi Jié wáng, gù yìng zhī yǐ zhì zé jí.</div><div class="ex-en">Heaven's course is constant, not preserved for the sage-king nor abolished for the tyrant; therefore meeting it with good order brings fortune. (after Xunzi)</div></div>
          <div class="example"><div class="ex-cn">证据不足，故本案不予立案。</div><div class="ex-py">Zhèngjù bùzú, gù běn àn bù yǔ lì'àn.</div><div class="ex-en">The evidence being insufficient, this case is therefore not accepted for filing.</div></div>
        </div>
      </div>
      <div class="card c-sienna">
        <div class="card-head"><span class="card-cn">病故</span><span class="card-py">bìnggù</span><span class="card-en">to die of illness; to pass away from sickness</span></div>
        <div class="tags"><span class="tag tag-v">V 动词</span></div>
        <div class="card-def">病 (bìng, illness) + 故 (here, to pass away). A measured, somewhat formal way to say that someone died of illness, used in obituaries, official notices, and dignified speech. The 故 draws on the deceased sense: the dead belong now to what has passed. The related 已故 (yǐgù, the late, deceased) is placed before a name, as in 已故的张教授 (the late Professor Zhang). Plain 去世 (qùshì) is the common neutral term for "to pass away"; 病故 specifies the cause as illness.</div>
        <div class="examples">
          <div class="example"><div class="ex-cn">老人去年因病故于家中，享年八十九岁。</div><div class="ex-py">Lǎorén qùnián yīn bìng gù yú jiā zhōng, xiǎngnián bāshíjiǔ suì.</div><div class="ex-en">The old man passed away from illness at home last year, at the age of eighty-nine.</div></div>
          <div class="example"><div class="ex-cn">这是已故作家留下的最后一部手稿。</div><div class="ex-py">Zhè shì yǐgù zuòjiā liúxià de zuìhòu yí bù shǒugǎo.</div><div class="ex-en">This is the last manuscript left behind by the late writer.</div></div>
        </div>

    <div class="section-head"><span class="sh-cn">成语</span><span class="sh-py">chéngyǔ</span><span class="sh-en">Idioms &amp; Set Phrases</span><span class="sh-rule"></span></div>

    <div class="chengyu-grid">
      <div class="cy">
        <span class="cy-cn">温故知新</span>
        <span class="cy-py">wēn gù zhī xīn</span>
        <span class="cy-en">review the old to learn the new: deepening understanding by returning to what is known</span>
        <span class="cy-note">温 (wēn, to warm, to review, as one reheats food) + 故 (the old, what was learned before) + 知 (zhī, to know) + 新 (xīn, the new). From the Analects of Confucius, Chapter 2.11: 温故而知新，可以为师矣, "He who reviews the old and discerns the new is fit to be a teacher." The Confucian theory of learning in four characters: mastery is not piling up fresh information but returning to foundational material and finding new meaning each time. The 故 here is the old sense of the character, the prior knowledge one warms over again. Still cited constantly in education and self-cultivation.</span>
      </div>
      <div class="cy">
        <span class="cy-cn">一见如故</span>
        <span class="cy-py">yī jiàn rú gù</span>
        <span class="cy-en">to feel like old friends at first meeting: instant rapport</span>
        <span class="cy-note">一 (yī, one) + 见 (jiàn, to meet) + 如 (rú, as if, like) + 故 (an old friend, drawing on the 故人 sense). To meet someone for the first time and feel as though you have known them for years. The phrase captures that rare warmth of immediate kinship, where strangers talk freely from the first moment as if resuming an old acquaintance. 他们一见如故，聊了整整一夜 (they hit it off at once and talked the whole night through). The 故 carries the full weight of "old friend," so the idiom collapses the distance between first sight and long friendship.</span>
      </div>
      <div class="cy">
        <span class="cy-cn">明知故犯</span>
        <span class="cy-py">míng zhī gù fàn</span>
        <span class="cy-en">to knowingly do wrong: to break a rule while fully aware it is wrong</span>
        <span class="cy-note">明 (míng, clearly) + 知 (zhī, to know) + 故 (deliberately, the willed-action sense) + 犯 (fàn, to violate, to offend). To commit an offense while clearly knowing it is forbidden, the opposite of an honest mistake. The 故 here is the deliberate sense: not only did the person know the rule, they broke it on purpose. The idiom is a sharp reproach, common in moral and legal contexts: 明知故犯，罪加一等 (to offend knowingly compounds the guilt). It pairs the knowing (知) with the willing (故) to mark the offense as fully culpable.</span>
      </div>
      <div class="cy">
        <span class="cy-cn">故弄玄虚</span>
        <span class="cy-py">gù nòng xuán xū</span>
        <span class="cy-en">to deliberately mystify: to make something seem more obscure or profound than it is</span>
        <span class="cy-note">故 (deliberately) + 弄 (nòng, to play with, to manipulate) + 玄 (xuán, mysterious, dark, profound) + 虚 (xū, empty, unreal). To purposely dress something up as mysterious in order to confuse or impress, when in fact there is little substance behind it. The 故 is again the deliberate sense, marking the mystification as a calculated act. Used to criticize people who hide simple matters behind smoke: 别故弄玄虚，有话直说 (stop being deliberately mysterious, just say it plainly). 玄虚 by itself means a deceptive show of depth.</span>
      </div>

    <div class="scholar" data-glyph="故">
      <div class="scholar-label">记忆法 jìyìfǎ · Master Retention Image</div>
      <p>Picture a hand with a rod tapping at something old. That is 攵, the strike radical, set beside 古, the ancient. The single image holds the whole character: a deliberate hand acting upon what is already old. From that one picture the five senses unfold like spokes. The thing the hand acts upon is a cause (缘故). What it taps is old and former (故乡, 故人, 故事). The hand acts on purpose (故意). Trace the rod back along the strike and you find the reason, so 故 also means therefore (因为A，故B). And the oldest things of all, those wholly past, are the dead (病故, 已故).</p>
      <p>The discipline for a learner is to feel which spoke is active in a given compound. In 故乡 and 故事 the warmth of the former rules; the home and the tale are cherished, not blamed. In 故意 and 故弄玄虚 the willed hand comes forward, usually with a critical edge. In 故障 and 事故 the hidden cause is what matters, the fault to be traced. And the lone literary 故 is pure logic, the cause turned into a hinge between premise and conclusion.</p>
      <p>Hold 故 against its near neighbor 旧. Both reach into the past, but 故 is the former, the prior, the cause-that-was, while 旧 is the worn, the used, the thing aged by handling. An old friend known long ago is a 故人; a coat worn thin is 旧. 温故知新 uses 故 because the old here is prior knowledge to be warmed over, not a worn object. When you meet 故, ask which thread the context has pulled: the cause behind, the time before, the will within, the hence that follows, or the life now wholly past.</p>
