    <span class="section-anchor" id="etymology"></span>
    <div class="section-head"><span class="sh-cn">字源</span><span class="sh-py">zìyuán</span><span class="sh-en">Etymology &amp; Structure</span><span class="sh-rule"></span></div>
    <div class="scholar" data-glyph="病">
      <div class="scholar-label">字源洞见 zìyuán dòngjiàn · Etymological Insight</div>
      <p>病 = 疒 + 丙. The outer frame 疒 (the sickness radical) is the semantic component, and 丙 (bǐng) sitting inside it is the phonetic. 疒 itself is a compressed picture: a person lying down (人) tipped against a low bed or couch, with two short strokes that older paleographers read as drops of sweat or as the lines of the bedframe. Wherever 疒 appears as a radical, the word concerns being unwell: 疼 (téng) ache, 痛 (tòng) pain, 疲 (pí) fatigue, 疯 (fēng) madness. It is one of the most semantically transparent radicals in the script.</p>
      <p>The phonetic 丙 carries the sound and only the sound. In the old Heavenly Stems counting system 丙 is the third stem, and on its own it has no medical meaning; it was selected because its Old Chinese reading was close to the reading of 病. The Shuōwén Jiězì glosses 病 plainly as 疾加也, an illness that has worsened, distinguishing it from the lighter 疾 (jí). In the earliest texts 疾 is the ordinary ailment and 病 is the grave one, the sickness that has crossed into danger.</p>
      <p>From the concrete sense of bodily sickness the character extended outward to anything wrong or defective. A flaw in an argument, a fault in a person's character, a bug in a machine: all can be a 病. This is the same move English makes when it calls a society "sick" or a plan "ailing." The radical keeps the body in view even when the subject is abstract, which is why 毛病 (máobìng) covers both a chronic cough and an annoying habit.</p>
      <p>Japanese borrowed the character as 病 (byō / yamai) with the identical sickness radical, and it anchors the everyday word 病気 (byōki, illness) and 病院 (byōin, hospital). The Korean reading is 병 (byeong). Across the whole Sinographic world the glyph names the same thing: a body laid up on a bed, no longer whole.</p>

    <div class="section-head"><span class="sh-cn">疾病</span><span class="sh-py">jíbìng</span><span class="sh-en">Illness Words · Falling Ill</span><span class="sh-rule"></span></div>
    <div class="cards">
      <div class="card c-red">
        <div class="card-head"><span class="card-cn">生病</span><span class="card-py">shēng bìng</span><span class="card-en">to fall ill, get sick</span></div>
        <div class="tags"><span class="tag tag-v">V 动词 dòngcí</span></div>
        <div class="card-def">生 (shēng) means to give rise to or produce, so 生病 is literally "to produce illness," the standard verb for getting sick. It is a separable verb: an object or aspect marker can split the two halves.</div>
        <div class="examples">
          <div class="example"><div class="ex-cn">他昨天生病了，没来上班。</div><div class="ex-py">Tā zuótiān shēng bìng le, méi lái shàngbān.</div><div class="ex-en">He fell ill yesterday and did not come to work.</div></div>
          <div class="example"><div class="ex-cn">孩子一生病，全家都紧张。</div><div class="ex-py">Háizi yì shēng bìng, quánjiā dōu jǐnzhāng.</div><div class="ex-en">The moment the child gets sick, the whole family tenses up.</div></div>
        </div>
      </div>
      <div class="card c-ochre">
        <div class="card-head"><span class="card-cn">病人</span><span class="card-py">bìng rén</span><span class="card-en">patient, sick person</span></div>
        <div class="tags"><span class="tag tag-n">N 名词 míngcí</span></div>
        <div class="card-def">病 + 人 (rén, person): a sick person, the general word for a patient in a hospital or clinic. It names the person by their condition rather than their role, unlike 患者 (huànzhě), which is the more clinical register used in medical writing.</div>
        <div class="examples">
          <div class="example"><div class="ex-cn">医生正在给病人看病。</div><div class="ex-py">Yīshēng zhèngzài gěi bìngrén kàn bìng.</div><div class="ex-en">The doctor is examining the patient.</div></div>
        </div>
        <div class="note"><span class="note-label">辨析 biànxī · 病人 vs. 患者</span> 病人 is everyday speech; 患者 is the formal term in news reports and hospital signage.</div>
      </div>
      <div class="card c-teal">
        <div class="card-head"><span class="card-cn">看病</span><span class="card-py">kàn bìng</span><span class="card-en">to see a doctor; to treat a patient</span></div>
        <div class="tags"><span class="tag tag-v">V 动词 dòngcí</span></div>
        <div class="card-def">看 (kàn) means to look at, so 看病 covers both sides of the consultation: a patient goes to 看病 (see a doctor about an illness) and a doctor 看病 (examines and treats the sick). Context fixes which side is meant.</div>
        <div class="examples">
          <div class="example"><div class="ex-cn">我下午要去医院看病。</div><div class="ex-py">Wǒ xiàwǔ yào qù yīyuàn kàn bìng.</div><div class="ex-en">I have to go to the hospital this afternoon to see a doctor.</div></div>
        </div>
      </div>
      <div class="card c-violet">
        <div class="card-head"><span class="card-cn">疾病</span><span class="card-py">jí bìng</span><span class="card-en">disease, illness (formal)</span></div>
        <div class="tags"><span class="tag tag-n">N 名词 míngcí</span></div>
        <div class="card-def">疾 (jí) and 病 are near-synonyms paired into one formal noun. In classical usage 疾 was the slight ailment and 病 the serious one; fused together, 疾病 is the neutral cover term for disease in medical and official language.</div>
        <div class="examples">
          <div class="example"><div class="ex-cn">心脏疾病是常见的健康问题。</div><div class="ex-py">Xīnzàng jíbìng shì chángjiàn de jiànkāng wèntí.</div><div class="ex-en">Heart disease is a common health problem.</div></div>
        </div>

    <div class="cards">
      <div class="card c-sienna">
        <div class="card-head"><span class="card-cn">毛病</span><span class="card-py">máo bìng</span><span class="card-en">defect, flaw, bad habit; malfunction</span></div>
        <div class="tags"><span class="tag tag-n">N 名词 míngcí</span></div>
        <div class="card-def">毛 (máo) means fine hair or fur, here the trivial detail; 毛病 is a small fault that keeps recurring. It stretches across a machine that breaks down, a person's annoying habit, and a minor recurring ailment, all the everyday senses of "something is wrong with it."</div>
        <div class="examples">
          <div class="example"><div class="ex-cn">这台电脑老出毛病。</div><div class="ex-py">Zhè tái diànnǎo lǎo chū máobìng.</div><div class="ex-en">This computer keeps malfunctioning.</div></div>
          <div class="example"><div class="ex-cn">他有个挑食的毛病。</div><div class="ex-py">Tā yǒu ge tiāoshí de máobìng.</div><div class="ex-en">He has a bad habit of being a picky eater.</div></div>
        </div>
        <div class="note"><span class="note-label">辨析 biànxī · 毛病 vs. 缺点</span> 毛病 is concrete and often fixable (a fault, a glitch); 缺点 (quēdiǎn) is an abstract shortcoming or weakness.</div>
      </div>
      <div class="card c-red">
        <div class="card-head"><span class="card-cn">病情</span><span class="card-py">bìng qíng</span><span class="card-en">condition of an illness</span></div>
        <div class="tags"><span class="tag tag-n">N 名词 míngcí</span></div>
        <div class="card-def">病 + 情 (qíng, situation, state): the state of a sickness, how the illness stands. Doctors use it to report whether a patient is stable, worsening, or improving.</div>
        <div class="examples">
          <div class="example"><div class="ex-cn">病人的病情已经稳定了。</div><div class="ex-py">Bìngrén de bìngqíng yǐjīng wěndìng le.</div><div class="ex-en">The patient's condition has already stabilized.</div></div>
        </div>
      </div>
      <div class="card c-ochre">
        <div class="card-head"><span class="card-cn">病房</span><span class="card-py">bìng fáng</span><span class="card-en">hospital ward, sickroom</span></div>
        <div class="tags"><span class="tag tag-n">N 名词 míngcí</span></div>
        <div class="card-def">病 + 房 (fáng, room): the room where patients are kept, a hospital ward. It pairs naturally with 病床 (bìngchuáng, hospital bed), bringing the original sickbed of the radical 疒 full circle.</div>
        <div class="examples">
          <div class="example"><div class="ex-cn">他被送进了急诊病房。</div><div class="ex-py">Tā bèi sòng jìn le jízhěn bìngfáng.</div><div class="ex-en">He was taken into the emergency ward.</div></div>

      <div class="scholar-label">记忆法 jìyìfǎ · Master Retention Image</div>
      <p>See the radical 疒 as a low wooden bed leaned up on its side against a wall, a sick person stretched along it with two beads of sweat sliding off the frame. That tilted bed is the sickness radical, and it is the first thing your eye should catch in 病. Whatever sits inside the bed, the body is laid up.</p>
      <p>Inside this one lies 丙 (bǐng), the third Heavenly Stem, doing nothing but holding the sound. Treat 丙 as a faceless lodger you cannot quite identify: it tells you how to say the word, bìng, in a sharp falling tone, like a doctor's clipped diagnosis. The 4th tone drop mirrors the patient sinking back onto the pillow.</p>
      <p>Then watch the bed travel. Add a person and you get 病人, someone laid up on it. Add poison and you get 病毒, the unseen thing that put them there. Add a room and you get 病房, a hall of these beds in a row. Add fine hairs, 毛病, and the bed shrinks to a nagging little flaw that will not stay fixed. Every compound is just a new visitor to the same sideways sickbed.</p>
