    <span class="section-anchor" id="etymology"></span>
    <div class="section-head"><span class="sh-cn">字源</span><span class="sh-py">zìyuán</span><span class="sh-en">Etymology &amp; Structure</span><span class="sh-rule"></span></div>
    <div class="scholar" data-glyph="穿">
      <div class="scholar-label">字源洞见 zìyuán dòngjiàn · Etymological Insight</div>
      <p>穿 = 穴 over 牙, a cave-mouth set above a tooth. 牙 (yá, molar) is the semantic element, the gnawing edge that bores a hole; 穴 (xué, cave/cavity) supplies the sound and frames the result as an opening cut clean through. The Shuōwén reads the whole as "to bore through," 通也, a tooth working an opening into solid ground.</p>
      <p>Hold the picture literally: a tooth set under the roof of a cave, grinding until it breaks the far side. That is the core sense, to pierce, to make a hole that goes all the way through. The small-seal form already shows the cave radical capping a clearly drawn 牙, the two stacked exactly as they sit today, with 穴 squared at the top and the tooth-strokes splayed beneath.</p>
      <p>From "bore through a wall" the verb extended along two lines. One follows the hole through space: to pass through, to cross, to thread, as a needle passes through cloth or a road passes through a mountain. The other threads the body through an opening: to slip an arm through a sleeve, a leg through a trouser-hole, which is how 穿 became the everyday word for putting on and wearing clothes. The clothing sense and the piercing sense are one motion, something passing through an aperture.</p>
      <p>Japanese keeps 穿 (sen) in the bookish 穿孔 (senkō, "to perforate") and 穿鑿 (sensaku, "to bore into, to pry"), holding the original drilling sense, while Mandarin let the clothing meaning grow into the most common use of all.</p>
    </div>

    <span class="section-anchor" id="wear"></span>
    <div class="section-head"><span class="sh-cn">穿戴</span><span class="sh-py">chuāndài</span><span class="sh-en">Clothing the Body</span><span class="sh-rule"></span></div>
    <div class="scholar" data-glyph="衣">
      <div class="scholar-label">引申义 yǐnshēnyì · Extended Sense</div>
      <p>To put on clothing is, in Chinese, to thread the body through an opening, the same motion as a needle through cloth. 穿 covers anything the body passes into: shirts, trousers, shoes, socks. It pairs against 戴 (dài), which covers what hangs on or wraps around without threading through, hats, glasses, watches, gloves. Learn the split early: you 穿 a coat but 戴 a scarf.</p>
    </div>

        <div class="tags"><span class="tag tag-vn">V+C 动补 dòngbǔ</span></div>
        <div class="card-def">穿 (to thread on) + 上 (shàng, up/onto, here a result complement). The complement 上 marks the garment as now in place. 穿上 stresses the completed act of getting it on, against bare 穿 which can describe the habit of wearing.</div>
        <div class="examples">
          <div class="example"><div class="ex-cn">快穿上鞋，我们要出门了。</div><div class="ex-py">Kuài chuān shàng xié, wǒmen yào chūmén le.</div><div class="ex-en">Hurry, put your shoes on, we're leaving.</div></div>
        </div>
        <div class="note"><span class="note-label">辨析 biànxī · 穿上 vs. 脱下</span> 穿上 puts a garment on; its opposite is 脱下 (tuō xià), to take it off. 穿 and 脱 are the wear/remove pair for threaded clothing.</div>

    <div class="scholar" data-glyph="穿" style="margin-top:2.5rem;">
      <div class="scholar-label">记忆法 jìyìfǎ · Master Retention Image</div>
      <p>Picture the roof of a low cave (穴, the cap of the character) and a single molar (牙, the splayed strokes beneath) set against it, grinding. Hour after hour the tooth works the stone until daylight breaks through the far side, a clean hole bored all the way out. That is 穿 in one frame: a tooth under a cave-mouth, drilling an opening that goes through.</p>
      <p>Run the compounds back through that hole. 穿过 (chuānguò) is your body walking through the bored opening and out the other side, through a forest or a tunnel. 看穿 (kànchuān) is your gaze doing the boring, the look drilling through a lie until the truth shows on the far side. 滴水穿石 (dī shuǐ chuān shí) is the same hole, drilled this time by nothing but a patient drip of water.</p>
      <p>Now turn the hole into a sleeve. When you dress, your arm is the thing passing through the opening, which is why 穿衣 (chuān yī) and 穿上 (chuān shàng) use the very same character as the drilling tooth. Whether it is an arm into a coat or a needle into cloth, 穿 is always one motion: something threading clean through an aperture.</p>
