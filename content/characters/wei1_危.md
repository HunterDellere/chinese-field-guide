    <span class="section-anchor" id="etymology"></span>
    <div class="section-head"><span class="sh-cn">字源</span><span class="sh-py">zìyuán</span><span class="sh-en">Etymology &amp; Structure</span><span class="sh-rule"></span></div>
    <div class="scholar" data-glyph="危">
      <div class="scholar-label">字源洞见 zìyuán dòngjiàn · Etymological Insight</div>
      <p>危 stacks two bodies in a single vertical column: 危 = 厃 + 㔾. On top sits 厃 (yán), the jut of a cliff face, a rock ledge that hangs out over empty air. Beneath it crouches 㔾 (jié), the bent form of a kneeling person, knees folded, spine curled. A figure pressed against the foot of an overhang, with all that stone leaning out above the skull. That is the picture the glyph holds: a person caught under the precipice, the stone and the body read as one scene.</p>
      <p>The cliff component does double duty. 厃 fixes the meaning, the high and dangerous edge, and it also lends the sound, so 危 is a pictophonetic where the semantic and phonetic load rest on the same top piece. The Shuōwén Jiězì reads the character as a person standing at a high place, in dread, and the felt logic is identical: height and fear braided together. Standing on a ledge and standing under one are the same vertigo seen from two sides.</p>
      <p>From that physical precariousness the senses fan out. First the literal high place, surviving in classical lines where 危 simply means lofty or steep, a tower 危 against the sky. Then the danger that height implies, since anything that high can fall, and anything beneath it can be crushed. By the time the word reaches modern Mandarin the abstract sense has won: 危 is danger and peril, the verb to endanger, the adjective for anything teetering on the edge of collapse. The lofty reading lingers only in old poetry and a handful of fixed phrases.</p>
      <p>Japanese keeps the same glyph in 危ない (abunai), the everyday word called out as a warning, watch out, dangerous. Korean reads it 위 (wi), embedded in 위험 (wiheom), the exact cognate of 危险. Across all three the character does one job: it marks the moment just before the fall.</p>

    <span class="section-anchor" id="crisis"></span>
    <div class="section-head"><span class="sh-cn">危机</span><span class="sh-py">wēijī</span><span class="sh-en">The Turning Point</span><span class="sh-rule"></span></div>
    <div class="scholar" data-glyph="机">
      <div class="scholar-label">词义洞见 cíyì dòngjiàn · Lexical Insight</div>
      <p>危机 pairs 危 (danger) with 机 (the pivot, the mechanism, the moment a trigger releases). The popular Western reading, that the word means danger plus opportunity, is a romantic stretch: 机 here carries its sense of crux or critical juncture, not opportunity in the cheerful sense. Still, the glyph holds a real truth. A 危机 is the point where a situation is wound to its tightest, the spring that will either snap or fire. It is danger arrived at its decisive turn.</p>
    </div>

    <div class="scholar" data-glyph="危" style="margin-top:2.5rem;">
      <div class="scholar-label">记忆法 jìyìfǎ · Master Retention Image</div>
      <p>Picture a narrow mountain path. A traveler has dropped to his knees on a stone ledge, hands flat against the rock, because directly above him the cliff face juts out, a slab of grey stone overhanging his bent back. He does not look up. He waits, folded small, while a thread of gravel hisses down past his ear. That crouched body is 㔾, the kneeling figure at the bottom of the glyph; the overhang above is 厃, the jut of cliff that both names the height and gives the word its sound.</p>
      <p>Hold that frame and the whole family falls into place. Pair the danger with 险 (steepness) and you get 危险, the everyday word: this ledge is dangerous. Wind the moment to its breaking point, the spring about to fire, and the gravel becomes a 危机, the crisis at its turn. Add time pressure, a rockslide already loosening, and the situation is 危急. Let the rock actually strike and you have 危害, harm done.</p>
      <p>And remember the older face hidden in the same stone: 危 once meant simply high. The very ledge that endangers the traveler is, seen from the valley, a 危楼, a tower lifting into the clear air. Height and dread are one glyph because they are one feeling, the body's lurch at the edge of the fall.</p>
