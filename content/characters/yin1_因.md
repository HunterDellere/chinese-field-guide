    <!-- ── ETYMOLOGY ── -->
    <span class="section-anchor" id="etymology"></span>
    <div class="section-head">
      <span class="sh-cn">字源</span>
      <span class="sh-py">zìyuán</span>
      <span class="sh-en">Etymology &amp; Structure</span>
      <span class="sh-rule"></span>
    </div>

    <div class="scholar" data-glyph="因">
      <div class="scholar-label">字源洞见 zìyuán dòngjiàn · Etymological Insight</div>
      <p><strong>因 yīn shows a person lying inside an enclosure.</strong> The outer 囗 (wéi, an enclosed space) frames a 大 (dà, a person with arms and legs outstretched) resting within it. The early reading is concrete: a person stretched out on a mat or bed, the thing one lies upon. From "the mat one rests on" came the sense of a support, a basis, the ground one leans on. That is the hinge of the whole character: what you rely on becomes what a thing rests upon, and what a thing rests upon is its cause.</p>
      <p>Three senses branch from the figure on the mat:</p>
      <p><strong>1. Cause, reason.</strong> The basis behind an outcome, what a result rests on: 原因 yuányīn (cause; reason), 因素 yīnsù (factor), 起因 qǐyīn (the cause that starts a thing).<br>
      <strong>2. Because of, due to.</strong> Used as a connective to name the reason for something: 因为 yīnwèi (because), 因此 yīncǐ (therefore; for this reason).<br>
      <strong>3. To follow, to go along with.</strong> The older sense of leaning on a given ground: to take an existing thing as one's basis and act accordingly, as in 因地制宜 yīn dì zhì yí (adapt measures to local conditions).</p>
      <p>The thread is the act of resting upon. A mat is what the body rests on; a basis is what an action rests on; a cause is what an effect rests on. Whether 因 names the reason, the connective that points to it, or the act of following an existing ground, it keeps the same image: one thing leaning on another that came before it.</p>
    </div>

        </div>
        <div class="note">
          <span class="note-label">辨析 biànxī · 原因 vs. 理由</span>
          <strong>原因</strong> is the objective cause, what actually brought a result about, neutral and factual. <strong>理由</strong> lǐyóu is the justification a person gives, the grounds offered to explain or defend a choice. A delay has an 原因 (the traffic); a person offers a 理由 for being late (an excuse or a reason they put forward).

    <!-- ── BECAUSE ── -->
    <span class="section-anchor" id="because"></span>
    <div class="section-head">
      <span class="sh-cn">因为</span>
      <span class="sh-py">yīnwèi</span>
      <span class="sh-en">Because &amp; Therefore</span>
      <span class="sh-rule"></span>
    </div>

    <div class="scholar" data-glyph="因">
      <div class="scholar-label">语法洞见 yǔfǎ dòngjiàn · Grammatical Insight</div>
      <p>The busiest modern job of 因 is as a connective that names a reason. <strong>因为</strong> yīnwèi introduces the cause clause and pairs naturally with <strong>所以</strong> suǒyǐ in the result clause: 因为下雨，所以我们没去 "because it rained, we didn't go." The cause comes first, marked by 因为; the result follows, often marked by 所以. Either marker can be dropped once the relationship is clear.</p>
      <p>The mirror image is <strong>因此</strong> yīncǐ, literally "because of this," which sits at the head of the result clause and points back at a cause already stated: 他病了，因此没来上班 "he was ill, and therefore did not come to work." Its close relative 因而 yīn'ér works the same way in more written registers. Where 因为 looks forward to a cause it is about to give, 因此 and 因而 look back at a cause already given and draw the conclusion. The literary 由于 yóuyú overlaps with 因为 but leans formal and prefers the front of a sentence.</p>
    </div>

    <!-- ── FRAME ── -->
    <span class="section-anchor" id="frame"></span>
    <div class="section-head">
      <span class="sh-cn">因果</span>
      <span class="sh-py">yīnguǒ</span>
      <span class="sh-en">Cause and Effect: the 因 / 果 Frame</span>
      <span class="sh-rule"></span>
    </div>

    <div class="scholar" data-glyph="因">
      <div class="scholar-label">语义洞见 yǔyì dòngjiàn · Semantic Insight</div>
      <p>因 has a natural partner in 果 guǒ, "fruit, result." Together they name the whole arc from cause to consequence: <strong>因果</strong> yīnguǒ, cause and effect. The image is agricultural and apt: 因 is the seed-ground you rest a thing on, 果 is the fruit that grows out of it. What you plant in the 因 is what you reap in the 果.</p>
      <p>The pair carries weight in Buddhist thought, where 因果 names the law of karma: every deed is a cause that ripens into a result, and 种因得果 "plant the cause, gain the fruit" captures the moral physics of action and consequence. In ordinary speech 因果关系 yīnguǒ guānxì simply means "a causal relationship," the link a scientist or a detective tries to establish. The four-character 前因后果 qián yīn hòu guǒ stretches the frame across time: the causes before and the effects after, the full story from start to finish.</p>
    </div>

    <!-- RETENTION IMAGE -->
    <div class="scholar" data-glyph="因" style="margin-top:2.5rem;">
      <div class="scholar-label">记忆法 jìyìfǎ · Master Retention Image</div>
      <p>See the enclosure 囗 as a mat, and the 大 inside it as a person stretched out, resting upon it. That is the literal picture: a body lying on what supports it. Hold the idea of resting-upon and every sense of 因 follows from it.</p>
      <p>What the body rests on is a support; what an action rests on is its basis; what an effect rests on is its cause (原因). When you point at that cause to explain a result, 因 becomes the connective: because (因为), and therefore (因此). And when you take an existing ground and build on it instead of fighting it, 因 is to follow, as in adapting to local conditions (因地制宜). Plant the cause and you reap the fruit, 果: that is the whole 因果 arc, from the mat at the bottom to the harvest at the end.</p>
