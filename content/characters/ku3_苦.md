    <div class="section-head"><span class="sh-cn">字源</span><span class="sh-py">zìyuán</span><span class="sh-en">Etymology &amp; Structure</span><span class="sh-rule"></span></div>
    <div class="scholar" data-glyph="苦">
      <div class="scholar-label">字源洞见 zìyuán dòngjiàn · Etymological Insight</div>
      <p>苦 is a tidy pictophonetic: 苦 = 艹 + 古. The grass radical 艹 (cǎo) sits on top and carries the meaning, marking the word as a plant; 古 (gǔ) sits below and carries the sound, supplying the near-rhyme that gave kǔ its reading. The whole was originally the name of a specific bitter herb, a wild plant you would spit out the moment it touched your tongue.</p>
      <p>The Shuōwén Jiězì glosses 苦 as 大苦, identifying it with a bitter medicinal plant. The seal form already shows the three-stalk grass crown above the 古 element, the same stacked arrangement that survives in the modern square. Nothing pictographic happens in the lower half: 古 is there for its voice, not its picture of an old mouth.</p>
      <p>From the taste, the sense reached outward by an obvious bridge. A bitter herb is unpleasant to swallow; an unpleasant life is bitter to live. So 苦 stretched from flavor to hardship (苦难 kǔnàn, suffering), then to the effort that hardship demands (刻苦 kèkǔ, painstaking), and finally to the adverb that colors a verb with grim persistence: 苦练 (kǔliàn) is to train bitterly, to drill until it hurts.</p>
      <p>Buddhism deepened the word permanently. When the First Noble Truth was rendered into Chinese, 苦 (kǔ) became the standard translation of Sanskrit duḥkha, the pervasive unsatisfactoriness of existence. To Chinese ears, the doctrine that 人生是苦 (rénshēng shì kǔ), life is suffering, lands with the directness of a flavor you already know.</p>

    <div class="section-head"><span class="sh-cn">辛劳</span><span class="sh-py">xīnláo</span><span class="sh-en">Toil &amp; Effort</span><span class="sh-rule"></span></div>
    <div class="cards">
      <div class="card c-red">
        <div class="card-head"><span class="card-cn">辛苦</span><span class="card-py">xīnkǔ</span><span class="card-en">hard, toilsome; thanks for your trouble</span></div>
        <div class="tags"><span class="tag tag-adj">Adj 形容词 xíngróngcí</span></div>
        <div class="card-def">辛 (xīn), pungent or laborious, pairs with 苦 (kǔ), bitter, to name effort that wears you down. As a set phrase it doubles as warm thanks: telling someone 辛苦了 acknowledges the toil they just put in for you.</div>
        <div class="examples">
          <div class="example"><div class="ex-cn">这份工作很辛苦，但我喜欢。</div><div class="ex-py">Zhè fèn gōngzuò hěn xīnkǔ, dàn wǒ xǐhuan.</div><div class="ex-en">This job is hard, but I like it.</div></div>
          <div class="example"><div class="ex-cn">大家辛苦了，先休息一下吧。</div><div class="ex-py">Dàjiā xīnkǔ le, xiān xiūxi yíxià ba.</div><div class="ex-en">Thanks for your hard work, everyone, let us rest a moment.</div></div>
        </div>
        <div class="note"><span class="note-label">辨析 biànxī · 辛苦 vs. 痛苦</span> 辛苦 is physical toil and fatigue; 痛苦 (tòngkǔ) is inner pain or anguish. A long workday is 辛苦; a grief is 痛苦.</div>
      </div>
      <div class="card c-ochre">
        <div class="card-head"><span class="card-cn">吃苦</span><span class="card-py">chīkǔ</span><span class="card-en">to endure hardship; to bear suffering</span></div>

        <div class="card-def">Literally to eat bitterness: 吃 (chī) takes 苦 as its object, treating hardship as something swallowed. A core virtue word, the capacity to 吃苦 is praised as the backbone of anyone who endures and gets ahead.</div>
        <div class="examples">
          <div class="example"><div class="ex-cn">年轻人应该多吃点苦。</div><div class="ex-py">Niánqīng rén yīnggāi duō chī diǎn kǔ.</div><div class="ex-en">Young people ought to endure a bit more hardship.</div></div>
        </div>
      </div>

    <div class="scholar" data-glyph="苦" style="margin-top:2.5rem;">
      <div class="scholar-label">记忆法 jìyìfǎ · Master Retention Image</div>
      <p>Picture a bowl of dark herbal medicine cooling on the table, a clump of wild grass still floating in it. You raise it to your lips, and the taste is so bitter your face puckers. That clump of grass is the radical 艹 sitting on top of 苦; the bowl itself, old and well-used, is 古 (gǔ), the ancient sound underneath. Grass over old: a bitter old remedy.</p>
      <p>Now follow the bitterness outward. The same drink that pinches your tongue is what 良药苦口 promises: good medicine tastes bad. Every compound on this page is some version of that swallow. 吃苦 (chīkǔ) is choosing to drink it; 刻苦 (kèkǔ) is drinking it daily until it sharpens you; 痛苦 (tòngkǔ) is when the bitterness reaches the heart, not just the tongue.</p>
      <p>Hold the last note: 苦尽甘来 (kǔ jìn gān lái). You finish the bitter cup, and only then does the sweet arrive. Whenever you meet 苦, taste the grass-over-古 bitterness first, then remember it is the kind that, drunk to the bottom, turns sweet.</p>
