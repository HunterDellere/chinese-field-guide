    <span class="section-anchor" id="etymology"></span>
    <div class="section-head"><span class="sh-cn">字源</span><span class="sh-py">zìyuán</span><span class="sh-en">Etymology &amp; Structure</span><span class="sh-rule"></span></div>
    <div class="scholar" data-glyph="害">
      <div class="scholar-label">字源洞见 zìyuán dòngjiàn · Etymological Insight</div>
      <p>害 stacks three pieces under one cover: the roof 宀 on top, a cutting element below it, and a mouth 口 at the base. Read the structure as ⿱宀(丰口): a sheltered space, an edge that severs, and the mouth that speaks. The graph was assembled for its meaning rather than its sound, so there is no clean phonetic to point at; every stroke pulls toward the idea of injury done within an enclosed place.</p>
      <p>The seal form already shows the roof drawn tight over the lower elements, the way a household closes around a quarrel. The Shuōwén Jiězì glosses 害 as 傷 (shāng), to wound, and ties the lower mouth to slander and dispute: harm that begins as words spoken under a shared roof before it becomes a blade. The cutting stroke gives the violence; the 口 gives the human source of it.</p>
      <p>From that concrete wound the sense fans out. 害 becomes the noun for harm and calamity (灾害 zāihài), the verb for injuring a person or a cause (害人 hàirén), and the causative for inflicting a state on someone: 害病 (hàibìng), to be struck by illness. The most everyday branch turns inward, where the wound one fears is one's own: 害怕 (hàipà), to be afraid.</p>
      <p>Japanese keeps the character as 害 (gai) in 害虫 (gaichū, a harmful insect) and 公害 (kōgai, public pollution), and Korean reads it 해 (hae) in 해충 and 손해 (sonhae, loss). Across all three the through-line holds: a thing that wounds, whether a pest, a poison, or a word.</p>
    </div>

    <span class="section-anchor" id="fear"></span>
    <div class="section-head"><span class="sh-cn">恐惧</span><span class="sh-py">kǒngjù</span><span class="sh-en">害怕 · The Inward Wound</span><span class="sh-rule"></span></div>
    <div class="scholar" data-glyph="怕">
      <p>害 carries a sense that surprises learners: to fear. When the harm a person dreads is turned inward, 害 supplies the dread itself, paired with 怕 (pà, to fear) into the most common spoken word for being afraid. The injury here is anticipated rather than inflicted.</p>
    </div>

    <span class="section-anchor" id="harm"></span>
    <div class="section-head"><span class="sh-cn">损害</span><span class="sh-py">sǔnhài</span><span class="sh-en">Harm, Danger &amp; Calamity</span><span class="sh-rule"></span></div>
    <div class="pattern">
      <p>The original sense of 害 stays fully alive in modern compounds, where it marks anything that wounds: people, health, interests, the environment. It pairs left or right with verbs of damage (危, 损, 伤) and with the bound 有害／无害 frame that rates a thing as harmful or harmless.</p>
    </div>

    <div class="scholar" data-glyph="害" style="margin-top:2.5rem;">
      <div class="scholar-label">记忆法 jìyìfǎ · Master Retention Image</div>
      <p>Picture a low house at dusk with the roof 宀 pulled down over a single lit room. Inside, a blade lies on the table and a mouth is mid-sentence, the words sharp enough to draw blood. The shelter that should protect has become the place where the wound is made. That is 害: harm done under a roof, by an edge and by speech.</p>
      <p>Hold the scene and the compounds fall out of it. Turn the blade outward on others and you get 危害 and 伤害, the harming of people and interests. Turn the dread inward, toward the person who fears the room, and you get 害怕. Let the lit room hold a quarrel so cutting it draws admiration, and the same word swings to 厉害, fierce enough to impress.</p>
      <p>For the tone, hear 害 fall like the roof beam coming down: a sharp fourth-tone hài, the drop that ends an argument and seals the wound shut.</p>
