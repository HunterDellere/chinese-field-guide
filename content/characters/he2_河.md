    <span class="section-anchor" id="etymology"></span>
    <div class="section-head"><span class="sh-cn">字源</span><span class="sh-py">zìyuán</span><span class="sh-en">Etymology &amp; Structure</span><span class="sh-rule"></span></div>
    <div class="scholar" data-glyph="河">
      <div class="scholar-label">字源洞见 zìyuán dòngjiàn · Etymological Insight</div>
      <p>河 = 氵 + 可. The three-drop water radical 氵 on the left carries the meaning, water; 可 (kě) on the right supplies the sound. This is the standard pictophonetic pattern of the water family, where the splash of 氵 fixes the domain and a borrowed syllable does the rest. In archaic readings the 可 element sat much closer to the word's old pronunciation than the modern hé suggests.</p>
      <p>The first thing 河 named was not rivers in general but one river. In oracle-bone and bronze inscriptions 河 is the proper name of the great northern waterway the Shang and Zhou kings sacrificed to, the muddy artery we now call 黄河 (huánghé), the Yellow River. To early scribes 河 meant that river the way a capital city needs no qualifier. The Shuōwén Jiězì defines it precisely as a watercourse rising in the northwest and flowing east to the sea.</p>
      <p>Over the Han and after, the name slid loose from its single owner. 河 broadened from the Yellow River to any river of the north, then to rivers as a category, while its southern counterpart 江 (jiāng) traveled the same road out from the Yangtze. The split still echoes in usage: northern waterways skew toward 河, southern ones toward 江, a fossil of two rivers that each once owned a word.</p>
      <p>Japanese keeps the character as 河 (ka / kawa) for larger rivers and reads the everyday word 川 (kawa) more often; Korean carries it as 하 (ha), surfacing in place names and surnames. Across the script's reach 河 still trails its origin, a word that started as one river's name and never fully left the water it was born in.</p>

    <div class="scholar" data-glyph="河" style="margin-top:2.5rem;">
      <div class="scholar-label">记忆法 jìyìfǎ · Master Retention Image</div>
      <p>Stand on the loess plain and watch the 黄河 go by: water so thick with yellow silt it pours like liquid clay. That is 河 in its birthplace. On the left of the glyph, the three drops of 氵 are the silt-heavy current itself; on the right, 可 (kě) is just the sound, a borrowed syllable riding the water the way a raft rides the flood.</p>
      <p>Hold the order in mind: first 河 was a name, this one muddy river, and only later a word for all rivers. So when you read 黄河, hear the original swallowing its later self, yellow added back to a name that once needed nothing. Every other 河 word is that first river loaning out its character: 运河 a river people dug, 银河 a river of stars, 河边 the bank you walk along at dusk.</p>
