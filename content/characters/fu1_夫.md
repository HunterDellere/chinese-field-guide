    <!-- ── ETYMOLOGY ── -->
    <span class="section-anchor" id="etymology"></span>
    <div class="section-head">
      <span class="sh-cn">字源</span>
      <span class="sh-py">zìyuán</span>
      <span class="sh-en">Etymology &amp; Structure</span>
      <span class="sh-rule"></span>
    </div>

    <div class="scholar" data-glyph="夫">
      <div class="scholar-label">字源洞见 zìyuán dòngjiàn · Etymological Insight</div>
      <p><strong>夫 fū is a pictograph built on 大 dà, the figure of a standing person seen from the front.</strong> Above the outstretched 大 a single horizontal stroke marks a hairpin run through the topknot. In early society a man bound up his hair and pinned it when he came of age, so the added stroke turns the generic person into a grown, capped adult: a man who has reached maturity. The simplified and traditional forms are identical, four strokes that have changed little across the script's long history.</p>
      <p>From that core image of the adult man, the senses fan out, and the character also carries a second, literary reading. The everyday reading <strong>fū</strong> spans three related fields:</p>
      <p><strong>1. Husband.</strong> The grown man as one half of a married pair: 丈夫 zhàngfu (husband), 夫妻 fūqī (husband and wife), 夫人 fūrén (madam, a man's wife), 夫妇 fūfù (a married couple).<br>
      <strong>2. An adult man, a laborer.</strong> A man defined by his work: 农夫 nóngfū (farmer), 渔夫 yúfū (fisherman), 车夫 chēfū (cart driver). Note that 大夫 read <strong>dàifu</strong> means doctor, a special colloquial reading.<br>
      <strong>3. Skill, the effort that mastery takes.</strong> 功夫 gōngfu (skill, craft, kung fu) and 工夫 gōngfu (time and effort spent), where the man's labor becomes the work itself.</p>
      <p>The second reading is <strong>fú</strong>, a particle of classical and literary Chinese. At the head of a sentence it works as a soft opener, "now, as for," signalling that a general statement or reflection is about to follow; mid-sentence or at the close it can point like "this, that." It carries no concrete meaning of its own, only a measured, essayistic tone. A learner meets it most in 论语 and 道德经 style prose, almost never in modern speech.</p>
    </div>

    <!-- ── HUSBAND & COUPLE ── -->
    <span class="section-anchor" id="husband"></span>
    <div class="section-head">
      <span class="sh-cn">夫妻</span>
      <span class="sh-py">fūqī</span>
      <span class="sh-en">Husband &amp; the Married Couple</span>
      <span class="sh-rule"></span>
    </div>

    <div class="scholar" data-glyph="夫">
      <div class="scholar-label">语义洞见 yǔyì dòngjiàn · Semantic Insight</div>
      <p>The warmest face of 夫 is the husband, the grown man as one half of a marriage. 丈夫 zhàngfu is the plain everyday word for one's husband. Pair the man with the wife and you get the couple seen from two angles: 夫妻 fūqī, husband and wife as a unit, and 夫妇 fūfù, a more formal pairing of the same idea. 夫人 fūrén, finally, is the polite term for another man's wife, "madam" or "lady," a word of respect rather than of intimacy. Together they map the married man from the inside (my husband) out to the formal (so-and-so's wife).</p>
    </div>

You introduce a stranger as 王先生 (Mr. Wang), but you call the man you married your 丈夫. One states the bond, the other shows respect.

    <!-- ── MEN & LABORERS ── -->
    <span class="section-anchor" id="laborer"></span>
    <div class="section-head">
      <span class="sh-cn">农夫</span>
      <span class="sh-py">nóngfū</span>
      <span class="sh-en">Men &amp; Laborers</span>
      <span class="sh-rule"></span>
    </div>

    <div class="scholar" data-glyph="夫">
      <div class="scholar-label">语义洞见 yǔyì dòngjiàn · Semantic Insight</div>
      <p>Strip away the marriage and 夫 is simply the grown man, and most often the man at work. Set a kind of labor before it and you name the worker: 农夫 nóngfū the farmer who tills the field, 渔夫 yúfū the fisherman who casts the net, 车夫 chēfū the driver who pulls or drives the cart. These read with a slightly old, plain flavor, the laboring man of an earlier age. One compound breaks the pattern in sound: 大夫, when read <strong>dàifu</strong>, means a doctor, a colloquial word for a physician. The same two characters read 大夫 dàfū once meant a senior official in the old court, so the reading you choose decides the meaning entirely.</p>
    </div>

    <!-- ── SKILL, EFFORT & THE PARTICLE FÚ ── -->
    <span class="section-anchor" id="skill"></span>
    <div class="section-head">
      <span class="sh-cn">功夫</span>
      <span class="sh-py">gōngfu</span>
      <span class="sh-en">Skill, Effort &amp; the Particle fú</span>
      <span class="sh-rule"></span>
    </div>

    <div class="scholar" data-glyph="夫">
      <div class="scholar-label">语义洞见 yǔyì dòngjiàn · Semantic Insight</div>
      <p>When the man's labor becomes the thing itself, 夫 names skill and the effort that buys it. 功夫 gōngfu is the famous word: the mastery won by long practice, hence kung fu in the martial sense, but also any hard-earned competence. Written 工夫 gōngfu it leans toward the time and trouble that go into a task: 花工夫 to put time and care into something. Apart from these everyday senses sits the wholly separate literary reading <strong>fú</strong>: a classical particle with no concrete meaning, used to open a measured sentence ("now, as for") or to point quietly like "this, that," the hallmark of essay-style classical prose.</p>
    </div>

    <div class="cards">

      <!-- 功夫 -->
      <div class="card c-violet">

        <div class="examples">
          <div class="example">

            <div class="ex-py">Zhè dào cài hěn jiàn gōngfu.</div>
            <div class="ex-en">This dish really shows the cook's skill.</div>
          </div>

        <div class="note">
          <span class="note-label">辨析 biànxī · 功夫 vs. 工夫</span>
          <strong>功夫</strong> gōngfu stresses the skill that practice produces: kung fu, real mastery. <strong>工夫</strong> gōngfu stresses the time and effort spent: 花了不少工夫 took quite a bit of time and trouble. In modern use the two are often interchanged, but the cleaner split is skill for 功夫 and time-and-effort for 工夫.
        </div>

            <div class="ex-cn">夫学须静也。</div>
            <div class="ex-py">Fú xué xū jìng yě.</div>
            <div class="ex-en">Now, learning requires stillness. (classical style)</div>
          </div>
        </div>
        <div class="note">
          <span class="note-label">读音 dúyīn · fū vs. fú</span>
          Read <strong>fū</strong>, 夫 carries real meaning: husband, man, skill. Read <strong>fú</strong>, it is an empty literary particle that only sets a tone. Modern readers meet 夫 fú almost only inside classical texts and set phrases; in living speech the character is nearly always fū.
        </div>
      </div>

        <span class="cy-py">yī fū dāng guān</span>
        <span class="cy-en">one man guards the pass: a position so strong that a single defender holds off an army</span>
        <span class="cy-note">From the line about the road to Shu, 一夫当关，万夫莫开, "one man holding the pass, ten thousand cannot force it open."         <span class="cy-cn">匹夫之勇</span>
        <span class="cy-py">pǐ fū zhī yǒng</span>
        <span class="cy-en">the courage of a common man: reckless bravery without strategy</span>
        <span class="cy-note">匹夫 pǐfū is an ordinary fellow of no rank. The phrase names the brute, headlong courage of one who rushes in to fight without thought or plan, contrasted with the cool, calculated valor of a true leader. It is a gentle put-down: brave, but foolishly so.</span>

      <div class="cy">
        <span class="cy-cn">千夫所指</span>
        <span class="cy-py">qiān fū suǒ zhǐ</span>
        <span class="cy-en">pointed at by a thousand men: condemned by everyone</span>
        <span class="cy-note">Literally "what a thousand men point their fingers at." It describes a person reviled by the whole public, the target of universal blame and reproach. To be 千夫所指 is to stand utterly condemned in the eyes of the crowd, a heavy figure for public disgrace.</span>

        <span class="cy-note">Literally "the husband leads, the wife accompanies." A traditional image of marital harmony in which the couple act in accord, one taking the lead and the other in step. It reflects an older ideal of the household; today it is often used warmly, and sometimes lightly, for any well-matched, agreeable pair.</span>

    <div class="scholar" data-glyph="夫" style="margin-top:2.5rem;">
      <div class="scholar-label">记忆法 jìyìfǎ · Master Retention Image</div>
      <p>Hold one picture at the center of 夫: a grown man standing tall (大) with a hairpin run across his topknot, the cap of adulthood. That single image of the come-of-age man is the seed of every sense.</p>
      <p>Marry the man and he is a husband: 丈夫 husband, 夫妻 and 夫妇 the couple, 夫人 the lady of the house. Send him to work and he is the laboring man: 农夫 farmer, 渔夫 fisherman, and, in a special reading, 大夫 dàifu the doctor. Let his labor harden into mastery and he becomes the skill itself: 功夫 the craft won by practice, 工夫 the time it costs. And when the same glyph is read fú, the man steps off the stage entirely, leaving behind an empty literary particle that only opens a measured classical sentence. Whenever you meet 夫, ask first which reading is in play, fū the man or fú the particle, and then which of the man's many roles the compound has in mind.</p>
