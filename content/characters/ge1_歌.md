    <span class="section-anchor" id="etymology"></span>
    <div class="section-head"><span class="sh-cn">字源</span><span class="sh-py">zìyuán</span><span class="sh-en">Etymology &amp; Structure</span><span class="sh-rule"></span></div>
    <div class="scholar" data-glyph="歌">
      <div class="scholar-label">字源洞见 zìyuán dòngjiàn · Etymological Insight</div>
      <p>歌 splits cleanly into two parts: 歌 = 哥 + 欠. The right side, 欠 (qiàn), is the radical and carries the meaning. It pictures a kneeling person with a wide-open mouth, breath streaming out, the same body that supplies the breathy throat-sounds in 吹 (to blow) and 欢 (to be glad). On the left, 哥 (gē) is pure sound: it hands the whole character its gē reading and nothing of its meaning. This is a textbook pictophonetic: 欠 is semantic, and 哥 is the phonetic.</p>
      <p>The Shuōwén Jiězì glosses 歌 as 詠 (yǒng), to draw a sound out long, and lists a variant 謌 written with the 言 (speech) radical. Both versions point at the same act: voice carried on a sustained exhale. The 欠 element won out because singing is felt in the body as breath rather than as ordinary talk, so the open-mouthed exhaling figure fit better than the plain speech radical.</p>
      <p>哥 itself doubles 可 (kě), and in older usage 哥 once meant a chanted refrain before it settled into its modern sense of elder brother. So even the phonetic side carries a faint memory of song stacked twice over. The meaning of 歌 has stayed remarkably stable: from the folk airs collected in the 诗经 (Shījīng) down to a track on a phone today, it names both the sung thing and the act of singing it.</p>
      <p>Japanese borrows the glyph as <i>ka</i> / <i>uta</i> (歌), the <i>uta</i> at the heart of 和歌 (waka) court poetry; Korean reads it <i>ga</i> (가) in words like 가수 (gasu, singer). Across all three the kneeling exhaling mouth still does the work.</p>

    <div class="section-head"><span class="sh-cn">唱歌</span><span class="sh-py">chànggē</span><span class="sh-en">Singing in Daily Life</span><span class="sh-rule"></span></div>
    <div class="cards">
      <div class="card c-red">
        <div class="card-head"><span class="card-cn">唱歌</span><span class="card-py">chànggē</span><span class="card-en">to sing</span></div>
        <div class="tags"><span class="tag tag-vn">VO 离合词 líhécí</span></div>
        <div class="card-def">唱 (to sing aloud) plus 歌 (song). A verb-object pair, so a measure word or modifier slides into the seam: 唱一首歌 (sing one song). The most basic, neutral way to say someone is singing.</div>
        <div class="examples">
          <div class="example"><div class="ex-cn">她很喜欢唱歌。</div><div class="ex-py">Tā hěn xǐhuan chànggē.</div><div class="ex-en">She really loves to sing.</div></div>
          <div class="example"><div class="ex-cn">我们一起唱了一首歌。</div><div class="ex-py">Wǒmen yìqǐ chàng le yì shǒu gē.</div><div class="ex-en">We sang a song together.</div></div>
        </div>
        <div class="note"><span class="note-label">辨析 biànxī · 唱 vs. 歌</span> 唱 is the action of voicing; 歌 is the thing voiced. You can 唱 a song, an opera line, or a scale, but only a 歌 is sung.</div>
      </div>

        <div class="examples">

        </div>

    <div class="scholar" data-glyph="歌" style="margin-top:2.5rem;">
      <div class="scholar-label">记忆法 jìyìfǎ · Master Retention Image</div>
      <p>Picture an elder brother (哥) standing at the head of a long table, head tipped back, mouth thrown wide on a held note. That open exhaling mouth is the right-hand 欠, the breath leaving the body in a sustained stream. The brother on the left supplies the gē sound; the yawning mouth on the right supplies the meaning. Hear them together: 哥 gē, mouth open, gē 歌.</p>
      <p>Once the brother starts, the whole room joins: 唱歌 (chànggē) everyone sings, the 歌词 (gēcí) words rolling out, until the floor clears and they 载歌载舞 (zài gē zài wǔ) sing and dance at once. Every 歌 compound is just another voice pulled into that one wide-open breath, from a child's 儿歌 (érgē) to a stadium's 国歌 (guógē).</p>
