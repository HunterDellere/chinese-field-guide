    <span class="section-anchor" id="etymology"></span>
    <div class="section-head"><span class="sh-cn">字源</span><span class="sh-py">zìyuán</span><span class="sh-en">Etymology &amp; Structure</span><span class="sh-rule"></span></div>
    <div class="scholar" data-glyph="牛">
      <div class="scholar-label">字源洞见 zìyuán dòngjiàn · Etymological Insight</div>
      <p>牛 is a pictograph of an ox's head seen straight on, the curved horns sweeping up and out from the crown. In the oracle-bone script the two horns flare to the top, the ears jut sideways, and a long line drops through the middle for the muzzle and snout. The seal script squares those horns into the top stroke and that vertical spine that survives untouched in the four strokes you write today: the slanted top, the long crossbar, the rising horn-tip, and the descending muzzle.</p>
      <p>The Shuōwén Jiězì reads the glyph as the animal entire, a creature of weight and patience, and groups under it the whole vocabulary of the herd. 牛 is its own radical, the semantic flag on any character that has to do with cattle: 物 (wù, thing, originally a mottled ox), 特 (tè, special, once a single bull set apart for sacrifice), and 牧 (mù, to herd, an ox beside a hand holding a switch).</p>
      <p>The horns are the giveaway. Set 牛 beside its near-twin 午 (wǔ) and the eye learns the difference: 牛 keeps the upward kick on its top-left stroke, the horn that no other common character carries. That single rising tick is the ox's signature.</p>
      <p>The same head crossed into Japanese as ぎゅう / うし and into Korean as 우 (u), still meaning ox throughout the Sinographic world, still the radical for everything bovine.</p>

    <span class="section-anchor" id="figurative"></span>
    <div class="section-head"><span class="sh-cn">引申</span><span class="sh-py">yǐnshēn</span><span class="sh-en">Figurative: Slang &amp; Idiom</span><span class="sh-rule"></span></div>
    <div class="scholar" data-glyph="牛">
      <div class="scholar-label">用法 yòngfǎ · Usage Note</div>
      <p>On the street 牛 swings loose from the animal. Said of a person or a feat, 牛 (or the fuller 牛逼 niúbī, vulgar but everywhere) means impressively strong, gutsy, top-tier. The ox lends its raw power and stubbornness to the praise. Use 牛 freely; keep 牛逼 for friends and informal speech.</p>
    </div>

    <div class="scholar" data-glyph="牛" style="margin-top:2.5rem;">
      <div class="scholar-label">记忆法 jìyìfǎ · Master Retention Image</div>
      <p>Stand in front of the ox and look it dead in the face. Two horns curve up and out from the top of the skull, the broad muzzle drops straight down between them, and the ears flick to either side. That frontal head is the whole character: the slanted top stroke and the long crossbar are the brow and horns, the vertical that drops through them is the snout.</p>
      <p>Hold onto the upward kick on the top-left, the one horn that rises. That tick is what keeps 牛 from collapsing into 午 (wǔ, noon). When you write the ox, flick that horn up first, then lay the crossbar of the brow, then drop the muzzle.</p>
      <p>From that head the rest follows by sense. Milk and meat hang off it on the table (牛奶, 牛肉); blow the hide up too big and you are bragging (吹牛); and when someone is genuinely powerful, you call them 牛, the beast itself, all stubborn muscle and forward weight.</p>
