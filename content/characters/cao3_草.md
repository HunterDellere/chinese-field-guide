    <!-- ── ETYMOLOGY ── -->
    <span class="section-anchor" id="etymology"></span>
    <div class="section-head">
      <span class="sh-cn">字源</span>
      <span class="sh-py">zìyuán</span>
      <span class="sh-en">Etymology &amp; Structure</span>
      <span class="sh-rule"></span>
    </div>

    <div class="scholar" data-glyph="草">
      <div class="scholar-label">字源洞见 zìyuán dòngjiàn · Etymological Insight</div>
      <p><strong>草 cǎo = 艹 (cǎo, the grass radical) + 早 (zǎo, early).</strong> The top element 艹 is the grass radical, the two short tufts that mark a word as belonging to plants. Below it sits 早 zǎo, which here supplies the sound, the dipping cǎo echoing the rising-then-clipped zǎo. This is a pictophonetic build: 艹 carries the meaning of growing things, 早 carries the pronunciation.</p>
      <p>The grass radical itself began as a doubled 屮 (chè), the sprout, a single shoot pushing two leaves out of the ground. Stack two sprouts and you get a clump; flatten the clump into the conventional 艹 strip and you have the marker that crowns hundreds of plant characters, from 茶 (chá, tea) to 菜 (cài, vegetable). The original word for grass was in fact written 艸, the bare doubled sprout, before 早 was added underneath to fix the sound and 草 became standard.</p>
      <p>From the plant in the field the character spreads into three families of meaning. First the literal: grass, herbs, weeds, the green that covers a meadow (草地 cǎodì, 草原 cǎoyuán). Second the harvested: grass cut and dried into straw and thatch (稻草 dàocǎo, rice straw). Third, and most surprising, the figurative: because wild grass grows fast and tangled, 草 came to mean hasty and rough, hence a first draft (草稿 cǎogǎo) and the flying, untidy "grass script" of calligraphy (草书 cǎoshū).</p>
      <p>The same character carries into Japanese as <em>kusa</em> and 草書 (<em>sōsho</em>), the running cursive hand, and into Korean as 초 (<em>cho</em>) in words like 초안, a draft. Everywhere the thread is the meadow: dense, quick-growing, never quite tidy.</p>
    </div>

    <!-- ── STRAW ── -->
    <span class="section-anchor" id="straw"></span>
    <div class="section-head">
      <span class="sh-cn">稻草</span>
      <span class="sh-py">dàocǎo</span>
      <span class="sh-en">Straw &amp; Herbs</span>

    <div class="scholar" data-glyph="草">
      <div class="scholar-label">语义洞见 yǔyì dòngjiàn · Semantic Insight</div>
      <p>Cut the grass and dry it, and 草 names the harvest. 稻草 dàocǎo is the straw left after rice (稻 dào) is threshed, the golden stalks bundled for fuel, fodder, thatch, and the stuffing of a scarecrow (稻草人). 干草 gāncǎo is hay, grass dried to feed livestock through the winter. The same plant that grew green in the meadow now lies cut and useful.</p>
      <p>A second branch leads to medicine. 草药 cǎoyào is herbal medicine, the leaves, roots, and stems of plants gathered and brewed in the tradition of 中草药 zhōng cǎoyào, Chinese herbal pharmacy. Here 草 carries its oldest, widest sense: not just grass underfoot but every useful green growing thing, the herb as well as the blade.</p>
    </div>

    <!-- ── DRAFT ── -->
    <span class="section-anchor" id="draft"></span>
    <div class="section-head">
      <span class="sh-cn">起草</span>
      <span class="sh-py">qǐcǎo</span>
      <span class="sh-en">Rough &amp; Draft</span>
      <span class="sh-rule"></span>
    </div>

    <div class="scholar" data-glyph="草">
      <div class="scholar-label">引申洞见 yǐnshēn dòngjiàn · Extended-Sense Insight</div>
      <p>Wild grass grows fast, dense, and untidy, and from that picture 草 leaps into the abstract. A 草稿 cǎogǎo is a rough draft, the first tangled version of a text before it is tidied; to 起草 qǐcǎo is to draft a document, to throw down the first growth of words. The adjective 草率 cǎoshuài means careless and hasty, doing a thing as roughly as grass grows, and 潦草 liáocǎo describes sloppy, scrawled handwriting.</p>
      <p>The most beautiful turn is in calligraphy. 草书 cǎoshū, "grass script," is the flying cursive hand in which strokes run together and characters dissolve into gesture, as wild and quick on the page as grass across a field. What began as a weed in the meadow becomes the freest, most expressive script a brush can make.</p>
    </div>

      <p>Picture a meadow at first light: the grass radical 艹 is the tufted green spreading across the top, and beneath it the early sun 早 zǎo rises over the field, lending its sound to the whole. That is 草, grass under the morning, the plant that covers every open ground.</p>
      <p>From that one field the senses branch. Left growing, it is the meadow you cross (草地, 草原) and the weeds you pull (杂草). Cut and dried, it is straw for the roof, hay for the ox, the herb in the medicine pot (稻草, 草帽, 草药). And because grass grows so fast and tangled, the word leaps to the desk: a hasty rough draft (草稿), a careless hand (草率), the flying grass-script of the brush (草书). Whenever you meet 草, see the meadow first, then ask whether you stand in it, harvest it, or borrow its wild speed for the page.</p>
