    <span class="section-anchor" id="etymology"></span>
    <div class="section-head"><span class="sh-cn">字源</span><span class="sh-py">zìyuán</span><span class="sh-en">Etymology &amp; Structure</span><span class="sh-rule"></span></div>
    <div class="scholar" data-glyph="狗">
      <div class="scholar-label">字源洞见 zìyuán dòngjiàn · Etymological Insight</div>
      <p>狗 splits cleanly left and right: 狗 = 犭+ 句. The left element 犭is the compressed standing form of 犬 (quǎn), the beast radical, three slim strokes that read like a four-legged animal caught mid-stride. The right element 句 (jù) carries no canine meaning at all; it supplies the sound. This makes 狗 a textbook pictophonetic, the most common construction in the Chinese script: one half points at the category, the other half whispers the pronunciation.</p>
      <p>The semantic 犭attaches 狗 to a whole pack of animal graphs, 猫 (māo, cat), 猪 (zhū, pig), 猴 (hóu, monkey), every one of them flagged as a creature by that same lean radical. In the seal script the radical still shows the high back and dropped tail of a hound; the modern 犭flattens it into the upright three-stroke form you write today. The phonetic 句, meanwhile, also seeds 句 (sentence), 够 (enough), and 钩 (hook), a family bound not by meaning but by that recurring -ou sound.</p>
      <p>Classical Chinese kept a fine distinction that modern Mandarin has nearly erased. 犬 named the full-grown dog, 狗 the puppy or smaller breed; the Shuōwén glosses 狗 as a young dog. In everyday speech 狗 has long since swallowed 犬 to mean any dog, while 犬 survives mostly in compounds and formal registers (警犬 jǐngquǎn, police dog). Japanese reads the same character いぬ (inu) or ク (ku), keeping it as the standard word for dog.</p>

    <div class="scholar" data-glyph="狗" style="margin-top:2.5rem;">
      <div class="scholar-label">记忆法 jìyìfǎ · Master Retention Image</div>
      <p>Picture a lean hound straining at a gate. Its taut body is the left radical 犭, three quick strokes that are all spine and legs, an animal leaning forward, ears up. The radical tells you instantly: this is a beast. What it cannot tell you is how to say it. For that, lean in and listen to the dog's collar tag clinking, 句, 句, gǒu, gǒu. The sound rides in on the right half of the character.</p>
      <p>Now let the meaning fan out through the compounds. Shrink the hound to a 小狗 (xiǎogǒu) and it is a puppy tumbling at your feet. Set it on a scent and it becomes a 猎狗 (liègǒu) nose to the ground. Chain it by the door and you have a 看门狗 (kānméngǒu) barking at every footstep. Corner it against a wall and you watch 狗急跳墙 (gǒu jí tiào qiáng), the desperate leap. Every one of these is the same eight-stroke glyph: beast on the left, the clink of gǒu on the right.</p>
