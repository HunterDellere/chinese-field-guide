    <div class="section-head"><span class="sh-cn">字源</span><span class="sh-py">zìyuán</span><span class="sh-en">Etymology &amp; Structure</span><span class="sh-rule"></span></div>
    <div class="scholar" data-glyph="简">
      <div class="scholar-label">字源洞见 zìyuán dòngjiàn · Etymological Insight</div>
      <p>简 stacks bamboo over a gate: 简 = ⺮ + 间. The bamboo radical ⺮ on top is semantic and the whole point of the character; 间 (jiān) sits beneath as the phonetic, lending the sound while the original 閒 it derives from once pictured moonlight slipping through a gateway. Read the glyph from the top down and you are looking at a thing made of bamboo.</p>
      <p>That thing is a 简, a single strip of split bamboo, planed flat, scraped smooth, and ruled for a vertical column of brush characters. Before paper spread in the Han, every document lived on these slips. A clerk wrote one column per strip, then bound a row of them edge to edge with cord, and the bundle rolled up into the earliest Chinese book. Archaeologists still pull such tied bundles, 简牍 (jiǎndú), from Warring States and Han tombs, the silk cord rotted away but the bamboo legible.</p>
      <p>The abstract senses fall straight out of the object. A bamboo slip is narrow, so only what is essential fits: hence 简单 (jiǎndān), simple, and 简短 (jiǎnduǎn), brief. A slip carried to someone became a written message, so 简 took on the literary sense of a letter, preserved in 书简 (shūjiǎn). And because writing demands choosing what to keep, 简 reaches further into to select, surviving in 简拔 (jiǎnbá), to pick out and promote.</p>
      <p>Japanese keeps the slip and the brevity in 簡 (kan), as in 簡単 (kantan), simple. The mainland simplified the lower 閒 to 间, but the bamboo on top, the part that names the thing, never moved.</p>

    <div class="scholar" data-glyph="简" style="margin-top:2.5rem;">
      <div class="scholar-label">记忆法 jìyìfǎ · Master Retention Image</div>
      <p>Picture a Han clerk at a low desk before paper exists. He lifts one narrow strip of split bamboo, 竹 (zhú) over it in your mind, and through the gateway 间 behind him the late sun slants in. The strip is the width of a single column, and that narrowness decides everything: only the essential words will fit. He writes one short line, then sets the slip aside.</p>
      <p>Hold that image and the whole character family unrolls from it. The slip is too narrow for the elaborate, so what survives on it is 简单 (jiǎndān), simple, and 简短 (jiǎnduǎn), brief. Tie a few slips with cord and you have a 简历 (jiǎnlì), a brief life laid out in columns, the ancestor of the résumé. Carry one slip to a friend and it becomes 书简 (shūjiǎn), a letter.</p>
      <p>When you later meet 简化 (jiǎnhuà), to simplify, see the clerk shaving a fat bundle of slips down to a thin one, cutting until only the spine of the meaning remains. The bamboo on top of the glyph is always the same gesture: take the wide and unruly, plane it onto a narrow strip, keep only what counts.</p>
