字源zìyuánEtymology & Structure
字源洞见 zìyuán dòngjiàn · Etymological Insight

脸 splits cleanly down the middle: 脸 = ⺼ + 佥. The left is ⺼, the flesh-moon radical (肉 in its squeezed-in writing form), which marks body parts across the whole lexicon, from 肝 (gān, liver) to 腿 (tuǐ, leg). The right is 佥 (qiān), here a phonetic component carrying the sound. This is a textbook pictophonetic build: meat on the left tells you the domain, 佥 on the right tells you how to say it.

The phonetic 佥 belongs to a family of characters that once shared an initial cluster, which is why its modern descendants scatter across readings like jiǎn (检, 捡), liǎn (脸), and qiān (签). The lips and teeth that produced those sounds drifted apart over the centuries, but the written marriage of flesh plus 佥 froze 脸 at liǎn.

The earliest sense was narrow: 脸 named the part of the face below the eyes, the cheeks where rouge sat and blushes rose. Classical texts more often used 面 (miàn) for the face as a flat surface. 脸 was the warmer, fleshier word, and over time it swallowed the whole front of the head. From there it made the leap every body word in Chinese seems to make, from skin to standing: the 脸 you wash in the morning is the same 脸 you can lose in public, the visible flesh that society reads as a measure of your worth.

Japanese kept 顔 and 面 for the face and rarely borrowed 脸, which is one reason 脸 feels distinctly modern-Chinese in flavor, a colloquial word that climbed from cheek to character to reputation entirely on home ground.

神色shénsèThe Face That Shows
脸色liǎnsècomplexion; facial expression; mood read off the face
N 名词 míngcí
脸 (face) + 色 (color, look). Literally the color of the face, used both for physical complexion and for the mood it betrays. To 看脸色 (kàn liǎnsè) is to read someone's expression and act accordingly, a core social skill.
他今天脸色不太好，是不是病了？
Tā jīntiān liǎnsè bú tài hǎo, shì bú shì bìng le?
He doesn't look well today, is he sick?
在公司里，谁都得看老板的脸色。
Zài gōngsī lǐ, shéi dōu děi kàn lǎobǎn de liǎnsè.
At the company, everyone has to read the boss's mood.
笑脸xiàoliǎnsmiling face; a smile worn for others
N 名词 míngcí
笑 (laugh, smile) + 脸 (face). A face shaped into a smile, especially the welcoming one you put on for guests or customers. The opposite expression is 板着脸 (bǎnzhe liǎn), to hold the face stiff and stern.
她总是带着笑脸迎接客人。
Tā zǒngshì dàizhe xiàoliǎn yíngjiē kèrén.
She always greets guests with a smile.
辨析 biànxī · 笑脸 vs. 微笑 笑脸 is the face as a whole worn smiling; 微笑 (wēixiào) is the act of smiling itself, the gentle curve of the mouth.
红脸hóngliǎnto blush; to flush red (with anger or embarrassment)
V/N 动名词 dòngmíngcí
红 (red) + 脸 (face). The cheeks going red, whether from shyness or from a flare of temper. 脸红 (liǎnhóng) reverses the order for the same blush; 红脸 also names the stern, blunt role someone plays in a good-cop bad-cop pairing.
被这么一夸，她马上就脸红了。
Bèi zhème yì kuā, tā mǎshàng jiù liǎnhóng le.
Praised like that, she blushed at once.
面子miànziThe Face You Keep
文化洞见 wénhuà dòngjiàn · Cultural Insight

In Chinese the face is also a social account. 脸 here means dignity, the standing you hold in the eyes of others. You can 丢脸 (lose face) through a public misstep, 给面子 (give face) by honoring someone in front of a crowd, or 不要脸 (have no shame) by acting past all decency. 脸 and its near-twin 面子 (miànzi) carry this weight: a single embarrassment in front of the wrong people costs more than the act itself, because the audience is the currency.

丢脸diūliǎnto lose face; to be disgraced
V/N 动名词 dòngmíngcí
丢 (to lose, drop) + 脸 (face). To suffer public embarrassment, to have your standing slip in front of others. The image is literal: dignity dropped like a coin in the street.
考试不及格让他觉得很丢脸。
Kǎoshì bù jígé ràng tā juéde hěn diūliǎn.
Failing the exam made him feel humiliated.
别在客人面前吵架，太丢脸了。
Bié zài kèrén miànqián chǎojià, tài diūliǎn le.
Don't argue in front of the guests, it's too embarrassing.
辨析 biànxī · 丢脸 vs. 丢人 丢脸 and 丢人 (diūrén) are near-synonyms for losing face; 丢人 leans slightly more colloquial and personal, 丢脸 a touch more about social standing.
翻脸fānliǎnto turn hostile; to fall out with someone
V 动词 dòngcí
翻 (to flip, turn over) + 脸 (face). To flip the face from friendly to cold in an instant, breaking off goodwill. The vivid version is 翻脸不认人 (fānliǎn bú rèn rén), to turn cold and pretend you never knew the person.
为了一点小事，他们俩就翻脸了。
Wèile yìdiǎn xiǎoshì, tāmen liǎ jiù fānliǎn le.
Over one small thing, the two of them fell out.
脸皮liǎnpífacial skin; (figurative) sense of shame; nerve
N 名词 míngcí
脸 (face) + 皮 (skin). The skin of the face, and by extension one's capacity for shame. 脸皮厚 (liǎnpí hòu), thick-skinned, means shameless or unembarrassable; 脸皮薄 (liǎnpí báo), thin-skinned, means easily mortified.
他脸皮太厚了，被拒绝了还接着问。
Tā liǎnpí tài hòu le, bèi jùjué le hái jiēzhe wèn.
He's so thick-skinned, he keeps asking even after being turned down.
日常rìchángEveryday Face
洗脸xǐliǎnto wash one's face
V 动词 dòngcí
洗 (to wash) + 脸 (face). The first ritual of the morning. A verb-object pair, so you can split it: 洗个脸 (xǐ ge liǎn), to give the face a quick wash.
起床后我先刷牙洗脸。
Qǐchuáng hòu wǒ xiān shuāyá xǐliǎn.
After getting up I brush my teeth and wash my face first.
脸蛋liǎndàncheeks; (affectionate) little face
N 名词 míngcí
脸 (face) + 蛋 (egg, oval). The egg-smooth oval of the cheeks, a warm word usually aimed at children or a pretty face. Often softened further to 脸蛋儿 (liǎndànr) with the northern -r ending.
小宝宝的脸蛋红扑扑的，真可爱。
Xiǎo bǎobao de liǎndàn hóngpūpū de, zhēn kě'ài.
The baby's little cheeks are rosy and red, so adorable.
脸盆liǎnpénwashbasin
N 名词 míngcí
脸 (face) + 盆 (basin). The basin you fill to wash your face, a fixture of older Chinese households before plumbing reached every sink. A small window onto domestic life packed into two characters.
她端着一盆热水，把脸盆放在桌子上。
Tā duānzhe yì pén rè shuǐ, bǎ liǎnpén fàng zài zhuōzi shàng.
Carrying a basin of hot water, she set the washbasin on the table.
成语chéngyǔIdioms & Set Phrases
嬉皮笑脸xī pí xiào liǎngrinning cheekily: to behave with a flippant, unserious grinA face stuck in a smirk, used to describe someone who refuses to take a moment seriously. Often a parent's rebuke to a child who jokes through a scolding.
愁眉苦脸chóu méi kǔ liǎnknitted brows and a bitter face: a picture of gloom and worry愁 (worry) eyebrows over a 苦 (bitter) face. The whole head sags with anxiety. A vivid, everyday way to say someone looks miserable.
不要脸bú yào liǎnto want no face: shameless, brazenNot a classical four-character chengyu but a fixed colloquial phrase: to want no face is to discard all sense of shame. A sharp insult, stronger than it looks in English.
劈头盖脸pī tóu gài liǎnsplitting the head and covering the face: full in the face, without holding backDescribes a torrent that lands straight on you, a downpour of rain or a barrage of scolding, leaving no chance to dodge.
记忆法 jìyìfǎ · Master Retention Image

Picture a butcher's slab at dawn, a slice of pale flesh (⺼) laid out under the light. Now stamp it with a maker's seal, the character 佥, pressed into the meat to certify whose it is. That stamped cut of flesh is your 脸: a piece of the body that the world reads like a label.

Hold that double image and every compound falls into place. The flesh side gives you the morning rituals, 洗脸 at the basin, the rosy 脸蛋 of a child, the 脸色 a doctor checks. The stamped-seal side gives you the social ledger, where you can 丢脸 (drop the seal in the street), 翻脸 (flip it over and show a cold blank back), or wear 脸皮 thick enough that no stamp embarrasses you.

The cheeks redden, the seal smudges, the standing slips: in Chinese the meat and the mark are one word, which is why a blush and a disgrace both live in 脸.
